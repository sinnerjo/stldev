Why Dribbling Comes Before Passing: A Coaching Philosophy, Tested Against the Research

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · October 10, 2026 · 8 min read

Develop dribblers first. Let passing show up later, once the field gets big enough that dribbling alone stops working. That is the philosophy behind how we coach young players, and it is worth being honest about which parts of it are backed by real research, which parts are informed coaching judgment, and which parts serious people actually disagree with.

Who this guide is for

A coach deciding what to actually prioritize in practice at each age, from first touches at 3v3 up through full sided 11v11. This is a philosophy with its reasoning shown, not a claim that every other approach is wrong.

The core ideaWhat this philosophy actually says

At the youngest ages, a player's job is simple: keep the ball, move with it, and try to get past whoever is in front of them. No shape, no patterns, no pressure to find a teammate. As players get older and the field grows from 3v3 to 4v4 to 7v7 to 9v9 to 11v11, the game itself starts asking a different question. There is more ground to cover, defenders are farther apart, and a player who can only dribble eventually runs into a situation dribbling cannot solve alone. Passing becomes the answer to a problem the player has actually felt, not a drill they were told to run before they understood why.

What the research actually backsIndividual ball mastery first is well supported

This part of the philosophy has real, direct backing. US Youth Soccer's own Player Development Model does not hedge on it: "American players need to spend more time with the ball. From ages 4 to 14, ball skills must take precedence over the score and the win/loss record." The same document gives the mechanical reason small sided formats exist at all: "fewer players result in more touches of the ball."

U.S. Soccer's own Player Development Framework for the U7 to U8 age group goes further and gets specific about what those touches should look like. Activities at this age are "mostly formed around deliberate play with the ball," and dribbling is defined plainly as the priority skill: "the player on the ball decides to progress forward by driving at an opponent, using body feints and/or change of speed to dribble past (eliminate) them." Passing is not absent from the framework, but it is introduced as a choice inside that same decision, not a separate lesson: a player learns to either "engage opponent: create 1v1" or "find the free player: pass." Passing shows up as "an option," in the framework's own words, layered on top of a foundation that is dribbling and 1v1 play first.

Not a new ideaA well known methodology built on the same premise

This philosophy has a name in the wider coaching world. Coerver Coaching, one of the most widely used individual skill curriculums globally, puts ball mastery at the literal base of its training pyramid: "the touch, control and confidence that affects every other part of the pyramid." Its own stated reasoning for why this comes first is direct: "the success of any team system, tactic or formation ultimately depends on the quality of the individual player skills." One correction worth making here, since it gets repeated inaccurately even by coaches who use the method: Coerver Coaching was not founded by Wiel Coerver himself. It was built by Alf Galustian and former Chelsea player Charlie Cooke, who were inspired by the Dutch coach's ideas and named the method after him.

The Brazil argument, honestlyWhat futsal and tight spaces actually prove

The idea that Brazilian players are better dribblers because they grow up in tight spaces and playing futsal is one of the most repeated claims in youth soccer coaching, and there is real data behind part of it. A peer reviewed study tracking the developmental activities of elite under 16 players found that of the Brazilian players studied, the large majority engaged in futsal during childhood, and that Brazilian players "accumulated a relatively high amount of time in soccer specific play activity and futsal during childhood" compared to players from several other countries studied.

What that study does not do is prove futsal causes better dribbling through some measurable mechanism. A separate systematic review of talent development in futsal found that coaches and players overwhelmingly believe it works, with close to 90 percent of surveyed participants saying pitch size and futsal improved their skills, but the review is direct that this is "perceptual data rather than longitudinal intervention studies demonstrating causality." In plain terms: it is a strongly and widely held belief among people who know the game, but it has not been proven the way a controlled study proves something. Worth repeating that distinction honestly instead of dressing up a belief as settled science.

Why free, game like touches matterThe case for unstructured play

Sport psychology has a real framework for why lots of unstructured ball time at a young age tends to pay off later. Researcher Jean Côté and colleagues, in an official position statement for the International Society of Sport Psychology, distinguish "deliberate play," activities like backyard soccer that are "regulated by flexible age adapted rules" and aimed at "inherent enjoyment," from "deliberate practice," which is structured, effortful, and aimed directly at improving performance. Their point is that deliberate play in childhood "serves as a way for youth to explore their physical capacities in various contexts" and builds a base of motor and decision making experience that gets carried into a player's main sport later. Worth being honest about scope here: this framework is about general childhood motor development, not soccer specifically, and it does not by itself say dribbling must come before passing. It supports the broader instinct that free, repeated, low pressure ball contact at a young age is valuable, which is the foundation this whole philosophy rests on.

Where the evidence runs outThe one claim that is coaching logic, not settled research

Here is the most honest thing to say about this philosophy: the specific idea that a bigger field is what causes passing to become necessary, because the extra space makes dribbling alone insufficient, is reasoned coaching judgment, not a proven finding. A search for research making that exact causal argument did not turn one up. The closest available data point actually cuts the other way. A study altering pitch size and player numbers in small sided games found that "as pitch size became larger, less passes and receptions were made, although this was not significant," while players "dribbled more during the 1v1 and 2v2 games," meaning the smallest, most cramped formats produced more individual dribbling actions, not the largest ones. That study's finding on pitch size was not statistically significant either way, so it does not disprove the philosophy, but it does mean nobody should present "bigger field forces more passing" as an established fact. It is a reasonable, experience based read on the game, and it is presented here as exactly that.

The other campA real, credible disagreement worth knowing

Not every respected voice in player development agrees that skill and tactics should be taught in sequence at all. A body of research often called ecological or nonlinear pedagogy argues the opposite directly: that "the development of skill and team coordination (i.e., tactics) are not separate topics requiring different approaches but entirely interdependent aspects of the same whole." In that view, a young player should be learning to dribble and to read a passing option at the same time, inside small sided games designed to present both choices, rather than mastering one first and discovering the other later. This is a legitimate, research backed position, not a fringe opinion, and a coach who structures practice around teaching skill and decision making together rather than sequentially is not doing it wrong. It is a different, defensible bet about how players learn.

Putting it to workWhat this actually looks like by format

  • 3v3 and 4v4. Almost pure individual ball mastery. Praise a kid for trying a move and getting past someone, even if it does not work every time. Do not correct a player for "not passing" at this stage. There is often no better option to find yet.
  • 7v7. Start naming passing as an option once a player has actually felt a defender they could not beat alone. This is where the earlier US Soccer framework's language fits exactly: engage the opponent, or find the free player. Both are live choices now.
  • 9v9 and 11v11. Passing becomes central because the field genuinely demands it, longer distances, more space to cover, defenders who can recover if a dribble is not decisive. Combination play, third man runs, and switching the point of attack all depend on a passing foundation the player now has a real reason to want.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Teaching passing patterns at 3v3 or 4v4 before a player has a reason to want an alternative to dribbling.
  • Discouraging a failed dribble attempt at a young age instead of praising the decision to try one.
  • Presenting this philosophy as proven science rather than the reasoned, research informed judgment call it actually is.
  • Ignoring a player who is ready for passing just because of their age or format, when the game in front of them is already asking for it.
