Recognizing Burnout in a Young Athlete: The Signs Before They Quit

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · October 7, 2026 · 7 min read

By the time a player tells you they want to quit, burnout has usually been building for months. The behavior changes show up earlier, if you know what you are looking at. This guide covers what real sport psychology research says burnout actually is, what it looks like before a kid walks away, what causes it, and what a coach or parent can actually do about it.

Who this guide is for

Coaches and parents who have noticed a player pulling away from a sport they used to love, and want to know if what they are seeing is ordinary tiredness, a real warning sign, or something that deserves more than a coaching fix.

Before the signsThe real dropout numbers, not the famous one

You have probably heard that 70 percent of kids quit organized sports by age 13. We went looking for where that number actually comes from, and it does not hold up well. A 2024 peer reviewed review of youth sport dropout research points out plainly that "it is surprising that the significance of current dropout statistics and findings are rarely questioned," and traces the 70 percent figure back through a chain of papers citing each other rather than a single traceable original study. Reporting that investigated the claim directly found researchers describing it as a statistic that "may not have a clear primary source," repeated for decades without one.

The number we would actually stand behind comes from a study that used real, methodical survey data, the Monitoring the Future study, tracked from 8th grade through 12th grade. It found an average attrition rate of 32 percent across all sports, meaning 32 of every 100 athletes who play a sport in 8th grade are gone from it by 12th grade. Soccer specifically showed 31 percent attrition for boys and 53 percent for girls over that same span. Whatever the real number is for your team, it is not nothing, and it is not evenly spread between boys and girls.

The frameworkWhat burnout actually is, in three parts

The most used model in sport psychology research, built by researcher Thomas Raedeke and measured with a tool called the Athlete Burnout Questionnaire, breaks burnout into three parts: "emotional and/or physical exhaustion," "reduced sense of accomplishment," and "devaluation of sport." Translated out of research language, that is a player who is worn out, a player who no longer feels like their effort is getting them anywhere, and a player who has quietly stopped caring about the thing they used to care about. Real burnout usually involves some mix of all three, not just one bad week of tiredness.

What to watch forThe behavior signs before a kid quits outright

The American Academy of Pediatrics, in its 2024 clinical report on overtraining and burnout in young athletes, lists the observable signs to watch for: fatigue, "depressive symptoms," loss of interest or motivation, sleep disturbances, irritability, anxiety symptoms, trouble concentrating, "unexplained musculoskeletal complaints," weight changes, decreased performance, and decreased enjoyment in the sport. On a soccer field, that tends to actually look like this:

  • Dread before practice or games, not simple reluctance to leave the couch, but a pattern of complaints, stalling, or a mood shift specifically tied to soccer.
  • Vague physical complaints with no clear cause, a sore leg or a stomach ache that never quite becomes a diagnosable injury and tends to show up before practice more than during it.
  • Irritability that is specific to the sport, a kid who is otherwise fine at school or at home but short tempered around anything soccer related.
  • Performance dropping despite continued or increased effort. The AAP is direct about this one: "athletes with decreased performance despite adequate recovery, nutrition, and hydration may require further evaluation for burnout." This is different from a normal slump, the player is still showing up and trying, and it is not working.
  • Losing interest in the parts of the sport they used to love, skipping the extra reps they used to ask for, no longer wanting to watch soccer at home, going quiet about a team they used to talk about constantly.

A related but different thingBurnout is not the same as overtraining syndrome

These two get used interchangeably, and they are related but not identical. The AAP describes overtraining syndrome as "an accumulation of stress" that results in "a persistent performance decrease" and "requires weeks or months of rest to restore," a mostly physical and performance based problem. Burnout is more about motivation and emotion than the body's ability to recover. The AAP places them on the same spectrum, stating plainly that "burnout is part of a spectrum that includes overreaching and overtraining syndrome." In practice, a player can be burned out without being physically overtrained, and a player recovering from overtraining syndrome is also at real risk of becoming burned out along the way.

What actually causes itThe research backed risk factors

  • Specializing in one sport too early. The AAP's own policy statement on youth sports specialization is direct: "burnout, anxiety, depression, and attrition are increased in early specializers." A peer reviewed analysis of nearly 1,500 adolescent athletes found specializers scored significantly higher on all three burnout dimensions than players who sampled multiple sports.
  • Low autonomy. The AAP notes that "young people who primarily identify themselves as an athlete or who feel like they have limited control over their sports decisions are at higher risk for developing burnout."
  • A climate built around winning instead of improving. A 2023 study coauthored by Raedeke himself found that when parents, coaches, and peers created what researchers call a "mastery" climate, focused on effort and improvement, burnout and disengagement went down. A climate focused purely on outcome and winning showed no such benefit.
  • Too many structured hours with no real rest. The National Athletic Trainers' Association recommends that athletes "not participate in organized sport and/or activity more hours per week than their age," and that they get "a minimum of two days off per week" from organized training.

What to actually doWhat the research says helps

  • Give the player real say in their own soccer decisions. The AAP's own recommendation is to "encourage athletic autonomy and intrinsic motivation, measure success on participation and effort, and foster positive experiences."
  • Talk and coach in terms of effort and improvement, not just results. This is the one piece of this guide with a direct research link to lower burnout, not just a reasonable guess.
  • Support more than one sport, especially before puberty. The AAP states that "participating in multiple sports, at least until puberty, decreases the chances of injuries, stress, and burnout."
  • Build in real time off. The AAP's specific numbers: at least three months off across the year, taken in month long chunks rather than scattered days, plus one to two days off every week during the season.

A careful noteWhen it might be more than burnout

Sport burnout and a broader mental health concern can look similar from the sideline, and this guide is not the place to sort out which one you are seeing. If a player seems sad, withdrawn, or changed well beyond soccer itself, not just tired of practice, that is worth more than a coaching adjustment. The American Academy of Pediatrics puts it simply: "if you have any questions or concerns about your child's mental health, don't hesitate to talk with your pediatrician." That is the right next step, not a checklist, and not something to try to diagnose from the sideline.
