Coaching a Multilingual Team: Communication Habits That Actually Work

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · October 1, 2026 · 10 min read

Most advice for coaching a team where not every family speaks English as a first language stops at "use a translation app." That is a start, not a plan. The habits that actually work have less to do with which app is on your phone and more to do with who you route information through, which channel you use for what, and whether you are willing to slow down and show a thing instead of only saying it.

Who this guide is for

Coaches with one or more families on the roster who are more comfortable in a language other than English, whether that is one family or half the team. This is not about diversity in the abstract, it is about the specific, practical problem of making sure a parent actually understands the schedule, and a player actually understands the instruction, regardless of what language either of them thinks in first.

The short version
  • Do not make the player your translator for anything that matters, playing time, behavior, safety, or schedule changes.
  • Translate important written information once, by a person, rather than live, by an app, every time.
  • Use a channel parents can review, not just one they have to catch and understand in the moment.
  • Demonstrate the skill, do not just describe it. This helps every player, and it is the single most reliable tool across a language gap.
  • Ask early who is comfortable translating, and give that parent a real role, not just an assumption you are making about them.
  • Before you read a quiet or unresponsive player as a behavior problem, rule out whether they understood you at all.

The default to avoidThe player is not your translator

The easiest option on a youth team is also the one worth avoiding by default: letting the player translate between you and their own parent. Federal guidance to schools on this exact situation is direct. Districts "must provide translation or interpretation from appropriate and competent individuals and may not rely on or ask students, siblings, friends, or untrained school staff to translate or interpret for parents." That rule was written for classrooms, but the underlying reasoning applies just as much to a sideline conversation about playing time or a phone call about a behavior issue.

The research on children who translate for their families, a real, decades old field called language brokering, backs this up. It is not automatically harmful, one researcher's review found it can build "a stronger bond between parents and children," and a young adult who grew up doing it described it plainly: "it has never felt like a burden, but it hasn't always been easy or convenient." But the same body of research documents real costs too, "negative feelings such as stress, burden and depression, especially when associated with poor parenting relationships," and kids who quietly protect their parents by leaving things out, softening "racist comments or offensive language" rather than translating them directly. A child who has to decide in real time whether to tell their parent the coach is unhappy with their attitude is carrying something that is not theirs to carry.

The practical fix is not complicated. For anything with real weight, a playing time conversation, a behavior issue, a safety concern, route it through an adult, a bilingual parent volunteer, a staff member, or a scheduled call with an actual interpreter, not through the player in the car on the way home.

Translation appsWhat they are actually good for

Translation apps are a real tool, not a fraud, but they are also not a substitute for a human when something matters. A 2025 clinical study comparing Google Translate's voice mode against qualified human interpreters found clinically significant error rates of "33.3% for Google Translate in speech/voice mode versus 4.8% for interpreter-generated translations," and that gap widened for less common languages, "42.9% vs. 14.3%" compared to more commonly spoken ones. A separate study of written translation for medical instructions found Google Translate got the substance right most of the time, "594 (92%) and 522 (81%) sentences were accurately translated into Spanish and Chinese, respectively," but the errors that did happen were concentrated in exactly the details you would not want to get wrong, like specific instructions.

Neither study is about a soccer sideline, and we have not found one that is, so treat this as a reasonable extension rather than a direct finding: a live spoken conversation in a noisy, fast moving environment is a harder case than either study tested, not an easier one. The honest takeaway is not "never use a translation app." It is that a translation app is fine for a quick, low stakes exchange, confirming a practice time, saying good game, and a poor tool for anything where a misunderstanding actually costs someone something.

Written informationTranslate the important stuff once, by a person

For anything you send home in writing, a schedule, a permission form, a season overview, the strongest guidance we found comes from Colorín Colorado, a respected bilingual education resource. Their direct advice is to "avoid using translation websites, which are imprecise and often inaccurate" for materials families will actually rely on, and to have a real person handle it, ideally "have other native speakers review written translations" once a first draft exists. If you are asking a volunteer parent to help, the same source recommends you "offer training, provide a list of translated terms, give them enough time to complete the translation," rather than asking for a rushed favor the night before a form is due.

The other piece worth doing early, and often skipped: just ask. Colorín Colorado's guidance includes finding out how "parents prefer to receive communication (phone, e-mail, text message, etc.)" rather than assuming. The National PTA's own standard for family communication says the same thing in fewer words, effective communication means working to "learn about and meet families' communication preferences," not defaulting to whatever is easiest for the coach.

Channel choicePick something parents can review, not just hear

One detail from a small academic study of teacher to family texting is worth building a habit around. Researchers found some parents actually preferred a text message over a phone call for a specific reason, a text is something "the father could show the texts to family members for translation," while a phone call has to be understood in real time or it is gone. The same study noted that "parents with limited English skills were less likely to answer a phone call" in the first place. This is one study, not a broad survey, so treat it as a useful signal rather than a settled fact, but the logic holds regardless of which specific app or language is involved: a message a parent can save, reread, and share with someone else at home beats a conversation that has to land correctly the first time.

We looked for solid data on which specific apps immigrant or multilingual families actually prefer and found it thin and group specific rather than universal, real but limited evidence that WhatsApp use is unusually high among Hispanic households in the United States, for example, is not the same as a rule for every language community. The safer habit is the one above, ask what channel this family prefers, and default to something reviewable over something spoken whenever the content matters.

On the fieldShow it, do not just say it

This is the habit that helps regardless of language, and it happens to matter most when language is a barrier. A systematic review of observational learning in physical education found "strong evidence supported the effect of observational learning on students' motor skill learning compared to students who did not participate in observational learning," in eleven of the fourteen studies it reviewed. Demonstration is not a workaround for players who do not speak English well, it is simply a better way to teach a physical skill to anyone, and it removes the need for translation entirely for a huge share of what happens at practice.

Do not assume a clever verbal cue will bridge the gap either. A study on coaching cues in youth sport found "the type of cue or analogy provided to a youth performer has little subsequent effect on sprint or jump performance," and recommended coaches "take a more specific approach that is suited to the level or preferences of a particular individual" instead of a one size fits all phrase. In practice, that means simple, consistent hand signals for common instructions, spread out, press, switch, a full speed demonstration before you ever explain it in words, and pairing a newer or non English speaking player with a patient teammate who can mirror the movement for them.

One point of contactFind your bilingual parent early

At your preseason meeting, covered in our preseason parent meeting guide, ask directly whether any parent is comfortable helping translate for the team during the season, in writing and occasionally by phone. Do not assume the most fluent looking parent wants the job, ask, and treat a yes as a real role worth thanking publicly, not a favor you quietly lean on all year. This mirrors the same idea schools use when they assign "each family a school contact who speaks their language," just built for a team instead of a school.

We looked specifically for a youth soccer or governing body resource on coaching multilingual teams, from US Soccer, US Youth Soccer, or a well known club, and could not find one. The most useful practical advice we found came from a general coaching trade publication, not a soccer specific source, which put the standard plainly: "if the majority of the team looks confused, then we have not done a good job explaining our intentions," and "if an athlete is important enough to be a part of your team, then it's important enough to stop instruction to make sure they grasp the content." That this kind of guidance barely exists for youth soccer specifically is itself worth knowing, you are working a real gap, not missing something that is well documented elsewhere.

Watch outDo not mistake a language gap for an attitude problem

One caution worth naming directly, because it is an easy mistake to make without noticing. A player who does not respond to an instruction, freezes during a drill they clearly do not understand, or seems checked out during a talk they cannot fully follow can look like the same problem our behavior and effort guide covers, low effort or a bad attitude. Sometimes it is exactly that. Sometimes it is a comprehension gap wearing the same face, and the two can be genuinely hard to tell apart from the sideline in the moment. This is our own practical suggestion, not something we found in a study, but it costs nothing to check: before you correct or discipline a player for not listening, ask a simple question first, in whatever way you can, to find out whether they actually understood what you said.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Making the player translate a hard conversation about their own playing time, behavior, or a safety issue.
  • Sending a schedule or form through a translation app instead of having a person check it once, in advance.
  • Assuming the most confident looking bilingual parent wants to be your translator without ever actually asking.
  • Defaulting to a phone call when a written message a family can save and share would work better.
  • Explaining a drill at length instead of demonstrating it first and explaining second.
  • Reading confusion as defiance, and disciplining a player before checking whether they understood you.
