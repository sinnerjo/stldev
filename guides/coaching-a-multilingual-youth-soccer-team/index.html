Coaching a Multilingual Team: Communication Habits That Actually Work

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 22, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 12 min read

Most advice for coaching a team where not every family speaks English as a first language stops at "use a translation app." That is a start, not a plan. The habits that actually work have less to do with which app is on your phone and more to do with who you route information through, which channel you use for what, and whether you are willing to slow down and show a thing instead of only saying it.

Who this guide is for

Coaches with one or more families on the roster who are more comfortable in a language other than English, whether that is one family or half the team. This is not about diversity in the abstract. It is about a practical problem: making sure a parent understands the schedule and a player understands the instruction, whatever language either of them thinks in first.

The short version
  • Do not make the player your translator for anything that matters: playing time, behavior, safety, or schedule changes.
  • Translate important written information once, by a person, rather than live, by an app, every time.
  • Use a channel parents can review, not just one they have to catch and understand in the moment.
  • Demonstrate the skill, do not just describe it. This helps every player, and it is the single most reliable tool across a language gap.
  • Ask early who is comfortable translating, and give that parent a real role, not just an assumption you are making about them.
  • Check understanding by asking players to show you, not by asking "do you understand?"
  • Before you read a quiet or unresponsive player as a behavior problem, rule out whether they understood you at all.

The default to avoidThe player is not your translator

The easiest option on a youth team is also the one worth avoiding by default: letting the player translate between you and their own parent. Federal guidance to schools on this exact situation was direct. A 2015 Education Department and Justice Department fact sheet said schools "may not rely on or ask students, siblings, friends, or untrained school staff to translate or interpret for parents." One caution on that source: the Education Department rescinded the companion 2015 Dear Colleague letter on English learners in August 2025, and reporting at the time noted that the underlying legal requirements did not change. We could not confirm the current status of the fact sheet itself, so read it as a statement of good practice, not as current policy. The rule was written for classrooms, but the reasoning applies just as much to a sideline conversation about playing time or a phone call about a behavior issue.

The research on children who translate for their families, a decades old field called language brokering, is mixed. Crafter and Iqbal's paper describes studies that found benefits, including stronger parent and child bonds, alongside other scholars who documented "negative feelings such as stress, burden and depression, especially when associated with poor parenting relationships." Umme Orthy, writing about translating for her own family, put it plainly: "It has never felt like a burden, but it hasn't always been easy or convenient." Antonini's overview of the field also reports former brokers describing self censorship in sensitive situations, softening "racist comments or offensive language" to spare the adults they were interpreting for. A child who has to decide in real time whether to tell their parent the coach is unhappy with their attitude is carrying something that is not theirs to carry.

The practical fix is not complicated. For anything with real weight, such as a playing time conversation, a behavior issue, or a safety concern, route it through an adult: a bilingual parent volunteer, a club staff member, or a scheduled call with an actual interpreter. Do not route it through the player in the car on the way home.

Translation appsWhat they are actually good for

Translation apps are a real tool, not a fraud, but they are not a substitute for a human when something matters. A 2026 exploratory study in the journal Healthcare compared Google Translate's voice mode against qualified human interpreters, using 14 recorded physician statements translated into six languages. It found clinically significant error rates of "33.3% for Google Translate in speech/voice mode versus 4.8%" for interpreter generated translations. Google Translate's own error rate was higher for the four less common languages in the study than for the two common ones, 42.9% versus 14.3%. The authors describe the sample as relatively small, so treat the numbers as a signal, not a benchmark.

A separate 2019 study in JAMA Internal Medicine ran Google Translate on 100 sets of emergency department discharge instructions. About 92 percent of the Spanish and 81 percent of the Chinese came out accurate, and only a small share of the inaccuracies could cause serious harm. The UCSF summary notes that those were mostly traced to grammar or typing errors in the original English, while colloquial phrases, medical jargon, and long complicated sentences caused many of the other mistakes. Apps have changed since then, but the practical lesson holds: write short, plain sentences with no slang before you translate anything.

Neither study is about a soccer sideline, and we have not found one that is, so treat this as a reasonable extension rather than a direct finding. A live spoken conversation in a noisy, fast moving environment is a harder case than either study tested, not an easier one. The takeaway is not "never use a translation app." A translation app is fine for a quick, low stakes exchange, like confirming a practice time or saying good game. It is a poor tool for anything where a misunderstanding actually costs someone something.

Written informationTranslate the important stuff once, by a person

For anything you send home in writing, such as a schedule, a permission form, or a season overview, the strongest guidance we found comes from Colorín Colorado, a respected bilingual education resource. Their advice is to "avoid using translation websites, which are imprecise and often inaccurate" for materials families will actually rely on, and to have "other native speakers review written translations" once a first draft exists. If you are asking a volunteer parent to help, the same source recommends you "offer training, provide a list of translated terms, give them enough time to complete the translation," rather than asking for a rushed favor the night before a form is due.

The other piece worth doing early, and often skipped, is to ask. Colorín Colorado's guidance includes asking parents "how they prefer to receive communication (phone, e-mail, text message, etc.)" rather than assuming. The National PTA's standard for family communication points the same way: it lists the indicator "Learn about and meet families' communication preferences" instead of defaulting to whatever is easiest for the school, or the coach.

Channel choicePick something parents can review, not just hear

One detail from a small pilot study of teacher and family texting in a high poverty urban preschool program (20 teachers and 30 family members in focus groups) is worth building a habit around. One teacher preferred texting a child's father over calling because "the father could show the texts to family members for translation," and six teachers reported that parents with limited English skills "were less likely to answer a phone call." A text can be saved, reread, and shared. A phone call has to be understood in real time or it is gone. This was preschool, not youth sports, and it is one small study, so treat it as a useful signal rather than a settled fact. The logic holds whichever app or language is involved: a message a parent can reread and share with someone at home beats a conversation that has to land correctly the first time.

We looked for solid data on which apps multilingual families prefer and found nothing we could cite for youth sports. Preferences differ a lot by language community and by country of origin, so do not guess. Ask each family which channel they prefer, and default to something reviewable over something spoken whenever the content matters.

On the fieldShow it, do not just say it

This is the habit that helps regardless of language, and it matters most when language is a barrier. A 2022 systematic review of observational learning in physical education included 18 studies. Fourteen of them compared observational learning with none, and the authors concluded that "strong evidence supported the effect of observational learning on students' motor skill learning." Eleven of those 14 studies reported improved motor skills. These were physical education students, not youth soccer players, but the finding fits what most coaches see. Demonstration is not a workaround for players who do not speak English well. It is a better way to teach a physical skill to anyone, and it removes the need for translation entirely for a large share of what happens at practice.

Do not count on a clever verbal cue to bridge the gap either. A PLOS ONE study of 173 male youths tested different cues and analogies before a 20 meter sprint and a vertical jump. It concluded that "the type of cue or analogy provided to a youth performer has little subsequent effect on sprint or jump performance," and suggested coaches "might take a more specific approach that is suited to the level or preferences of a particular individual." It was not about language barriers, so it tells us only that a catchy phrase is not a substitute for showing the skill.

Here is what we would do about that, as our own suggestions and not findings from the study. Use a few simple, consistent hand signals for common instructions, for example a flat palm pushed outward for spread out and a sweeping arm across the body for switch the field. Give a full speed demonstration before you explain anything in words. Pair a newer or non English speaking player with a patient teammate who can mirror the movement. Then check understanding by asking the player to show you, not by asking "do you understand?" A nod is not comprehension.

One point of contactFind your bilingual parent early

At your preseason meeting, covered in our preseason parent meeting guide, ask directly whether any parent is comfortable helping translate for the team during the season, in writing and occasionally by phone. Do not assume the most fluent looking parent wants the job. Ask, and treat a yes as a real role worth thanking publicly, not a favor you quietly lean on all year. A single named person also means every family knows exactly who to ask when a message is unclear.

We looked specifically for a youth soccer or governing body resource on coaching multilingual teams, from US Soccer, US Youth Soccer, or a well known club, and could not find one. The most useful practical advice we found came from a 2012 article in Coach and Athletic Director, a coaching trade magazine, which interviewed high school coaches. Soccer coach Brian Cochran said "if the majority of the team looks confused, then we have not done a good job explaining our intentions." Basketball coach Jeremy Hartman argued that "if an athlete is important enough to be a part of your team, then it's important enough to stop instruction" to make sure they grasp the content. That this kind of guidance barely exists for youth soccer specifically is itself worth knowing. You are working in a real gap, not missing something well documented elsewhere.

Watch outDo not mistake a language gap for an attitude problem

One caution worth naming directly, because it is an easy mistake to make without noticing. A player who does not respond to an instruction, freezes during a drill they clearly do not understand, or seems checked out during a talk they cannot fully follow can look like the problem our behavior and effort guide covers: low effort or a bad attitude. Sometimes it is exactly that. Sometimes it is a comprehension gap wearing the same face, and the two can be hard to tell apart from the sideline. This is our own practical suggestion, not something we found in a study, but it costs nothing to check. Before you correct or discipline a player for not listening, find out whether they understood. Ask them to show you what you just asked for, in whatever way you can.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Making the player translate a hard conversation about their own playing time, behavior, or a safety issue.
  • Sending a schedule or form through a translation app instead of having a person check it once, in advance.
  • Assuming the most confident looking bilingual parent wants to be your translator without ever actually asking.
  • Defaulting to a phone call when a written message a family can save and share would work better.
  • Explaining a drill at length instead of demonstrating it first and explaining second.
  • Asking "do you understand?" and taking a nod as a yes.
  • Reading confusion as defiance, and disciplining a player before checking whether they understood you.

Ask your AI assistant coachBuild a one page term card for your team's actual languages

An AI assistant can put together a quick reference card of the practice and game words you use most, translated into the languages your team's families actually speak. You print it once and hand it out, rather than reaching for an app mid drill. This is a good use for a low stakes, everyday list of terms, not for anything with real weight like a playing time or behavior conversation.

"I coach a youth soccer team with families who speak [languages]. Build a one page reference card with these terms translated into each language: pass, shoot, spread out, press, switch the ball, offside, throw in, corner kick, practice starts at [time], and bring a ball and water. Write each term in the language's native script and add a simple pronunciation guide in plain English spelling, and flag any term where the translation might differ by country or dialect. Keep it simple enough to read at a glance on the sideline." Paste this once, then print and reuse the card all season

Before you print it, ask a fluent speaker, ideally your bilingual parent volunteer, to read the card. AI translation is weaker in less common languages, and a wrong word on a printed card gets repeated all season. Put only language names in the prompt, never family or child names.
