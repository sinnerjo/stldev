Coaching Youth Soccer for the First Time: Fun, Fair Minutes and Small Goals

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 23, 2026 · 14 min read

You do not need to be an expert to coach a great first season. You need a plan, a ball for every player, a fair way to share the minutes, and a simple goal for each game that has nothing to do with the score. If the kids enjoy it and want to come back for the second practice, you are doing your job.

Who this guide is for

New and volunteer coaches in recreational and club soccer, especially at the youngest ages, where the most important results are fun, touches on the ball and kids who return next season.

The short version
  • Focus on enjoyment. Kids who have fun keep playing, and kids who keep playing keep improving.
  • In training, every player has a ball. No lines, no laps, no lectures.
  • Turn drills into games with a score, a race or a small stake, and give each one only one or two things to learn.
  • Bring a plan and a backup plan, and know what comes next so you can switch activities fast.
  • In games, share the playing time equally. Plan your substitutions before kickoff.
  • Measure improvement, not the scoreboard. Pick one or two game goals that do not involve winning or losing.
  • Get to know your players off the field. Learn their names and ask about their day. You may never know how much it matters.

Your real jobFocus on enjoyment

The US Youth Soccer coaching manual states it plainly: "The primary motivation for children to participate in sports is to have fun and improve." Fun is not a reward for good training. It is the reason kids show up, and it is what keeps them coming back long enough to get good.

Research backs this up. George Washington University researcher Amanda Visek and her team studied 142 youth soccer players, 37 coaches and 57 parents and found 81 things that make sports fun. The top three fun factors were being a good sport, trying hard and positive coaching. Winning ranked near the bottom.

The stakes are real. A 2025 Aspen Institute survey of 3,827 young people aged 10 to 17 asked former players why they stopped. The most common answers were feeling "not good enough" (29 percent) and "bad coaching" (21 percent). A first season that feels welcoming, active and encouraging is the best protection against both. One youth coaching guide puts it bluntly: kids who stay in the game get coaching for years, and kids who quit at 8 get none.

TrainingEveryone needs a ball

The US Youth Soccer manual is direct: "Every player must have a ball to use at every practice," and it suggests bringing a couple of extra balls so nobody is left waiting. One beginner practice guide says the same thing in simple math: if you have 12 kids, you need 12 balls.

A ball per player is the easiest way to guarantee touches, and touches are how young players learn. It also solves the problem coaches worry about most, which is standing around. The same manual says having players stand in lines waiting for a turn generally signals an inappropriate activity, and it recommends the phrase "lines, laps and lectures" as a checklist of what to avoid. SoccerDrive states it as a rule: no laps, no lines, no lectures. As it points out, in a line one player is working while everyone else is just waiting.

How to get enough balls

  • Ask every family to bring a ball to every practice, labeled with the player's name. Most families already own one.
  • Keep a small team bag of spares for kids who forget.
  • If you are short, use game formats that share the ball, such as 2v2 or 3v3, in small spaces. Avoid drills where one child works and the rest wait.

Start the moment kids arrive

Young children want to play as soon as they get there. The US Youth Soccer manual recommends "instant activities" that children can join immediately, and one first practice guide advises not waiting for everyone to show up. A simple arrival activity, such as dribbling anywhere in a grid, lets you greet parents and set up cones while the kids are already playing.

TrainingGamification: turn drills into games

Gamification just means borrowing the parts of a game that make it fun, such as a score, a challenge or a way to win, and building them into an ordinary activity. Instead of "dribble across the grid five times," it becomes "how many times can you cross the grid in 30 seconds without losing the ball?" The task barely changes. The framing does, and kids notice.

A youth sports training review describes gamification as applying game design to an activity that was not a game to begin with, and ties it to three things young players need in order to stay motivated: a sense that they are getting better, some choice in how they attempt a task, and a shared challenge with teammates. A 2025 systematic review of 19 studies on gamification in physical education found it consistently improved motivation, and one study it summarized found the gains in physical activity held up even after the gamified program ended.

You do not need an app, a scoreboard or a prize box to gamify a practice. Small changes do the job:

  • Add a number. Count goals, gates passed or completed passes, and let players try to beat their own count on the next try.
  • Add a race. Two players or two small teams doing the same task side by side is instantly more fun than doing it alone.
  • Add a small stake. The pair that loses a 1v1 gets the next rep on defense, or the team that concedes fewer goals in a scrimmage picks the next warm up game.
  • Let them choose. Offer two versions of a challenge, an easier one and a harder one, and let each player pick which to try.

Keep the reward inside the game itself, such as bragging rights or picking the next activity, rather than always reaching for stickers or prizes. The review above also found that gamification can backfire when it leans too hard on outside rewards instead of the fun of the task itself, so use prizes sparingly, if at all.

PreparationHave a plan and a backup plan

Coach Dave Wright of the Player Development Project writes that "good planning is an essential part of coaching." A plan does not need to be fancy. It needs to be written down, timed, and short enough to carry in your pocket. Published practice templates for young players tend to look alike: a quick arrival activity, a warm up game, one or two skill activities that turn into games, a small sided game, and a short huddle at the end.

Here is a sample 60 minute plan built from those templates. Treat it as a starting point and adjust it to your age group.

TimeActivityWhat it does
0 to 10 minArrival activity: a small sided game (2v2 or 3v3) players can jump into the moment they get therePlay starts immediately, and early or late arrivals join right in
10 to 25 minWarm up game: dribble tag or red light, green lightTouches, movement, laughter
25 to 45 minSkill game: dribble through gates, then race a partner, then add a defenderLearn a skill by playing, not by lining up
45 to 55 minSmall sided game: 3v3 or 4v4 to small goalsDecisions, passing, shooting
55 to 60 minShort huddleRecap the one thing you focused on today, praise effort by name, a positive send off

Your backup plan

Things will go wrong. Fewer kids show up, more show up, the field is soaked, or the activity you were proud of falls flat in two minutes. A backup plan means you already know what you will do.

  • Make the scrimmage your safety net. One coaching guide says "the best backup plan is always the scrimmage," because kids love to play the real game. If a drill flops, switch to a small sided game with a single simple challenge.
  • If the group is small, lean into it. Sports Steps notes that a thin roster lets you shrink the space, raise the tempo and give every player far more ball time than a full squad would get. Shift to 2v2, 3v3 or 4v4, and keep one ball per player for individual work.
  • If the group is big, split into two or three small games running side by side instead of one long queue.
  • If the space is small or wet, keep the ball at each player's feet and reduce the running, using dribbling games and short passing games.
  • Use a simple test. If you ever look up and see four kids standing in a line waiting for a turn, change the activity right then. That advice comes from a first time coach guide, and it is a good rule for every session.

FlowKeep players engaged and know what is next

Dead time is where practices fall apart. A child who is waiting for you to find the next cone is a child who is about to wander off or start a wrestling match. The fix is not to talk more. It is to know what comes next so the switch takes seconds.

  • Write the plan on an index card with the activities, times and setup notes. Check it between activities instead of trying to remember.
  • Set up the next activity early. Ask a parent helper to lay out the next grid while you finish the current one, or design activities that use the same setup and change one rule.
  • Build on what you just did. The gate dribbling example in the sample plan moves from basic navigation to racing a partner to adding a defender, so the players already know the space and the rules.
  • Keep instructions brief. Wright advises keeping instructions between exercises simple and brief, and one first time coach guide suggests staying under 30 seconds and showing the activity instead of describing it.
  • Add tasks gradually. Start with the simplest version and add one challenge at a time, instead of explaining a complicated game up front.
  • Use planned water breaks as resets. A short break is a natural moment to reorganize, praise something specific and start the next game.
  • Keep a backup activity in your back pocket. Pick one simple, no setup game you know cold, such as a scrimmage or a quick tag game, before every practice. When an activity is not landing, do not try to fix it in front of the group. Call the backup activity and move on. A few seconds of confusion is fine. A few minutes of it and you lose the group's attention for the rest of practice.
  • Give each activity one main point, two at most. A single small sided game can touch on dribbling, passing and defending all at once, but young players cannot absorb all three in one session. Pick the one part you actually want them to work on today, coach that, and let the rest happen on its own. Bring the same activity back another week and pick a different focus. The repetition is not wasted. It is how the other skills get their turn.

Game dayEqual playing time, every game

For young players, the minutes are the point. The US Youth Soccer manual tells coaches to "be sure everyone plays an appropriate amount of time regardless of the score." Score is not a reason to shorten a child's turn.

In our equal playing time guide, we argue that at 9v9 and below every player should get at least half of every game, injuries and other issues aside, because game time and repetitions matter more for long term development than winning today. Even at 11v11, where players begin to earn positions, we think the floor should stay at 50 percent.

The easiest way to keep your promise is to decide before kickoff. One first time coach guide says to write a substitution plan before the game, in shifts, and stick to it regardless of score. Decide who starts, who comes on at each break, and how the shifts repeat, so you are never making it up while the game moves.

MindsetDo not worry about wins and losses

You will lose games in your first season, and that is fine. Young players are not developing for this Saturday. They are developing for the next several years.

The US Youth Soccer manual recommends playing down competition at the earliest ages, and suggests asking players "how did you play today?" instead of "did you win?" The Positive Coaching Alliance describes a Double Goal Coach who wants to win but treats teaching life skills as the more important goal. Its Mastery framework, called the ELM Tree, values effort, learning and mistakes over the outcome. As one coach in a MOJO Sports profile put it, winning does not tell the whole story: a team can lose, but give everything, learn, and bounce back from mistakes.

ICOACHKIDS puts the same idea in one sentence: "Get the process right and the score will take care of itself."

Game goalsSet one or two goals that do not involve the score

Not worrying about the score does not mean having no aim. It means picking a target you can control. David Feigley of the Rutgers Youth Sports Research Council explains that performance goals work better than outcome goals because athletes control them directly, while "outcomes are frequently controlled by others." He adds that performance orientations tend to promote more skill development, more risk taking and greater self improvement.

So before each game, choose one or two goals like these:

Game goalHow you know you hit it
Everyone takes at least one shotCount shots by player
Play out from the back instead of kicking it longCount the times the goalkeeper or defenders pass to a teammate
Wide players stay wide and spread the fieldLook at the shape when your team has the ball
Each player tries one dribbling move you practicedAsk them at halftime
Encourage a teammate after a mistakeListen for it, and celebrate it out loud

How to use game goals

  1. Pick one or two. More than that and the players will not remember them.
  2. Say them in one sentence before the game. "Today we are going to see who can pass out of the back."
  3. Check in at halftime. Ask how it is going and what they could try next.
  4. Review after the game. Ask "Did we do it?" before anyone asks about the score. Celebrate progress, even when the game was lost.
  5. Tell the parents. Share your goals and send them our parent sideline guide so they cheer for the same things.

If you want numbers instead of gut feeling, a tool like MatchTap can count events such as shots, so you can see whether you hit your goal without keeping a page of tally marks.

Beyond the fieldGet to know your players off the field too

Some of the most important coaching you do will not look like coaching at all. Learn every player's name in the first week and use it. Ask how school went, what they are excited about, or whether they have a big test coming up. None of it takes more than a minute, and it tells a child you see them as a person first and a player second.

A well known study of award winning high school football coaches found that their strongest relationships with players were built on trust and ongoing communication, and on treating players as young adults worth listening to rather than performers to manage. A Psychology Today piece on coaching effectiveness makes the same case for any level: coaches who know their players as whole people, and who remember the small things, build the relationships that make everything else about coaching work.

You may never know the full impact you have. A season with a young team can put you in front of a child for more hours a week than almost any adult besides a parent or teacher. Sports and Fitness Industry Association data cited by the Aspen Institute's Project Play found that only 28 percent of youth soccer coaches are trained in effective motivational techniques, which means the ones who simply take the time to connect already stand out. As one college coach quoted by Project Play put it, coaching has to go back to "the teaching and caring and serving portion," not just the job of running a practice.

This costs nothing and takes no extra time on the field. It is simply choosing to notice the kids in front of you.

Watch outCommon first season mistakes

  • Talking too much. Long explanations lose kids in seconds. Show it, then let them play.
  • Running lines and laps. They are boring, and the ball is where the learning happens.
  • Overplanning and never adjusting. Your plan is a guide. If the kids are bored or confused, change it.
  • Playing the strongest kids more. It is tempting in a close game. A written substitution plan removes the temptation.
  • Coaching every touch from the sideline. Let players make mistakes and solve problems on their own, the same way we ask parents to stay quiet.
  • Comparing your team to others. Compare your team to itself from last month.
