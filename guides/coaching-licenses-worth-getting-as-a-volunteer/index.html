Coaching Licenses Worth Getting as a Volunteer: What Each One Actually Covers

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 26, 2026 · updated October 19, 2026 · 12 min read

You do not need every credential a coaching education page can throw at you. Some of what follows is not really optional at all, some of it is genuinely worth the time and money, and some of it is built for people chasing coaching as a career rather than a volunteer running one team. Here is what each one actually covers, what it costs, and where the honest line is.

Who this guide is for

A recreational or club volunteer coach trying to figure out which certifications matter for the team in front of you, not a coach already on a professional pathway. If you never played the game, our guide to coaching without playing experience covers what to learn first.

The short version
  • Three things come first: SafeSport training and a background check are required almost everywhere. Concussion training is required by many states and clubs; take it anyway, it is free.
  • The Grassroots courses are the sweet spot. They are short, cheap in the examples below, and teach you how to run a session for your age group.
  • The D License is for coaches who plan to stay with a competitive or travel team for more than one season.
  • The C License and above are career pathways, not a volunteer's next step.
  • Prices and rules are set by your state association, so every dollar figure here is one state's published example, not a national number.

Start hereWhat to do this week

  1. Ask your club registrar what they require. One short message saves you from paying for things your club already covers. Ask what it needs, by when, and whether the club reimburses any of it. Some clubs do, and SafeSport is free for people registered through U.S. Soccer.
  2. Finish SafeSport and the background check first. Those two gate your registration, so they go ahead of any coaching course.
  3. Take the free CDC HEADS UP concussion course for coaches. It is online and free.
  4. Find your own state association's coaching education page (search "[your state] youth soccer coaching education") and note which courses it lists for your age group.

Everything after that is optional growth, and the rest of this guide helps you decide how much of it to take on. Order matters: required items, then the intro module and one Grassroots course for your age group, and only then the D License if you know you are staying in it.

Not optionalWhat comes before any license at all

Three things are not really "coaching education" in the usual sense, they are the baseline your club almost certainly requires before you are allowed near a team, license or no license.

  • SafeSport Trained. The U.S. Center for SafeSport's own site lists the individual 90 minute SafeSport Trained Core course at $20 per person. If you are registered through a soccer organization recognized by the U.S. Olympic & Paralympic Committee, which includes US Soccer, you likely will not pay it yourself, since the site says individuals under the jurisdiction of such a National Governing Body are not subject to a fee. The certificate is only good for a year: it stays valid for 12 months after you complete the course. This is not a club preference either. It traces back to a federal law, the Protecting Young Victims from Sexual Abuse and Safe Sport Authorization Act of 2017, which requires organizations to offer consistent training to adult members in regular contact with minor amateur athletes, as U.S. Soccer's safeguarding FAQ explains. The current rules are the 2025 Minor Athlete Abuse Prevention Policies, effective January 1, 2025: electronic messages to a minor athlete need a parent or another adult included or copied, and one on one interactions should be observable and interruptible.
  • A background check. Most clubs and state associations require one, and many run it through US Soccer's Safe Soccer Clearance Program. U.S. Soccer's clearance page lists the background check element at $24 for two years, but the cost and how long it stays valid can still be set by your state or club. As one published example, Kansas Youth Soccer says coaches and managers "need a background check that will cost them $20 and be valid for two (2) years after approval." Ask your club for your own number.
  • Concussion awareness training. The CDC offers a free course built for coaches specifically, "HEADS UP to Youth Sports Coaches: Online Concussion Training," aimed at helping coaches "spot signs and symptoms of possible concussions and steps to take if one occurs." Whether it is legally mandatory depends on your state and league rather than one national rule, but plenty of state soccer associations require it outright. Mississippi Soccer Association's own policy is typical of how this gets enforced: "all adult participants including coaches, assistant coaches, team managers, club staff, and board members must complete the CDC HEADS UP Concussion Training" before taking part in any sanctioned activity, alongside SafeSport training and a background check, all tied to US Soccer's Safe Soccer Framework. Check your own state association's specific policy rather than assuming Mississippi's applies to you, since this is set state by state.

None of the three above are things to skip because they feel like paperwork. They expire on a schedule, SafeSport yearly, the background check every two years, so budget a little recurring time for them every season, not just once. Ask your club whether it reimburses any of these costs before you pay out of pocket.

The free starting pointIntroduction to Grassroots Coaching

Before any format specific course, you complete a short introductory module in U.S. Soccer's online learning system. Missouri Youth Soccer describes it as a free 20 minute module, and Cal South lists it as a prerequisite for its Grassroots courses and points coaches to the U.S. Soccer Learning Center for details. It is worth doing in your first week as a coach regardless of what level you end up coaching.

Worth itThe 4v4, 7v7, 9v9, and 11v11 Grassroots courses

These are the courses that actually teach you something format specific. Missouri Youth Soccer describes its in person versions as four hour courses covering U.S. Soccer's Grassroots coaching philosophy, the Play Practice Play method, the Six Tasks of a Coach, and what players are like at each age. Not every course counts toward every license path. Missouri is direct about this: "Only the 7v7, 9v9, & 11v11 courses are accepted for the MYSA Coaching License requirement." If you coach only the youngest recreational ages, the 4v4 course still teaches useful content. It may just not be the one your state counts toward a license.

Pricing and format are set by your state association or club, not by U.S. Soccer nationally. In California, Cal South lists its online Grassroots courses at $25 and "4 hours total (Online)." Missouri's page lists no price for the same courses. Check your own state's page for the current number rather than trusting a figure from another state.

A real exampleWhat level actually requires what

How much of this you actually need depends heavily on what you coach, and state associations differ on exactly where the line sits. Wisconsin Youth Soccer Association publishes a clean, tiered example worth using as a mental model even if you coach elsewhere: recreational coaches need, at minimum, "1 In-Person Grassroots Module," coaches of 12U to 14U competitive and State League teams need 2 In-Person Grassroots Modules, one of which must be 9v9 or 11v11, or a US Soccer E License, and coaches of 15U and older State League teams need the US Soccer D License. That is a real, published example of how one state actually scales the requirement to the level of team you coach, not a rule that applies everywhere. Check your own state association's specific tiers, since not every state lays out age based tiers the way Wisconsin does.

The real next stepThe US Soccer D License

This is the license worth genuinely considering if you are coaching a competitive or travel team for more than one season, or if your state ties it to coaching older competitive age groups the way Wisconsin does above. It is a real time commitment, and the shape of it varies by state. Missouri describes "a 40 to 45 hour course, held over two weekends, at minimum 4 weeks apart." Nebraska lists a 16 week course with 9 virtual weeks and two in person weekends. Cal South lists 9 weeks and 55+ hours. Prerequisites usually include the introduction module plus several Grassroots courses. Missouri asks for "a minimum of three grassroots courses, in the form of two in person courses and one online course," one of which must be the 11v11 course. Cal South also expects access to a U7 to U12 team for the duration.

Cost is state administered, and the examples here show why you should ask rather than assume. Cal South lists a $550 course fee. Missouri and Nebraska do not publish a D License price on the pages checked, so contact your own association for the current figure.

Probably not, and here is whyThe C, B, and A Licenses

These exist, and no official source states outright that a volunteer should skip them, but the structure of each one makes the intended audience pretty clear. One state example, the Eastern Pennsylvania Youth Soccer Association, lists its C License as four months including the summative assessment, with four in person days and "80+ hours." It usually requires a D License held for at least 6 months, unless you meet a waiver, and "access to a team (U13+) in a participation environment for the duration of the course." Nebraska lists a C License fee of $1,599. The B License goes further still and is aimed at coaches working in older performance environments. That is a real career investment, not something to pick up because a rec team asked you to help out this fall. If you are coaching one team as a volunteer, the D License is very likely the ceiling worth reaching for.

A different pathUnited Soccer Coaches diplomas

United Soccer Coaches, formerly known as the NSCAA, runs a separate track from the U.S. Soccer license system. Its diplomas are education programs rather than licenses, so a diploma is not a substitute for a license. It is a different kind of credential, built around learning rather than certification.

The diplomas are organized by age group in a way that maps onto the same formats above. Its Foundations Diplomas page says the 4v4 Diploma, designed for coaches with players ages 8 and under, "can now be enjoyed and completed exclusively online." The 7v7/9v9 Diploma "focuses on the 9-12 year old player," and the 11v11/Pre-National Diploma is "designed for an intermediate coach" working with players 13 and older. Our goalkeeper basics guide draws on this same organization's separate Goalkeeping Level I Diploma, which shows how these diplomas fill gaps a general license course will not cover.

On cost, the organization's join page lists a Full Membership at $150 and says membership includes General Liability Insurance. A Recreation Coach tier is listed under Youth, but the page shows no price for it, so ask the organization directly whether it fits a volunteer better.

The actual verdictWhat is worth it, by situation

  • Every volunteer, no exceptions. SafeSport Trained, a background check, and whatever concussion training your state or club requires. The first two are required almost everywhere, the CDC course is free, and your club may cover some of the cost.
  • Coaching only the youngest recreational ages. The intro module, plus the 4v4 course if you want the content, even knowing it may not count toward a formal license in your state.
  • Coaching 7v7 through competitive club ages. The format specific Grassroots courses for your age group are genuinely useful and, in the examples above, inexpensive. They are worth doing even beyond whatever the minimum requirement is.
  • Coaching a competitive or travel team for multiple seasons. The D License is the real, worthwhile investment once you know you are staying in it.
  • Not sure yet? Do the required items and one Grassroots course this season. You can add the D License later, and you will know far more about what you want by then.
  • Considering the C License or beyond. Know exactly what you are signing up for first: a fee in the thousands in the example above, months of time, and a team you can coach through it.

One more low cost option many coaches overlook: most state associations also run referee courses. Taking one teaches you the Laws of the Game from the other side of the whistle. Check your state association for availability and fees before assuming it is offered near you.
