Coaching Players With ADHD

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach · October 21, 2026 · 17 min read

Somewhere on your roster there may be a player who hears the instruction, nods, and then does something else. It looks like not listening, and sometimes it is ADHD. The good news for a volunteer coach is that most of what helps (shorter instructions, less standing around, clear jobs, calm private feedback) helps every child on the team, so you do not need a diagnosis, a special program, or a second coach to start. This guide shows what to change in your practice, how to talk with the family, and how to use an AI assistant to redesign a session plan without ever typing a child's name or health information.

Who this guide is for

Volunteer and club coaches of players roughly ages 5 to 14, and the parents who want to help their coach help their child. It is not medical advice. A coach's job is to run a practice where more kids can succeed, not to diagnose, treat, or ask about treatment. If the behavior you are seeing is more about effort or attitude, start with our behavior and effort guide, and for a player who freezes rather than fidgets, see the quiet or anxious player guide.

The short version
  • Design for the whole team, not for a label. You can change the practice without knowing why a child is struggling.
  • Give one or two steps at a time, say them as commands, show them, and have players repeat them back.
  • Take the waiting out. Small groups, short drills, and a job during every break.
  • Correct privately with one short cue, and praise specific effort soon after it happens.
  • Ask every family the same open question, what helps their child at practice, and never ask for a diagnosis.
  • Use AI on the plan, never on the child. No names, no diagnoses, no photos, no parent notes in a prompt.
  • Soccer is good for kids, but it is not a treatment for ADHD, and the exercise research is thinner than the headlines suggest.

What to knowWhat ADHD looks like on a soccer team

The CDC estimates that about 7 million U.S. children aged 3 to 17, or 11.7 percent, had a current ADHD diagnosis in 2024, based on a national survey of parents. That is roughly one child in nine. For the ages most coaches work with the share is lower and climbs with age: in 2020 to 2022 data from CDC's National Center for Health Statistics, 8.6 percent of children aged 5 to 11 and 14.3 percent of those aged 12 to 17 had ever been diagnosed. On a roster of twelve younger players that works out to about one child on average, though your team could have none or two. Boys are diagnosed more often than girls (13 percent and 7 percent in CDC's 2022 to 2023 figures), but that describes diagnosis, not a reason to decide who on your team has it. You are not diagnosing anyone.

The CDC describes three presentations: mostly inattentive, mostly hyperactive and impulsive, and combined. A child might daydream, lose things, squirm, talk a lot, make careless mistakes, struggle to wait a turn, or take risks. The CDC also says children do not simply outgrow it, that no single test diagnoses it, and that sleep problems, anxiety, depression, and some learning disabilities can look similar. That last point is the practical one for a coach. The same behavior at practice can come from several causes, so the useful question is not "does this child have ADHD?" but "what could I change so this child can do the thing I just asked?"

Soccer asks for several hard things at once. Players have to wait through an explanation, hold the instruction in mind, resist grabbing the ball, and switch quickly between attacking and defending. Researchers call those skills working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility, and they are the ones studied in the exercise and ADHD research covered below. A child who struggles with them is not being rude. They are working against the way the session is built.

Before the seasonThe conversation with the family

Parents decide what to share, and many hold back because they do not want their child singled out. CHADD's guidance for coaches is to ask broad questions before the season, such as what helps a child do their best and what gets in the way, instead of asking about a specific diagnosis. A CHADD article by psychologist Carey Heller adds that a parent can either name the diagnosis or simply describe the attention and organization challenges, and that either helps a coach avoid mistaking lack of focus for lack of interest. USA Lacrosse's guide for coaches suggests asking about a child's interests, what motivates them, what distracts them, and what has worked with other coaches.

The simplest way to do this fairly is to send the same message to every family. Nobody is singled out, and the parents who have something to tell you have a natural place to say it. The AI prompt at the end of the practice section drafts that message for you.

If a parent does tell you, thank them, then ask two questions: what helps, and what would you like me to do when things are going badly. Keep it private. Do not mention it to the team, to other parents, or in the group chat, and do not write a child's diagnosis in a shared roster or team app. Do not ask a player in front of others whether they took their medication, and do not comment on it.

One safety point

A clinical review written for family doctors (Perrin and Jotwani, 2014) says stimulant medication can raise core body temperature and hide the feeling of tiredness, which is why heat is worth watching in athletes who take it. You are not the one to ask about medication, but you can run water breaks and shade for the whole team in hot weather, and if a family volunteers that their doctor gave them guidance for heat, follow it. This is one review, not a rule for every child.

Practice designRedesign the session, not the child

None of the advice below comes from a trial of soccer coaches. It comes from organizations and clinicians who work with these families, and it fits what teachers already know about attention and memory. It is reasonable practice, not a proven intervention, it costs nothing to try, and it helps the other eleven players too.

Fewer words, said once, shown once

Understood recommends giving direct commands instead of questions ("Dribble to the red cone" rather than "Can you dribble to the red cone?"), offering one or two directions at a time, and numbering the steps, because several steps in a row are hard to hold in working memory. CHADD adds that directions should be brief and repeated, and that players can repeat them back. A teacher would call that a check for understanding. You can do it without singling anyone out by asking the whole group to tell the player next to them what they are about to do. Show the drill once at game speed before anyone moves.

Make it visible

Cone colors and markers work as a map. CHADD describes visual and auditory cues, including hand signals and code words that let you redirect a player without a public correction, so agree on one with the whole team. Understood suggests picture schedules and checklists for following steps, which translate easily to a small whiteboard or a card showing what today's practice contains.

Take the waiting out

USA Lacrosse's coaching guide suggests short, high energy drills with movement breaks. In soccer that usually means small groups with a ball for every two or three players instead of a line, plus a job during transitions, such as setting out cones or demonstrating the next drill, which CHADD recommends for downtime. Allow controlled movement, too. CHADD suggests turning restlessness into small, purposeful motion, and USA Lacrosse says to allow fidgeting that helps a player self regulate, so a player who rolls the ball gently under a foot or bounces on their toes while you talk may still be listening.

Teach in parts, keep the shape the same

CHADD's example is to start with unguarded passes to targets, then add defenders. Our dribbling before passing guide and small sided game generator follow the same ladder. USA Lacrosse also recommends a consistent routine for self regulation and confidence, so open every practice the same way, for example free touches plus a job, and end with the same short closing circle.

Correct privately, praise specifically

CHADD's guidance is to give corrections privately, keep them objective, use a short verbal cue the player can remember, and avoid heavy punishment for behavior or effort because it can wear down self esteem further. USA Lacrosse recommends praising effort promptly so a child connects it with focus and progress. The popular "Magic Ratio" of five specific, truthful praises for each correction is a rule of thumb rather than a research finding, and our quiet or anxious player guide explains how to use it. Running as punishment teaches a child to dislike the game, a point covered in the behavior and effort guide.

Match dayGiving a player a job in the game

On match days, a clear job beats a general instruction. "Play well" gives a distracted player nothing to hold on to, while "When we lose the ball, get between it and our goal" is a task they can check themselves against. Keep the cue to one sentence, say it before kickoff, and say the same words in the first half. Substitutes need a job too. CHADD suggests giving a player who is not in the play a concrete task, such as counting passes or tracking a stat from the sideline, so the bench is time spent paying attention rather than waiting.

After a mistake, use the same reset phrase every time, and after a loss spend your first words on something specific the player did well. Our in game coaching guide covers how much to say during play. Do not steer a player into or away from a position because of ADHD. Rotate positions as you would for any child, using our positions guide.

AI promptsUse AI on the plan, never on the child

An AI assistant is useful here because the hard part of this work is tedious: going through a practice plan line by line and cutting words, waiting, and steps. That is exactly the kind of editing it does quickly. It cannot see your players, and it should never be asked to guess whether a particular child has ADHD. Put in only the age group, the number of players, the session length, the field size, the equipment, and your plan. Do not put in names, photos, birthdates, a diagnosis linked to any child, or anything a parent told you privately. If you need to refer to a player at all, describe a type of difficulty in general terms, such as "a player who has trouble waiting". That is the same standard used throughout these guides: jersey numbers or initials, or nothing identifying at all.

Prompt 1: Rebuild a plan you already have

"Here is my practice plan for [age group] players. I have [number] players, [number] minutes, a [field size] field, and [equipment]. [Paste the plan.] Rebuild it with these rules. Each instruction is two steps or fewer, in two short sentences or fewer. Find every moment a player stands and waits, and replace it with small groups or a job. Add a movement break after any explanation longer than one minute. Teach each new skill in parts, from easy to hard. Add one simple visual cue per drill, such as cone colors or a hand signal. Keep the same opening and closing routine. Do not give medical advice or speculate about any child. Show the result as a table with minutes, activity, what I say in 15 words or fewer, and player jobs, and tell me if you are unsure whether something suits the age group." Paste your own plan after the brackets

Prompt 2: Shorten your instructions

"Rewrite these instructions for [age group] players. Each should be a direct command of 12 words or fewer, numbered if there is more than one step, followed by a note on what I should demonstrate. Do not add rules I did not give you. [Paste instructions.]" Best for the drills where your explanation runs long

Here is the kind of change to expect. Before: "Okay everyone, in this drill I want you to split into two groups, and the first group will be attackers who start with the ball at the cone and try to get past the defenders and then shoot, and when the ball goes out we switch." After: "Two groups. Attackers: dribble past a defender, then shoot. Defenders: win the ball. Ball goes out, switch jobs. Watch me do it once." The second version is shorter, has the steps in order, and ends with a demonstration.

Prompt 3: Find the weak points

"Here is my revised practice plan: [paste it]. Act as an experienced youth coach. Pick the three moments where a player who has trouble waiting, holding several steps in mind, or staying focused through quiet stretches would struggle most, and say why. For each, suggest one change that keeps the activity the same for everyone. Do not diagnose anyone." Run it on the plan from Prompt 1

Prompt 4: Write the same note to every family

"Write a short, warm message from a volunteer youth soccer coach to all parents before the season. Ask every family three things: what helps their child do their best at practice, what tends to get in the way, and what has worked with other coaches. Say answers are optional and shared only with me, and that I do not need a diagnosis or any medical details. Keep it under 120 words and plain, in my voice. Here is how I usually write: [one or two sentences of your own]." Send the finished note to the whole team at once

Check what comes back. Count the steps in each instruction yourself, make sure the minutes add up to your session, delete anything that says "research shows" without naming a source, and read every line as a nine year old would hear it. The plan is a draft. You know your players and your field, and the AI does not. Our practice planning guide and guide to using AI as a coach cover the basics of both.

Honest limitsWhat the research does and does not say

It is tempting to tell a parent that soccer will help their child's ADHD. The evidence is encouraging but early, and the honest version is more useful.

  • Sport and exercise. A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis in Frontiers in Psychiatry (Xie and colleagues) pooled 23 studies of physical activity programs, mostly in children and adolescents. Before and after studies showed improvements in inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity, and emotional and behavioral problems. In the studies that had a comparison group, only the improvement in inattention was significant. The samples were small, results varied for some outcomes, and the authors called for large, rigorous, blinded trials. The review found open skill activities such as racket sports and obstacle courses linked with less inattention. Soccer is usually classed as an open skill sport, but this review does not single it out.
  • Structured aerobic exercise. A 2024 meta-analysis in Frontiers in Psychology (Yang and colleagues) combined nine randomized trials with 377 children aged 6 to 12. It found the largest benefit for inhibitory control, a moderate one for cognitive flexibility, and a smaller, less certain one for working memory. The programs typically ran two to three sessions a week for 30 to 90 minutes, which is not the same as one Saturday game. The authors noted weak methods, little blinding, samples that were about 80 percent boys, and inconsistent handling of medication, and advised caution.
  • Treatment. The American Academy of Pediatrics' 2019 guideline, as summarized in its clinician training materials, lists parent training in behavior management and classroom behavioral interventions, along with FDA approved medication, as first line options for ages 6 to 11, with school supports as a necessary part of the plan. The summary I read does not mention exercise. Sport is a good part of a child's week, not a replacement for what their doctor and family have chosen.
  • Players' own experience. One small qualitative study at the University of Alberta interviewed six young men aged 17 to 26 with ADHD who had played at least three seasons of organized team sport. They found sport enjoyable and beneficial, said symptoms strained their relationships with coaches and teammates, and said supportive coaches softened those effects. Six people cannot prove anything, but the finding matches what the coaching advice says.

When it is not workingKeep notes on what you see, not what you suspect

If a change does not help, keep a short log: what happened, when in the session, and what you tried. Write facts such as "left the group three times in the ten minutes after the water break," not labels such as "can't focus." Then talk with the family privately, starting from the observation and a question: "I've noticed this happens after water breaks. What have you seen help?" Never open with "I think he might have ADHD." That is a diagnosis, it is not yours to make, and it will end the conversation.

If a player's behavior puts anyone at risk, such as running toward a road or hitting teammates, bring in the parent and your club's coordinator, since you should not carry that alone. USA Lacrosse suggests a trusted teammate as peer support, which is worth doing only with the family's okay and without explaining why to the group. If the team asks why a player has a different job, "everybody is working on something different" is a complete and truthful answer.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Asking a parent whether their child has ADHD, when "what helps your child at practice?" gets you more and puts no one on the spot.
  • Announcing a diagnosis or posting it anywhere shared, including the roster, group chat, or team app.
  • Guessing at a cause out loud. The same behavior has many explanations, and sorting them out is not your job.
  • Using running or heavy punishment for behavior that may be about attention.
  • Praising only goals and wins, instead of effort and specific actions a player controls.
  • Putting a child's name or diagnosis into an AI prompt, or asking the AI to guess whether a child has ADHD.
  • Telling a family that soccer will fix it. It is good for children, but the research does not support calling it treatment.
