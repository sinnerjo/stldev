Coaching Youth Soccer When You Never Played: What Actually Matters, Versus What Parents Assume You Need to Know

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 24, 2026 · 11 min read

Every rec league needs more coaches than it has former players to fill those spots, so a lot of teams end up led by a parent who has never kicked a competitive ball in their life. If that is you, the anxiety is usually less about the players and more about the sideline, worrying that some parent who did play will spot the gap right away. Here is the more useful truth: the things that actually make a coach good have very little to do with your own playing history, and the specific gaps that do matter are closable in a weekend, not a career.

Who this guide is for

Coaches, often parent volunteers, taking on a team without ever having played soccer themselves, and wondering what they actually need to learn versus what they are only assuming they need. If this is also your very first season coaching anything, our first season guide covers the broader basics.

The short version
  • Playing experience and teaching ability are different skills. Plenty of former players never learned to coach, and plenty of non players teach it well.
  • Most coaches, including ones who played, are not formally trained either. You are closer to the norm than you think.
  • What actually matters is communication, fairness, and genuine investment in each player, not tactical depth.
  • Young players need far less technical instruction than most new coaches assume, and far more time actually playing.
  • Close real gaps on purpose, with a free course and a season's worth of practice plans, rather than trying to fake expertise.
  • Keep learning if you want to, through your state association's coaching license path or a membership group like United Soccer Coaches, once the free course is not enough.
  • When you cannot demonstrate something yourself, that is a solvable logistics problem, not a disqualifying one.
  • Be yourself on the sideline, not a persona borrowed from a coach you saw on TV.

The myth"If you cannot do it, you cannot teach it"

This is the belief doing most of the damage to new coaches before they have even run a practice, and it does not hold up. One coaching resource makes the case by pulling apart three things people tend to lump together: never having played the sport, being inexperienced as a coach, and giving low quality instruction. Those are three separate variables, not three names for the same problem, and a coach can be new to soccer while being excellent at the actual job of teaching it.

That same resource breaks down what effective teaching of any physical skill actually requires: content knowledge, meaning you understand the skill deeply, practical experience observing and coaching it, the ability to communicate clearly, and pedagogy, which is the skill of breaking a movement into parts, designing a good practice environment, and giving useful feedback. Notice what is missing from that list. Personal elite execution of the skill yourself is not one of the requirements. As the same piece puts it, slowing a skill down and breaking it into parts is "the standard method for teaching almost any physical movement," and demonstrating it yourself at game speed was never actually the pedagogically necessary part.

It is also worth naming honestly why most coaches did play growing up. Coaching, especially at the youth level, tends to pay little or nothing, which draws people motivated by love of the game rather than a paycheck, and people who loved a game growing up are more likely to have played it. That is a fact about who tends to volunteer, not a fact about what the job of coaching actually demands.

Reality checkWhat actually makes a coach good

One coach who built a career after never playing at a competitive level puts the whole case in a single line: "it's not the playing experience that makes you successful at coaching soccer, it's the ability to connect with your players and inspire them to want to grow and improve." She also points out an upside to starting without a playing background, not having a shortcut through instinct forced her to actually learn concepts thoroughly before she could explain them, which made her a clearer teacher, not a worse one.

The Positive Coaching Alliance lists what separates a good youth coach from an average one, and none of the five items on the list is a soccer résumé:

  • Empathy for the athlete as a whole person.
  • Clear and specific communication.
  • A focus on development over winning.
  • Consistency and integrity in how standards get applied.
  • Emotional regulation to keep the environment calm under pressure.

Their conclusion is direct: "being a good coach requires much more than technical knowledge or competitive experience."

You are also in far more company than the anxiety suggests. Research cited by the Aspen Institute's Project Play found that in 2017, only 36 percent of youth coaches nationally had received training in effective coaching techniques, and the number for soccer specifically was even lower, at 28 percent. Most of the coaches on the opposing sideline who did play never received formal training either. Feeling underprepared is closer to the default experience of coaching than the exception to it.

ExpectationsWhat parents assume you need, versus what you actually need

Parents watching from the sideline often assume a coach needs deep tactical knowledge, a real formation, and the instincts of someone who played the game at a high level. What young players actually need looks almost nothing like that. One long time coaching resource focused specifically on volunteer recreational coaches puts it bluntly: at this level, the entire approach should be "no lines, no laps, no lectures," built around small sided games like 1v1, 2v1, and 2v2 rather than formal drills borrowed from adult training.

That same guidance also corrects a specific misconception new coaches often import from other sports without realizing it. A young player who keeps tripping over the ball is not lacking strength or muscle, and stretching or sprinting drills will not fix it. What that player is missing is coordination and simple familiarity with the ball, which only comes from touching it far more often, not from anything resembling a fitness program. Letting players actually play, far more than any tactical instruction you deliver, is the single most effective teaching tool available to a coach at this level, and it costs you nothing in soccer specific expertise.

Closing the gapLearn the real gaps on purpose

None of this means there is nothing to learn. It means the learning curve is narrower and faster than it feels from the outside, and it is worth closing on purpose rather than hoping nobody notices. The coach quoted earlier describes doing this herself through focused self study of tactics, history, and the game, treating it the same way anyone picks up a new subject seriously, plus pursuing coaching courses specifically, both to build real knowledge and to build credibility with families who may be quick to doubt an unfamiliar coach.

The most direct version of that path already exists and is free. U.S. Soccer's Grassroots coaching pathway starts with an introductory module, followed by format specific courses for 4v4, 7v7, 9v9, and 11v11, each available as a two hour online course. The stated goals are exactly the gaps a new coach actually has: understanding the basic needs of players at that age, the basic structure of the game, the basic structure of a training session, and the basic tasks of a grassroots coach. Finishing the introductory module also comes with four ready made practice plans, which solves the separate, very real problem of not knowing how to fill an hour on the field.

Coach educationWhere to go once the free course is not enough

The Grassroots pathway above is the first segment of a longer path, not the whole thing, and it is worth knowing what comes next even if you never go further than the free courses. U.S. Soccer's own description of the pathway is direct about that: the eight Grassroots License Courses "complete the Grassroots Pathway segment of the full U.S. Soccer Coaching License Pathway." Everything past that point runs through your state association rather than a single national course, so what is actually offered, and what it costs, will vary by state.

  • U.S. Soccer D License. The usual next step, run by your state youth soccer association. One state association describes it as a course that "builds upon the Grassroots Courses," typically taught over two weekends of field and classroom work, with a couple of Grassroots courses required first.
  • U.S. Soccer C, B, and A Licenses. Further steps for coaches who want to keep going, each longer and more involved than the last. U.S. Soccer describes its B License course as running over about six months, "a combination of virtual learning and in-person meetings," with two separate four day in-person sessions.
  • United Soccer Coaches. A membership organization with its own online catalog, separate from the U.S. Soccer pathway. Its eLearning courses let members "learn from some of the best coaches in the game from the comfort of your own home," and its Development Diplomas are built "from novice to intermediate," which is a reasonable place for a first or second year coach to start.

None of this is required reading before your first practice. Treat it as a menu to come back to once the free Grassroots course is finished and you have a season or two of actual coaching behind you, enough to know which parts of the game you actually want to go deeper on.

On the fieldWhen you cannot demonstrate a skill yourself

This is the moment new coaches dread most, a player asking you to show them a move you cannot actually perform. It is a logistics problem, not a career ending one. Pick a player on your own team who can demonstrate it well and let them show the group while you narrate what to watch for, point to a video if you have one handy, or be honest and use a little humor about it. Players generally respect a coach who is straightforward about a limit far more than one who is caught faking it, and none of it changes whether you can still explain what a good version of the skill looks like, break it into steps, and give useful feedback once a player attempts it, which is the part of teaching that actually matters.

A common wrinkleIf you are also coaching your own child

Plenty of new coaches take the job specifically because their own kid is on the team, which adds a layer worth planning for separately from the playing experience question. One coaching resource focused on parent coaches recommends building in support and transparency from the start, recruiting an assistant coach rather than running the team entirely solo, and setting clear, written standards for playing time and positions that apply the same way to your own child as to everyone else's. It is also worth a real conversation with your own kid beforehand about what having a parent as coach will actually be like for them, since it changes their season too, not just yours.

Your own voiceBe yourself, not the coach you see on TV

New coaches without a playing background sometimes reach for a coaching persona borrowed from somewhere else, the intense sideline energy from a broadcast, or the demeanor of whichever famous coach they admire. It rarely fits, and players notice a performance faster than adults tend to assume.

One coach's description of this same instinct, from outside soccer, applies just as well here: "at the end of the day, you can't be anybody else. You have to be yourself." The coaches who actually connect with young players are not imitating anyone. They found what they are naturally good at, patience, humor, organization, a knack for explaining things simply, quiet encouragement, and built their coaching around that instead of a borrowed style that never quite fit.

This matters more, not less, for a coach without a soccer background. You are not going to out coach anyone on tactical detail in your first season, and trying to sound like you have could cost you more credibility than a genuine strength would have earned instead. Lean into whatever you are actually good at. A coach who is warm and organized but tactically simple will do more for a group of eight year olds than one imitating an intensity that was never really theirs.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Assuming you need deep tactical knowledge before you can run a good practice for young players.
  • Copying adult training methods, like laps and lecture heavy drills, instead of letting players actually play.
  • Faking a demonstration instead of handing it to a capable player or simply being honest about it.
  • Trying to learn everything at once instead of starting with one free, structured course built for exactly this gap.
  • Running the team entirely alone when a parent of your own child, an assistant coach and a written playing time policy would remove most of the pressure.
  • Imitating a coaching persona instead of leaning into whatever you are actually good at.
  • Assuming the coach on the other sideline knows more than you do. Most coaches, regardless of background, were never formally trained either.
