The Player Who Freezes Up: Building Confidence in a Quiet or Anxious Kid

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of MatchTap · September 21, 2026, updated October 18, 2026 · 15 min read

Every coach eventually gets a player who goes quiet on the field, drifts to the edge of play, and seems to be trying to disappear rather than compete. It can look like low effort from the sideline, but it is not the same problem. A player who has checked out is bored or discouraged. A player who is anxious is scared, usually of making a mistake in front of everyone watching, and the two need almost opposite responses.

Who this guide is for

Coaches with a player who is quiet, anxious, or seems to freeze rather than misbehave or visibly stop trying. If your player is checked out, defiant, or not putting in effort, our behavior and effort guide covers that different problem. If a player who used to love the sport is now dreading it, our burnout guide covers that pattern specifically.

The short version
  • Recognize the difference. A freeze response looks passive, but it comes from fear, not apathy or defiance.
  • Be consistent. A predictable coach and a predictable routine give an anxious kid one less thing to brace for.
  • Set small, obtainable goals. Confidence is built through real, achievable wins stacked on top of each other, not one big leap.
  • Praise often, and be specific. A quiet kid needs more evidence that they belong than a confident kid does.
  • Cut the negative language entirely. A correction that lands fine with a confident player can shut an anxious one down for the rest of the game.
  • Ask questions instead of giving orders. Handing a player real choices builds the sense of control that anxiety takes away.
  • Keep quiet words in sight of other adults. Away from teammates is enough, and it keeps the conversation safe for everyone.
  • Know when it is more than shyness. If worry lasts and interferes with play, the next step is the family and their doctor, not a bigger coaching plan.

Recognizing itFear looks different from apathy

Catherine Pearlman, a licensed clinical social worker with a PhD who writes about coaching shy kids for MOJO Sports, describes what to look for: shy kids have difficulty making eye contact and "often speak in a quiet tone or not at all in social situations." On the field, that same instinct shows up as a player who will not call for the ball, drifts toward the edge of the play instead of into it, and over apologizes for small mistakes nobody else even noticed.

What is happening underneath is closer to a freeze response than a discipline problem. Patrick Cohn, a sports psychologist who runs Youth Sports Psychology, writes about athletes who freeze, and quotes sports performance consultant Pomai Miyata on how it can feel. In competition, athletes might say "My stomach hurts, my legs feel like jelly, I'm not in control of my body." Miyata adds that when that happens, "athletes lose access to their muscle memory and things aren't automatic." That is a practitioner's view, not a study, but it explains why a normally capable player can look lost on a play they could make in their sleep at practice. Treating that moment as a discipline issue, benching a player who is frozen the same way you would a player who is dogging it, punishes fear as if it were a choice.

ConsistencyBe the one predictable thing

Of the ideas behind this guide, this one has the softest research backing but strong support from people who coach anxious kids. A study by Baker, Cote, and Hawes of 228 varsity and regional athletes, with an average age of about 18, found that coaching behaviors around competition strategies, which included "maintaining consistency during competitions," were linked to less anxiety, concentration disruption, and worry. The same study found that a negative personal rapport with the coach predicted higher scores on all four anxiety measures. It was a one time snapshot of older athletes, and it did not test consistency on its own or whether it helps an already anxious kid more than a confident one.

The more direct advice comes from Pearlman: shy kids like to mentally prepare for variations in their environment, so give the shy child updates and warnings to allow time for adjustment. A coach who runs the same warm up in the same order, gives a heads up before a new drill, and reacts the same way to a mistake every time is removing one entire category of thing an anxious kid has to brace for.

Small goalsConfidence is built, not announced

This is the best supported idea in this guide, and it has a name in sports psychology: Albert Bandura's concept of mastery experiences. Simply Psychology's summary of his theory calls mastery experience the strongest source of self efficacy, because successfully taking on a new challenge gives direct proof of capability, ahead of encouragement or watching others succeed. The sequencing matters as much as the goal itself. PositivePsychology.com, summarizing Bandura, warns that "regularly achieving easy success with little effort can lead people to expect rapid results," which can leave people easily discouraged by failure. The opposite mistake, a goal that is too big too soon, risks a setback before any real confidence exists to absorb it.

Applied on the field, that means picking one specific, genuinely achievable target for this player this week, such as calling for the ball once or taking one first touch under light pressure, and treating that single success as real evidence rather than a footnote. One sport psychology article makes this the coach's job under the heading "Getting athletes to feel they have mastered a skill." A pep talk about potential is not the same thing.

PraisePraise often, and make it specific

The Positive Coaching Alliance describes a "Magic Ratio" of 5:1 for keeping an athlete's Emotional Tank full: five parts specific, truthful praise for each piece of constructive correction. Treat it as a rule of thumb. For a quiet or anxious player it may matter more than for a confident teammate, because a shy kid is not walking around with much evidence of their own that they belong on the field.

A research summary behind the PCA's coaching model reports a 1979 study of male baseball players by Smith, Smoll, and Curtis, in which players who started with low self esteem benefited the most from positive coaching techniques. The same summary cites a gymnastics dissertation finding that gymnasts who perceived high amounts of punishment oriented feedback were more likely to quit. That one is about perceptions, and it shows an association, not that the feedback caused the quitting. Neither study was about soccer or anxious kids specifically, so use them as support for the habit, not proof.

The praise has to be specific and true to actually land. An empty "good job" does not give a doubting kid anything to hold onto, but "you called for that ball, that is exactly what we talked about" does. Pearlman also recommends rewarding a shy player's participation with quiet praise, which leads to the next point about delivery.

LanguageCut the negative language, not just the volume

A study of 1,166 athletes ages 9 to 18 by Ramis and colleagues found that a coach's controlling style was linked to higher competitive anxiety on all three forms measured, and the authors suggest it could be one cause of athletes' anxiety. It was a one time survey, so it shows an association, not proof. Patrick Cohn adds a practical observation: critical coaches hurt kids' confidence and make them perform tentatively. No study we found shows that a critical comment does more damage to an already anxious kid than a confident one, but the logic holds up, and the practitioner advice is consistent: a confident player can often shrug off a sharp correction, while the same words can convince an anxious player that the fear they already felt was justified. This is a case for zero negative language with this specific player, not just less of it. Phrase every correction as what to do next instead of what went wrong.

AutonomyAsk questions, do not just give orders

A six week study by Conroy and Coatsworth of 165 youth in a recreational summer swim league looked at two autonomy supportive coaching behaviors: showing interest in the athlete's input and praising autonomous behavior. Each predicted gains in satisfaction of all three basic psychological needs, which in self determination theory are competence, autonomy, and relatedness. The opposite style carries a cost tied to this problem. A survey of 252 athletes, mostly teens and adults, by Liu and colleagues found a controlling coaching style linked to higher fear of failure, indirectly through lower satisfaction of those basic needs. Both studies are one sport or one snapshot, so treat them as support, not proof.

In practice, this can be as simple as asking a player which side they want to receive a pass on instead of telling them, or asking what they saw on a play before offering your own read of it. A small, genuine choice hands a player a sliver of control in a sport that otherwise asks them to react to things happening at high speed all around them.

ContributionGive them a low pressure way in, not a spotlight

Inner Game Basketball, a youth basketball program, opens with a reminder that a shy kid is not broken and not going to be like this forever. It also makes a useful point about activity design: a drill where every kid takes a turn while everyone watches is a shy kid's nightmare, while a small sided game where everyone plays at once and nobody is watching any one kid is where shy kids relax and play. That lines up with the small sided, low lecture approach our own practice planning guide already recommends for everyone, and it is the format an anxious kid needs most. The same source notes that being able to see you in the room gives a nervous kid an anchor, and that as they get comfortable you can gradually step back.

A small role can be another low pressure way in. These are our suggestions, not research findings: handing out pinnies, managing the ball bag, counting the warm up, or keeping score in a small sided game. Pearlman suggests inviting a shy player to take part instead of pushing them, and rewarding it with quiet praise.

One adjustment to a rule that works well elsewhere on this site: our behavior guide recommends correcting quietly and praising publicly, and that still holds for a discipline conversation. For a genuinely shy kid, public praise can misfire the same way public correction does. Pearlman notes that shy kids "do not like to be singled out or feel different." A quiet word with this player, or praise folded into a normal team moment instead of a spotlight moment, often lands better than calling their name out in front of everyone. Keep that quiet word in view of other adults. The U.S. Center for SafeSport's one on one interactions policy says contact between an adult and a minor athlete must be "observable and interruptible," so talk away from teammates' ears, not out of sight.

When to get helpWhen it is more than shyness

Shyness and nerves are common and usually ease with the approach above. A coach is not a clinician, and this guide cannot tell you whether a player's anxiety needs more. The CDC says fears and worries are typical for children and become a concern when they do not fade or when they interfere with school, home, or play. It lists headaches and stomach aches among the physical signs, and notes that some anxious children keep their worries to themselves, so the symptoms can be missed.

If you see signs that last for several weeks, which is our rule of thumb, such as a player who dreads or refuses to come, complains of aches before games, or seems distressed instead of just quiet, a short word with the parent is the right step. Do it away from the players and in view of other adults. Share what you saw, ask how things are going at home, and let the family decide whether to talk to their child's doctor, which the CDC names as the first step. Do not guess a diagnosis or label the child. If a child is ever in crisis, call or text 988.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Treating a freeze response like a discipline problem, and benching a scared player the same way you would a player who is not trying.
  • Setting a goal too big for where this player actually is, which risks a setback before any real confidence exists.
  • Praising in a way that is vague or public when this specific kid would rather hear it quietly and specifically.
  • Softening criticism instead of cutting it entirely for a player who reads even mild correction as confirmation of their fear.
  • Making every decision for them instead of handing over small, real choices they can own.
  • Forgetting that this is not permanent. A shy or anxious player at eight is not the same player at eleven if the season goes well.
  • Having the quiet word where no other adult can see, when the safe version is away from teammates but in view of others.
  • Waiting out worry that keeps growing, instead of gently telling the family what you have seen.

Try thisDo this week

Pick one quiet player and one small goal for the week, and tell them the plan before practice, so there are no surprises. Give them one small role. Run your warm up in the same order each session. At the end of the week, notice one specific thing they did and tell them quietly, in view of another adult. These steps are our suggestion, but they put the most supported ideas in this guide, small wins and predictability, into practice.

Ask your AI assistant coachBrainstorm small goals, and prepare the parent conversation

AI is useful here for brainstorming and for finding calm words, not for judging a child. It cannot tell you whether a player is anxious or why, and it should never be asked to diagnose. If you are new to AI, start with our beginner guide, and for answers that sound like you, set up a coaching profile first. Our guide on using AI as a youth soccer coach covers the basics.

1. Small goals and praise. Good output is a short list of tiny goals and a few exact phrases you can say.

"I coach [age group] [format]. I have a quiet player who avoids the ball and rarely calls for a pass. Suggest five small, genuinely achievable goals I could set for this player over the next two weeks, one at a time, and give me three or four specific praise phrases I could use when they reach one, rather than a generic good job." Paste this before practice, then bring the goal with you

2. The parent conversation. Good output is a few calm sentences, with no diagnosis and no promises.

"I coach [age group] [format]. I have a quiet player, and I want to explain my approach to their parent: consistency, small achievable goals, and specific praise, rather than pushing them to be more outgoing. Help me write four or five calm, plain sentences I could say, describing what I am doing and why, without diagnosing their child or promising a specific outcome." Paste this before a parent conversation about your approach, not during it

3. A pressure test. Once you have a plan, ask the AI to find where it might backfire.

"Here is my plan for a quiet player: [paste it]. Act as an experienced youth coach and point out any step that could put this player on display, add pressure, or confuse a parent. Do not rewrite it, just list the problems." Fix what it finds, then use the plan this week

Describe the player only in general terms, such as "a quiet U10 player" or "one of my forwards," never by name or with enough detail that a stranger reading it could identify a real child or their family. Use AI to think through your own approach and language, not to evaluate or diagnose an actual player, or to guess how a specific parent will react. AI can also invent research and quotes, so check any source it names before you repeat it.
