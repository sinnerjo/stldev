The Player Who Freezes Up: Building Confidence in a Quiet or Anxious Kid

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 29, 2026 · 9 min read

Every coach eventually gets a player who goes quiet on the field, drifts to the edge of play, and seems to be trying to disappear rather than compete. It can look like low effort from the sideline, but it is not the same problem. A player who has checked out is bored or discouraged. A player who is anxious is scared, usually of making a mistake in front of everyone watching, and the two need almost opposite responses.

Who this guide is for

Coaches with a player who is quiet, anxious, or seems to freeze rather than misbehave or visibly stop trying. If your player is checked out, defiant, or not putting in effort, our behavior and effort guide covers that different problem. If a player who used to love the sport is now dreading it, our burnout guide covers that pattern specifically.

The short version
  • Recognize the difference. A freeze response looks passive, but it comes from fear, not apathy or defiance.
  • Be consistent. A predictable coach and a predictable routine give an anxious kid one less thing to brace for.
  • Set small, obtainable goals. Confidence is built through real, achievable wins stacked on top of each other, not one big leap.
  • Praise often, and be specific. A quiet kid needs more evidence that they belong than a confident kid does.
  • Cut the negative language entirely. A correction that lands fine with a confident player can shut an anxious one down for the rest of the game.
  • Ask questions instead of giving orders. Handing a player real choices builds the sense of control that anxiety takes away.

Recognizing itFear looks different from apathy

One clinical psychologist who writes specifically about coaching shy kids describes what to actually look for: "shy kids have difficulty making eye contact" and "often speak in a quiet tone or not at all in social situations." On the field, that same instinct shows up as a player who will not call for the ball, drifts toward the edge of the play instead of into it, and over apologizes for small mistakes nobody else even noticed.

What is happening underneath is closer to a freeze response than a discipline problem. A youth sports psychologist describes the physical experience directly, from an athlete's own words: "my stomach hurts, my legs feel like jelly, I'm not in control of my body." Once that sets in, he explains, "athletes lose access to their muscle memory and things aren't automatic," which is exactly why a normally capable player can look lost on a play they could make in their sleep at practice. Treating that moment as a discipline issue, benching a player who is frozen the same way you would a player who is dogging it, punishes fear as if it were a choice.

ConsistencyBe the one predictable thing

Of the ideas behind this guide, this one has the softest research backing but strong support from people who actually coach anxious kids for a living. Research on coaching behavior and sport anxiety found that competition strategies including "maintaining consistency during competitions" were linked to less anxiety related disruption in young athletes, though that study did not test whether it helps an already anxious kid more than a confident one. The more direct advice comes from a psychologist who coaches shy kids specifically: "shy kids like to mentally prepare for variations in their environment. Give the shy child updates and warnings to allow time for adjustment." A coach who runs the same warm up in the same order, gives a heads up before a new drill, and reacts the same way to a mistake every time is removing one entire category of thing an anxious kid has to brace for.

Small goalsConfidence is built, not announced

This is the best supported idea in this guide, and it has a name in sports psychology: Albert Bandura's concept of mastery experiences. The theory holds that "mastery experience is the strongest source of self efficacy, successfully taking on a new challenge gives direct proof of capability," ahead of encouragement, watching others succeed, or anything else a coach can say. The sequencing matters as much as the goal itself. One summary of Bandura's work warns that "regularly achieving easy success with little effort can lead people to expect rapid results," while the opposite mistake, a goal that is too big too soon, risks a setback before any real confidence exists to absorb it. Applied on the field, that means picking one specific, genuinely achievable target for this player this week, calling for the ball once, taking one first touch under light pressure, and treating that single success as real evidence rather than a footnote, which is exactly how one sports psychology resource frames the coach's actual job here: "getting athletes to feel they have mastered a skill" is what changes their own sense of their ability, not a pep talk about potential.

PraisePraise often, and make it specific

The Positive Coaching Alliance puts a number on this that is worth actually using as a rule of thumb: "the magic ratio for a full tank is 5 to 1, five parts specific, truthful praise for each piece of constructive correction." For a quiet or anxious player, that ratio matters more than it does for a confident teammate, because a shy kid is not walking around with much evidence of their own that they belong on the field. A research summary on positive coaching techniques found that "players who started with low self esteem benefited the most from positive coaching techniques," while athletes who felt they were mostly getting punishment oriented feedback were more likely to quit the sport altogether. The praise has to be specific and true to actually land, an empty "good job" does not give a doubting kid anything to hold onto, but "you called for that ball, that is exactly what we talked about" does.

LanguageCut the negative language, not just the volume

Research on controlling coaching styles, questioning, criticizing, and pressuring language, found it to be a real predictor of competitive anxiety in young athletes generally. A sports psychologist who works with young athletes puts the practical effect plainly: "when coaches are critical, it hurts kids' confidence and makes them perform tentatively." No study specifically proves that a critical comment does more damage to an already anxious kid than a confident one, but the logic holds up and the practitioner advice is consistent: a confident player can often shrug off a sharp correction, while the same words can convince an anxious player that the fear they already felt was justified. This is a case for zero negative language with this specific player, not just less of it, phrase every correction as what to do next instead of what went wrong.

AutonomyAsk questions, do not just give orders

Research on autonomy supportive coaching, showing real interest in an athlete's own input and praising them for decisions they made themselves, found it directly predicted better satisfaction of players' basic psychological needs, including their sense of competence and control. The opposite style carries a real cost specifically tied to this problem: a more recent study found that a controlling coaching style was linked to a higher fear of failure in young athletes, largely by undermining exactly the sense of autonomy an anxious kid is already short on. In practice, this can be as simple as asking a player which side they want to receive a pass on instead of telling them, or asking what they saw on a play before offering your own read of it. A small, genuine choice hands a player a sliver of control in a sport that otherwise asks them to react to things happening at high speed all around them.

ContributionGive them a low pressure way in, not a spotlight

A coaching resource focused on exactly this kind of kid opens with the right framing: "that kid is not broken, they're not bad at sport, they're not going to be like this forever." The same source makes a specific, useful point about activity design: "a drill where every kid takes a turn while everyone watches is a shy kid's nightmare. A small sided game where everyone is playing at once and nobody is watching any one kid, that's where shy kids start to relax and actually play." That lines up exactly with the small sided, low lecture approach our own practice planning guide already recommends for everyone, and it happens to be the format an anxious kid needs most. Staying physically near a nervous player early on helps too, the same resource notes that "being able to see you in the room gives a nervous kid an anchor," before adding the part coaches often skip, "as they get comfortable, you can gradually step back."

One adjustment worth making to a rule that works well everywhere else on this site: our behavior guide recommends correcting in private and praising in public, and that still holds for a discipline conversation. For a genuinely shy kid, public praise can misfire the same way public correction does. The same resource on shy kids in sports notes plainly that "shy kids do not like to be singled out or feel different," even positively. A quiet word to this player alone, or praise folded into a normal team moment rather than a spotlight moment, often lands better than calling their name out in front of everyone.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Treating a freeze response like a discipline problem, and benching a scared player the same way you would a player who is not trying.
  • Setting a goal too big for where this player actually is, which risks a setback before any real confidence exists.
  • Praising in a way that is vague or public when this specific kid would rather hear it quietly and specifically.
  • Softening criticism instead of cutting it entirely for a player who reads even mild correction as confirmation of their fear.
  • Making every decision for them instead of handing over small, real choices they can own.
  • Forgetting that this is not permanent. A shy or anxious player at eight is not the same player at eleven if the season goes well.
