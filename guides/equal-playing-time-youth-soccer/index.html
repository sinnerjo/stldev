Equal Playing Time in Rec and Club Soccer: A Guide for 3v3, 7v7, 9v9 and 11v11

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 20, 2026 · 10 min read

Every season, a recreational or club youth coach faces the same quiet decision: who plays, and for how long? Our answer is simple. Through 9v9, playing time should be as close to equal as you can make it. At every age, including 11v11, no healthy and available player should get less than half of a game.

Who this guide is for

This guide is written for recreational and club soccer. Recreational means community leagues where anyone can sign up and volunteers usually coach. Club means teams that hold tryouts and play in competitive or travel leagues. The standard is the same in both: through 9v9, level of play does not decide minutes, whether the team is recreational or a club team that picked its roster at tryouts. We are not writing about high school varsity, college or professional academy settings, which have their own selection rules. We cite academy research only because it shows that even elite programs use a minimum.

The short versionOur playing time standard, by format

FormatTypical agesPlaying time standard
3v3 and 4v4U6 to U8Equal minutes. Rotate on a timer. No exceptions beyond injury or absence.
7v7U9 to U10Equal minutes across the season. At least 50% of every game.
9v9U11 to U12Same as 7v7, and rotate positions too. Level of play does not decide minutes.
11v11U13 and upRoles and starting spots can be earned. The 50% floor still applies to every player in every game.

Two clarifications so we are being fair to real coaching. First, equal is a season long goal, not a stopwatch contest inside every game. Some games will not split evenly, and that is fine. Second, the 50% floor is per game. It is what makes the season goal reachable, because it stops any one player from quietly falling behind. The floor only bends for real reasons: injury, illness, arriving late, or a discipline issue you have talked through with the player.

Formats and age bands come from U.S. Soccer's Player Development Initiatives, which set 4v4 for U8 and younger, 7v7 for U9 and U10, and 9v9 for U11 and U12, with 11v11 after that. Many clubs and leagues use 3v3 for the youngest players. Your league's rules always come first, whether it is a recreational or a club league, but as far as we could find, they usually spell out formats and substitution procedures without setting a minimum for minutes. In the playing rules St. Louis Youth Soccer publishes, for example, we found no minimum playing time requirement. That leaves the decision to the coach.

Why it mattersGame time and repetitions beat winning now

A youth soccer season is short, and a player's career is long. The question worth asking is not "how do we win Saturday?" but "who will this player be in three years?" Four ideas point the same direction.

1. Development happens on the ball

You cannot improve at something you rarely do. A small study of 34 boys with an average age of 11.9 counted touches in 4v4, 7v7 and 11v11 games. Total touches per game were highest in 4v4 (about 311), then 7v7 (about 214), then 11v11 (about 170). The authors concluded that the ball is in play far more in small sided games, which means more repeated decision making, and called 4v4 and 7v7 more developmentally appropriate for players under 12. It was a small study with short games, so treat the exact numbers as a sketch. The direction is what matters.

US Youth Soccer's Player Development Model makes the same case, describing small sided games as the place where players gain quality ball skills through "increased time with the ball." A player sitting on the bench is getting none of it.

2. Kids develop on different clocks

Two players in the same age group can be years apart physically. As the Player Development Project puts it, "The player at 4th or 5th in your group at age 12 might be your best player by 15." Cut that player's minutes now and you may never find out.

Research on an elite academy in Madrid, covering 136 players aged 12 to 15, found that late maturing players received significantly less competitive playing time. The authors concluded that selection tends to favor physical development and can limit opportunities for late maturing and relatively younger players. If minutes follow the calendar and growth spurts instead of long term potential, equal playing time is the fix.

3. Kids say they want to play

The Aspen Institute's Project Play recommends equal playing time "through age 12, at least," and notes that the Norwegian, Swedish and Danish federations recommend it for children 12 and under. Its 2025 national survey of 3,827 young people ages 10 to 17 found that 57% believe all or equal playing time is fairest, and only 11% supported restricted minutes for non elite players. Among former players, "I'm not good enough" (29%) and "bad coaching" (21%) were the top reasons for quitting. A bench does nothing for confidence.

4. Other countries already do this

Belgium removed league standings for young players, moved to four quarters with mandatory substitutions, and requires a minimum of 50% playing time, according to John O'Sullivan of the Changing the Game Project describing their model. Kris Van Der Haegen of the Belgian federation adds that some clubs go further and give every player at least 75%. His reasoning is plain: "you can't develop if you don't touch the ball."

The roster test

If a player was good enough to make your roster, that player is good enough to play for you. Roster spots are decisions. Minutes should follow them.

The mathWhat equal actually looks like

Equal playing time is simple arithmetic. Divide the players on the field by the players on your roster. That share of the game is what every player gets when time is split evenly.

3v335 players60%
4v446 players67%
7v7712 players58%
9v9914 players64%
11v111118 players61%

In a 48 minute 7v7 game with 12 players, equal time is 28 minutes each. The 50% floor also tells you how large a roster can be before fairness becomes impossible: twice the number of players on the field. That is 6 for 3v3, 8 for 4v4, 14 for 7v7, 18 for 9v9 and 22 for 11v11. Carry more than that and someone will fall under half no matter how careful you are. Goalkeepers add a wrinkle, since they count toward the players on the field. Rotate the position rather than locking one player in it.

U6 to U83v3 and 4v4: equal minutes, every game

At this age there is no real "level of play" to sort by. The gap between players is mostly birthdays and how many times each child has kicked a ball. Sorting them now just teaches the kids who need reps the most that they are not wanted.

The standard: equal minutes, with injury and absence as the only exceptions.

Why it is hard: the games are short, kids do not want to come off, and you are managing a handful of small humans, a parent or two, and a clock all at once.

What works:

  • Rotate on a fixed timer, such as every four or five minutes, rather than "when it feels right." A timer takes the coach out of it, and kids understand a rule better than a mood.
  • Make coming off routine and never a punishment. Do not pull a child for a mistake.
  • With 5 players in 3v3 or 6 in 4v4, everyone plays 60% to 67% of the game. That is plenty of room for equal minutes.
  • Let every child try every spot. Nobody at this age is a defender or a striker yet.

U9 to U107v7: equal across the season, 50% in every game

This is the first time a scoreboard starts to matter to the adults on the sideline. It is also the age where the gap between the biggest kid and the youngest kid can be very visible, and where coaches feel the first pull to play "the best seven."

The standard: equal minutes across the season, and at least half of every game for every available player. An occasional game may skew, but the floor does not move.

Why it is hard: rotations are more complex. There are seven spots, including a goalkeeper, and the more players you carry the more the bench grows.

What works:

  • Keep the roster near 10 to 12. At 12 players, everyone plays about 58%, which leaves a small cushion for injuries and absences.
  • Rotate positions along with minutes. Do not lock your tallest player in goal or your fastest player at forward. Nobody knows yet what these kids will become.
  • Share goalkeeper time. Give each willing player a turn, and let kids who do not want it skip it.
  • Plan substitutions in groups at natural stoppages, so the game keeps its flow and you are not making decisions every minute.

U11 to U129v9: the last stage where results should not decide minutes

Offside arrives, tactics get real, and players start to separate physically. This is the age where a coach is most tempted to shorten the bench and where the cost of doing it is highest. Project Play's recommendation of equal playing time "through age 12, at least" applies squarely here.

The standard: the same as 7v7, with equal minutes as the goal and at least 50% in every game. Level of play does not decide minutes. Some games, such as a tournament final, will tempt you to lean on your strongest group. If you do, hold the 50% line for everyone else.

Why it is hard: the differences between players are bigger, parents are more invested, and you are also trying to teach positions and shape. It is easy to let "who plays where" become "who plays."

What works:

  • Keep the roster at 14 or fewer where you can. At 9v9 the absolute ceiling for the 50% floor is 18, but 14 leaves a healthy 64% share.
  • Rotate positions. The center back who has only ever defended has a limited view of the game and a limited future.
  • Check totals at halftime. Anyone well behind on minutes should get on the field early in the second half.
  • Remember that the player who is fourth or fifth best today may be your best in three years.

U13 and up11v11: earn your role, keep your floor

This is where the idea of earning playing time, and earning a specific position, begins to make sense. Players are older, the game is bigger, and commitment and performance can reasonably shape who starts and who finishes. We are not arguing against that.

How this plays out depends on where you coach. In recreational soccer, every player who signed up is on your team, so there is no selection to defend and the floor is easy to justify. In club soccer, players earned their roster spots at tryouts, which is exactly why the roster test matters: you chose them.

The standard: roles and starting spots can be earned, and even so, every available player gets at least 50% of every game. In a 70 minute game, that is 35 minutes. Again, if a player was good enough to be put on your roster, that player is good enough to play for you.

Why it is hard: the pressure to win is real, rosters get large, and it is easy to let a bench player disappear. Larger rosters make the floor mathematically impossible past 22 players, and much harder well before that.

Elite settings show it can be done. The Player Development Project notes that even elite academies often use a minimum of about 50% game time, and the Belgian model described above uses the same floor for young players. The Madrid academy research is a caution as well. Even at this age, playing time can track physical maturity as much as ability. A floor protects the late developer from a system that only sees size.

What works:

  • Tell players how minutes are earned, in plain language, before the season starts.
  • Use starts, positions and closing minutes to reward effort and commitment. Keep the floor for everyone.
  • Keep the roster honest. If you cannot give every player half a game, you are carrying too many players.
  • Track it. At this age, coaches who feel sure they are being fair are often surprised by the numbers.

The real problemFair on paper, hard on the sideline

Nearly every coach agrees with equal playing time. Far fewer can prove they delivered it. During a game you are watching play, coaching, handling a parent's question, and trying to remember who sat out last. Memory is the weakest tool on the sideline, and a clipboard tally falls apart the moment a substitution or an injury scrambles the plan.

A simple sideline routine

  1. Before the game, mark absent players unavailable and set your starting lineup.
  2. Pick a rotation length and set the bench alert to match.
  3. Queue subs as the alerts fire, and confirm them at the next stoppage.
  4. At halftime, glance at minutes. Anyone behind gets on early in the second half.
  5. After the game, open the summary. That is your season record for fairness.
Talking to parents

Tell parents your standard before the first game. Something like: "In our program every healthy player plays at least half of every game, and through age 12 we work toward equal minutes over the season." Parents rarely argue with a rule they heard in advance, and a minutes report backs you up when it matters. If you coach a club team, where parents often expect a competitive season after tryouts, it helps even more to say it at your first parent meeting.
