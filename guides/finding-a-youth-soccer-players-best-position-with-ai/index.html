Finding a Player's Best Position With AI

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of MatchTap · October 26, 2026 · 14 min read

Every coach hits the same question around the middle of a season: where should this player actually play? You have a feeling, but a feeling formed from a few Saturdays, a loud parent, and whoever happened to be tallest is not much to build on. An AI assistant can help you slow down, line up what you have really seen, and turn it into two or three positions worth trying and a plan to test them. It cannot see your player, and it should never be the one that decides. This guide shows how to use it for the first job and not the second, with copy and paste prompts, and with the privacy rules that come first.

Who this guide is for

Coaches of players roughly ages 9 and up who play 7v7, 9v9, or 11v11 and want a more careful way to choose where a player lines up. For ages 8 and under, there are no positions to find, and our positions guide explains why. Pair this with our growth and maturity guide, because size is the easiest thing to mistake for skill.

The short version
  • Ask AI for candidates and a trial plan, not a verdict. Official guidance and the research both say positions should stay flexible through the early teens.
  • Never type a player's full name, or a photo, birthdate, school, or club, into an AI tool. Use a jersey number, a letter code, or no label at all.
  • Feed it a fixed checklist, a few game counts, and the player's own words, so the answer comes from what you saw and not from a general impression.
  • Make it argue against itself. A second prompt that looks for mistakes is the most useful one in this guide.
  • Test each candidate for a few games, track the minutes, and decide on what happens on the field, with the player's input.
  • Keep size and skill apart. A tall player is not automatically a defender.

The ideaWhy a candidate list beats a verdict

U.S. Soccer's page for 7v7 says players "should be encouraged to try all positions at this age group," and its U9 to U10 age group guide tells coaches to encourage players to play multiple positions and roles. The U13 to U14 and U15 to U16 guides, as we noted in our positions guide, still encourage different positions. For 11v11, U.S. Soccer's positional traits page lists six jobs: goalkeepers, full backs, central backs, central mids, wide forwards, and a center forward. In other words, the official guidance treats positions as something to explore, not something to settle early.

Research gives a second reason for caution. A 2017 study in the International Journal of Sports Medicine (Towlson and colleagues) looked at 465 elite male youth players from U13 to U18. Central defenders and goalkeepers were generally taller and heavier than other players at every age group, and more advanced in maturation at U13 and U14. Position specific physical differences showed up mainly later, at U15 to U16 and more clearly at U17 to U18. The authors concluded that relative age, maturation, and body size push players into key defensive roles early, and that selectors should treat playing positions as plastic until players reach full maturity. That study is of elite academy boys, so it will not describe your team exactly, but the warning travels: the player you want to put at center back may just be the one who grew first.

There is also no proven test for this. A 2022 scoping review in the Journal of Sports Sciences (McCalman and colleagues) covered 93 studies and 226 skill assessments in youth soccer. About 68 percent were done in settings that do not resemble a match, 99 percent focused on attacking skills, and the authors say there is no consensus on the best way to measure skill. The checklist below is therefore not a validated test. It is a way to organize what you have seen so that a person, or a program, can reason about it.

Your own memory is the weak link, too. In a study of elite youth coaches that we cover in our stat tracking guide, six coaches watched ten players across eight matches, and their unaided observations were accurate only about 39 percent of the time. A structured list and a few counts beat a hunch, which is the whole case for doing this carefully.

Safety firstBefore you type anything: protect the player

The prompts in this guide work with no real name at all, so there is no reason to type one. Leave out a full name, a photo or video, a birthdate, an address, a school, a medical detail, a diagnosis, anything a parent told you privately, and the name of the team, club, league, or tournament. A name is not the only way to identify a child. An age group plus a club plus a jersey number can point to one person, so the safest profile has none of the three together.

The rule

Describe the player, never identify the player. If you would not want the text to appear in a parent group chat, do not put it in an AI chat. This is the same standard we use in every guide on this site: jersey numbers or initials, or nothing identifying at all.

Habits that go with it

  • Use age groups, "U11" or "an 11 year old," never a birthdate.
  • One player per chat. Start a new conversation for the next player instead of comparing children side by side by name.
  • Strip exported files. A MatchTap CSV has a player column, and a SubGo CSV includes player names. Delete that column or swap in your codes before pasting anything.
  • Never upload photos, video, or screenshots that show faces, names, or numbers with a team.
  • Keep sensitive context out. Behavior notes, a medical condition, or a parent's private comment do not belong in a position prompt. Our ADHD guide follows the same rule.
  • Check your data settings. Anthropic says it uses consumer chats for model training only if you allow it, and that incognito chats are never used. OpenAI says turning off "Improve the model for everyone" stops training use of your chats, and that Temporary Chats are removed after 30 days and not used for training. Policies change, so read the current page for the tool you use.
  • Tell families in one line in your preseason message that you use AI tools for planning without names. Follow any club or league policy on tools and player information.

Even a coded profile is something to treat with care. It describes a child, and it lives in a chat history. Delete the conversation when you are done, and do not paste AI output that contains a real name into a team app.

Step oneGather three kinds of input

1. Your observations, in a fixed format

Rate the player on each item as S (strong), D (developing), or N (not seen yet). The "not seen yet" option matters, because the honest answer for many items is that you have not watched closely. These items come from the jobs in our positions guide and from general coaching practice. They are my checklist, not a validated instrument.

ItemWhat strong looks like
First touchKeeps the ball close, even with a defender near.
PassingFinds teammates, short and, when ready, longer.
1v1 with the ballBeats a defender or protects the ball.
FinishingShoots early, on target, when open.
1v1 defendingStays on feet, delays, wins it back.
PositioningStays between ball and goal, or finds space when attacking.
AwarenessLooks around before the ball arrives.
Work rateSprints back after losing the ball.
CommunicationTalks to teammates and helps organize.
ComposureStays calm after a mistake.
Speed and staminaKeeps pace late in the game. Score it separately from skill.

Goalkeeping is deliberately left off. Ask for volunteers and never push a child into goal, as our goalkeeper guide explains. Add a line or two of notes about what you saw in games, not only practice, because a player can look different when the game is fast.

2. A few game counts

Counts can add a reality check, but only if you read them carefully. In MatchTap's Player or Team individual modes, every tap is tagged to a player, and each finished game exports as a CSV with the timestamp, game clock, half, player, event, and note. Three details shape how you should use it. MatchTap does not add up season totals, so you build the running table yourself. Its CSV has no position column, so write the job and the minutes beside each game (SubGo's Summary screen shows positions and minutes if you use it). And counts reflect the job as much as the player: a defender will have more tackles than a forward, and that says nothing about talent. Use at least three to five games, which is my rule of thumb, and remember that opponents and weather vary.

3. The player's own words

Ask the player which jobs they enjoy, where they feel useful, and what they would like to try, in a relaxed moment and not at halftime. A 2021 study in the International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health (Sevil-Serrano and colleagues) surveyed 203 youth soccer players aged 10 to 19 in Spain. Coach autonomy support was linked to players feeling more competent, more autonomous, and more connected to teammates, which in turn was linked to stronger commitment to the sport, and intimidation was the controlling behavior most tied to frustrated needs. The design was a single survey, so it shows association and not cause, but it fits the common sense approach: a player who has a say is more likely to want to stay. Parents can add something too. Ask what their child says about the game at home, not where the parent wants them to play.

Step twoThe prompts

Use a fresh chat for each player, and swap each bracket for your own details. Keep the label you chose above in place of any name.

Prompt 1: Get candidates, not a verdict

"I coach a [age group] team that plays [format]. I want two or three positions worth trying for one player, not a final answer. Call the player [your code, such as Player 7]. My observations, where S is strong, D is developing and N is not seen yet: [paste your list, such as first touch S, passing S, 1v1 defending D]. Notes from games: [one or two sentences]. The player said: [their own words]. Using only what I gave you, suggest two or three positions with reasons tied to specific items above. For each one, tell me what evidence is missing and what I could watch for to check it. Do not guess anything about the player's size, maturity, or personality that I did not tell you." Paste your own list in place of the brackets

Prompt 2: Add the counts

"Here are [Player 7]'s counts from [number] games, with the job played and the minutes for each game. [Paste a simple table with no name column.] Tell me what these counts can and cannot show. Compare per game and per 10 minutes, point out which counts depend on the job played, and tell me whether the sample is big enough to say anything. Do not treat a count as a skill rating." Paste after Prompt 1, in the same chat

Prompt 3: Make it argue against itself

"Here is the recommendation you gave: [paste it]. Argue against it. List three reasons it could be wrong, including the chance that I am mistaking size, speed, or age within the age group for skill, that the player has only played one job, or that I am seeing what I expect to see. Then list three questions I should answer before I change anything." The most useful of the five, because assistants often go along with the way you frame a question

Prompt 4: Plan the trial

"Design a trial for [Player 7] at [candidate position] over [three] games. Give the minutes per game and which half, two things to watch for, one short cue I can say from the sideline, and a rule for deciding whether to keep, change, or stop, written now, before the games. Keep minutes fair for the whole team." Write the decision rule down before the first game

Prompt 5: Ask the player without a test feel

"Write four short questions I can ask a [age] year old about which jobs on the field they enjoy and feel useful in, and what they would like to try. No leading questions, no right answers, and nothing about being good or bad at something. Add one sentence I can say first to make clear this is not a test." Use it before Prompt 1 so their words go in
What a useful answer looks like

This is an example of the shape of a good reply, written by me about an invented player, not a recording. For "Player 7," a U11 9v9 player marked strong at passing, first touch and awareness and developing at 1v1 defending and positioning, a useful reply names central midfield first, because passing and looking around fit the job, and says the missing evidence is how the player handles pressure. It names an outside back second, noting that work rate fits but 1v1 defending is still developing, so it is worth a trial and not a verdict. It then lists what to watch (does the player look before receiving, and does she recover quickly after losing the ball) and asks the coach to confirm the player is not simply the oldest or biggest in the group. A weak reply says "Player 7 is a midfielder" and stops.

Step threeTrial it, then decide with your own eyes

A candidate is a hypothesis. Put the player there for a half, or a few practices, across at least three games, which is my suggestion and not a rule. Track minutes by position so you know the trial actually happened, because it is easy to remember it as longer than it was. Watch for the two things from Prompt 4, ask the player how it felt, and compare notes against your original checklist. Write the decision rule down first, such as "keep this up if she looks before receiving in most touches and still enjoys it," so the result is judged against a standard you set in advance.

Age changes how you use all of this. For ages 8 and under, skip it entirely. For U9 and U10, official guidance says to try all positions, so use AI to plan a fair rotation, not to find a single best fit. For U11 and U12, use candidates and trials, but keep every player rotating. For U13 and up, give the candidates more weight, and still revisit them each season because bodies and skills keep changing. These bands are my suggestions, built from the official guidance above.

If a player loves one position, you do not have to say no. Let them play it for part of the game, as our positions guide suggests, and use the rest of the time to try another candidate.

Honest limitsWhere AI goes wrong

  • It has not seen your player. It can only reorganize what you wrote, so vague input produces confident sounding nonsense.
  • It often goes along with your framing. That is why Prompt 3 exists. If you say you think she is a striker, expect to hear reasons she is a striker.
  • It can invent facts. Delete any line that says "research shows" without a source, and check anything about league rules yourself.
  • It cannot separate size from skill unless you do. The research above says physical size and maturity steer players into roles early.
  • Counts depend on the job. Without the position and minutes, a number means little.
  • A small sample misleads. Our player development tracking guide explains why one game tells you almost nothing.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Typing a player's full name, photo, birthdate, or school into an AI tool. Use a code or nothing at all.
  • Treating the answer as a decision. It is a list of ideas to test.
  • Locking a young player into one spot after one good or bad stretch, when official guidance expects rotation.
  • Picking the biggest kid for defense and the fastest for forward, without checking whether size is the only reason.
  • Pasting an unedited CSV, with the player column still in it.
  • Skipping the player's voice, or letting a parent's preference decide.
  • Never rechecking. A position that fit in September may not in May.
