Why Youth Soccer Practice Should Feel Like a Game, Not a Drill

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 27, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 13 min read

Young players do not show up wanting to learn something. They show up wanting to play. That difference sounds small, but it changes how practice should be built: activities need to feel fun and engaging, with a purpose the player actually feels in the moment, not a drill they are told is good for them. That same idea is also the reason to get rid of standing in line and put a ball at the feet of every player instead of one line waiting a turn.

The coaching philosophy behind this guide

Fun is not the reward for finishing the serious part of practice. Fun, with a real purpose the player can feel, is how the serious part of practice actually works for a young player. This guide checks that belief honestly against the research, including the parts that turned out to be the coach's own judgment rather than a proven finding.

The short version
  • Give every repetition a stake the player can feel right now, a point, a race, or a score to beat, instead of "this will help you later."
  • "No lines, no laps, no lectures" is popular shorthand, not an official rule, and nobody owns it.
  • Current U.S. Soccer guidance says to maximize time on the ball and keep stoppages minimal.
  • The research is strong on motivation and engagement, and less settled on whether games always teach skills faster.
  • Keep the stakes kind. Compete against your own score, not against the weakest player on the team.

The ideaMake the purpose something the player can feel, not just be told

Telling a seven year old "we are working on first touch today" means almost nothing to them. Telling them "score a point every time you stop your partner's pass and keep the ball under your feet" gives the exact same repetition a purpose they can actually feel while they are doing it. The skill being trained does not change. What changes is whether the player experiences the activity as something with real stakes right now, a game to win, or as a task being done to them for reasons an adult has in mind. Younger players especially do not yet have the patience or the abstract reasoning to buy into "this will help you later." They need the payoff to be now, inside the activity itself.

This is also the reasoning behind two practical habits this site has recommended before: small sided games that maximize touches on the ball for every player, and a practice structure that keeps explanations short so kids spend their time playing instead of listening. Both ideas point at the same target: less standing around being told something, more doing something that feels like it matters.

Where this comes from"No lines, no laps, no lectures"

The phrase is widely used in youth coaching, and nobody seems to own it. Skye Eddy of Soccer Parenting uses it as her own standard advice for recreational coaches. A Mojo coaching article makes the same point with "the three Ls," lines, laps, and lectures, which it credits to Vince Ganzberg of United Soccer Coaches. We could not trace the phrase to a single origin, so we treat it as popular coaching shorthand, not as language from any governing body's curriculum.

What it points at is real and specific. A line is a player standing still, waiting for a turn, getting almost no repetitions in the time available. A lap is conditioning disguised as training, with no ball and no decision making involved. A lecture is a coach talking while ten kids' attention drains out of them one sentence at a time. None of the three gets a young player a game they can feel any purpose in.

What official guidance saysMaximum time with the ball, minimal standing around

This part is not just coaching folklore. U.S. Soccer's current coaching guidance for youth says to "use games that maximize time on the ball and decision-making," and lists as a game based principle to "try to use minimal interventions and stoppages." It also says to develop decision making through age appropriate small sided games and to help players make the link between training and the game.

US Youth Soccer makes a related point in its guide to small sided games, which lists among its reasons that "SSGs offer more touches on the ball" and that "every child has to participate in all facets of the game." A one ball line gives each player few touches while they wait, and a small sided game or a ball for every pair gives far more. We could not find published touch counts to put a number on the difference, so we do not give one.

What the research saysGames with a purpose beat isolated repetition, for engagement

There is a real body of sport pedagogy research behind games based coaching, often called Teaching Games for Understanding (TGfU) or the constraints led approach. A 2023 theory paper in Frontiers in Sports and Active Living by Ribas and colleagues states that, compared with the traditional skill drill model, TGfU "achieves higher motivation, strategic knowledge transfer and student's connection to the activity." That paper presents no new data. It cites earlier studies, mostly from physical education, including one comparing TGfU with direct instruction in U12 football that measured physical activity levels, not motivation.

A 2021 review in Frontiers in Psychology by Karl Newell and Inez Rovegno, "Teaching Children's Motor Skills for Team Games Through Guided Discovery: How Constraints Enhance Learning," contrasts the older model, where "the student is portrayed as acquiring behaviors or information received from the teacher or coach as provider," with a guided discovery approach it describes as "preserving the child's motivation and enjoyment."

Honest limit: this research is strongest on motivation and engagement. It is more mixed and context dependent on whether games based training always produces faster skill acquisition than isolated drilling. Treat "kids stay more engaged" as well supported, and "kids always learn the skill faster this way" as a less settled, ongoing question.

Why "perceived purpose" is the right wordAutonomy, competence, and connection to others

The psychological case for "perceived purpose" has a name: Self-Determination Theory, from psychologists Richard Ryan and Edward Deci (American Psychologist, 2000). It identifies three basic needs that drive genuine, internal motivation and not just compliance: competence, relatedness, and autonomy. A game with a clear objective, a way to succeed at it, and other kids playing alongside touches all three at once. A line waiting for a turn touches none of them. That is our application of the theory to practice design, not a finding from a youth soccer study.

It is even in the nameThe "FUNdamentals" stage

The Canadian Sport for Life Long-Term Athlete Development model names its foundational stage "FUNdamentals," for boys 6 to 9 and girls 6 to 8. Its objective is to "learn all fundamental movement skills and build overall movement skills," and it says skill development at this stage "should be well structured, positive and FUN." That is a deliberate statement that fun and skill development are the same project at that age, not two things a coach has to trade off against each other.

The stakesFun is not a nice to have, it is why kids stay

A Project Play article, citing Hedstrom and Gould (2004), says: "Kids report that they play sports to have fun, do something they are good at, be with their friends, improve their athletic skills, and feel the excitement of competition." The same article lists a lack of fun and parental pressure to perform among the top reasons kids stop, citing a 2013 study by Sánchez-Miguel and colleagues on parents' behavior and children's enjoyment. We could not confirm that the study ranks those reasons, so treat that line as suggestive.

Dr. Amanda Visek at George Washington University mapped what makes sport fun in far more detail, with funding from the National Institute of Nursing Research at the NIH. Working with 142 youth soccer players plus coaches and parents, she identified 81 fun determinants grouped into 11 fun factors, and US Youth Soccer's coaching materials reproduce that work. Being a good sport, trying hard, and positive coaching ranked highest and together formed what the researchers called the youth sport ethos. In the original study winning ranked well down the list of 81 determinants, and a later George Washington University analysis placed it 40th. Our team culture guide goes deeper on this research.

Myth check: you may have heard that "70 percent of kids quit organized sports by age 13." Researchers quoted in a Tucson.com column could not find an original study for it, and CDC youth survey data cited there show organized sports participation among U.S. high school students has stayed above 50 percent since 2000, apart from the COVID years. A checkable number is the Monitoring the Future analysis from D'Youville University, using survey data collected between 2006 and 2011, which found an average 32 percent drop in participation in a given sport from 8th to 12th grade, with soccer at 31 percent for boys and 53 percent for girls. Those compare groups of students, not individual kids followed over time. Our burnout guide covers this in more detail.

Being straight about itThe one part of this idea that is coaching judgment, not a study

Everything above supports the broad idea strongly: games with a felt purpose beat isolated drilling and standing in line, for motivation, for touches on the ball, and for keeping kids in the sport. One specific piece of the philosophy, that a coach should avoid even calling something a "drill" out loud and should instead always frame it verbally as a game, is not something the research separately tested. Nobody has run a study isolating the effect of the word "drill" versus the word "game" while holding the actual activity identical. That piece is this site's own reasoned extrapolation from the broader games based learning research above, not a cited finding of its own. It is a sensible extrapolation, but it deserves to be labeled honestly as one.

In practiceWhat this actually looks like at training

At every format, the same three swaps apply. Replace a single ball line drill with a small sided game or a paired activity where every player has a ball, so the youngest players in their very first season are moving and touching the ball constantly instead of waiting. Replace a long verbal explanation with a short demonstration and a game that starts within thirty seconds, since a young attention span spends itself during the explanation, not during the activity. Replace a plain repetition count with a stake the player can feel. None of this changes what skill is being trained. It changes whether the player experiences training the same way they experience the parts of soccer they actually chose to show up for.

Before and after: one drill, one game

Before: six players in a line, one ball, each dribbles to a cone and back while five wait. After: everyone has a ball in a grid. For 30 seconds, players change direction as often as they can while keeping the ball close, and score a point each time they turn away from a cone. Rest, then try to beat the score. The skill is the same dribbling and turning, with far less waiting and a goal the player can feel.

Stakes that work

  • Beat your own score: count clean turns in 60 seconds, rest, then try to beat the number.
  • Gates: dribble through any gate in the grid, and count how many you get in a minute.
  • Partner game: one partner passes, the other controls it with one touch and keeps it under their feet. Score a point each time the ball stays inside a cone box, then swap.
  • Team challenge: a small team completes as many passes as it can against the clock.

Keep the stakes kind

A point system can backfire if the weakest player loses every round. Compete against your own previous score or as a team against the clock, rotate partners, and avoid public rankings of individuals. Keep the stakes about effort and improvement, not about beating a teammate. Our burnout guide covers research linking effort and improvement focused climates with lower burnout in a small survey study. The advice on partner rotation is our coaching judgment, not a research finding.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • One ball, one line, ten kids waiting, which is the single biggest killer of touches on the ball in youth practice.
  • A long explanation before a short activity, burning the attention span before the game even starts.
  • Calling everything "fun" without a real objective, which is just chaos with a positive label. The purpose still has to be real and understandable to the player.
  • Making the stake about beating a teammate, so the weakest player loses every round.
  • Overcorrecting into constant games with zero technical focus, when the games based approach works best paired with a clear skill objective for that day, not as a replacement for having one.
  • Assuming older, more competitive players have outgrown this, when a felt purpose and real stakes keep mattering well past the FUNdamentals age range, just in a more tactical form.

Ask your AI assistant coachTurn one drill into a game with a real stake

The idea in this guide is simple to state and harder to do on the spot at practice, turning a plain repetition into something with a purpose the player can feel. An AI assistant is useful here because it can offer three or four game versions of the same drill in seconds, so you can pick the one that fits your group instead of falling back on a line and a lecture. Two cautions. It tends to make rules too complicated for young players, and it can invent studies, so check any research claim it gives you. If you are new to this, start with our beginner guide to using Claude as a soccer coach.

"I want to work on [specific skill, such as receiving on the half turn or shielding the ball] with [age group] players. Give me three ways to turn that repetition into a small sided game with a real stake, a point, a race, or a small competition, no lines and no laps, that a player would actually feel while doing it. Keep the rules simple enough to explain in 30 seconds." Prompt 1: turn a drill into a game, while planning
"Here is my practice plan for [age group] players: [paste it]. Flag any activity where a player would wait more than about ten seconds, any activity with no clear objective the player can feel, and any rules too complicated for this age." Prompt 2: check the plan for lines and lectures
"Rewrite these point systems so every player competes against their own previous score or as part of a team against the clock, and nobody is ranked in front of the others: [paste]." Prompt 3: keep the stakes kind

Keep the actual skill identical across every version it suggests. What should change is only the stake and the framing, not the underlying repetition you are trying to build. Keep players anonymous in anything you paste.
