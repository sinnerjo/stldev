Why Youth Soccer Practice Should Feel Like a Game, Not a Drill

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · October 13, 2026 · 9 min read

Young players do not show up wanting to learn something. They show up wanting to play. That difference sounds small, but it changes how practice should be built: activities need to feel fun and engaging, with a purpose the player actually feels in the moment, not a drill they are told is good for them. That same idea is also the reason to get rid of standing in line and put a ball at the feet of every player instead of one line waiting a turn.

The coaching philosophy behind this guide

Fun is not the reward for finishing the serious part of practice. Fun, with a real purpose the player can feel, is how the serious part of practice actually works for a young player. This guide checks that belief honestly against the research, including the parts that turned out to be the coach's own judgment rather than a proven finding.

The ideaMake the purpose something the player can feel, not just be told

Telling a seven year old "we are working on first touch today" means almost nothing to them. Telling them "score a point every time you control the ball before your partner touches it" gives the exact same repetition a purpose they can actually feel while they are doing it. The skill being trained does not change. What changes is whether the player experiences the activity as something with real stakes right now, a game to win, or as a task being done to them for reasons an adult has in mind. Younger players especially do not yet have the patience or the abstract reasoning to buy into "this will help you later." They need the payoff to be now, inside the activity itself.

This is also the reasoning behind two practical habits this site has recommended before: small sided games that maximize touches on the ball for every player, and a practice structure that keeps explanations short so kids spend their time playing instead of listening. Both ideas point at the same target: less standing around being told something, more doing something that feels like it matters.

Where this comes from"No lines, no laps, no lectures"

That exact phrase is closely associated with Dr. Ron Quinn, a longtime youth soccer coach educator who won both the NSCAA Youth Long-Term Achievement Award and US Youth Soccer's Dr. Thomas Fleck Excellence in Youth Coaching Education Award. As one coaching resource puts it, quoting him directly: "As Dr. Ron Quinn says 'no lines, no laps, no lectures.'" The same phrase shows up as practical advice in state youth soccer association coaching clinics as well.

Fair framing: this is a widely repeated, well credentialed piece of coaching wisdom, not an official phrase written into the US Soccer or US Youth Soccer curriculum documents themselves. Treat it as popularized by a respected coach educator, not as governing body doctrine.

What it points at is real and specific. A line is a player standing still, waiting for a turn, getting almost no repetitions in the time available. A lap is conditioning disguised as training, with no ball and no decision making involved. A lecture is a coach talking while ten kids' attention drains out of them one sentence at a time. None of the three gets a young player a game they can feel any purpose in.

What official curriculum actually saysMaximum time with the ball, minimal standing around

This part is not just coaching folklore. It is written directly into the official U.S. Soccer Curriculum: "All practices must be done with the ball whenever possible," and "Players must spend the maximum time possible in contact with the ball and experiment by themselves." The same document tells coaches not to manage every single moment: "Do not stop the session too often. Players must learn to find solutions without constant coaching," and recommends small sided games specifically to develop attacking and defending principles rather than isolated, line based repetition.

US Youth Soccer's own player development materials make a related, practical point: smaller sided formats give every player dramatically more contact with the ball in the same amount of time than a full sided game or a single ball drill ever could. A one ball line drill might give a player a handful of touches in ten minutes. A small sided game with a ball for every pair, or a game built around constant possession, can give that same player hundreds.

What the research saysGames with a purpose beat isolated repetition, for engagement

There is a real, active body of sport pedagogy research behind games based coaching, often called Teaching Games for Understanding or the constraints led approach. A 2023 review in Frontiers in Sports and Active Living puts the comparison plainly: "Compared to the traditional skill drill technical model, TGfU achieves higher motivation, strategic knowledge transfer and student's connection to the activity." Separate 2021 research on teaching children's motor skills through guided discovery contrasts the older method, where "the student is portrayed as acquiring behaviors... received from the teacher or coach as provider," with a games based, discovery approach that "preserves the child's motivation and enjoyment" instead.

Honest limit: this research is strongest on motivation and engagement. It is more mixed and context dependent on whether games based training always produces faster skill acquisition than isolated drilling. Treat "kids stay more engaged" as well supported, and "kids always learn the skill faster this way" as a less settled, ongoing question.

Why "perceived purpose" is the right wordAutonomy, competence, and connection to others

The psychological case for "perceived purpose" mattering has a real name: Self-Determination Theory, from psychologists Richard Ryan and Edward Deci. Their foundational research identifies three needs that drive genuine, internal motivation, not just compliance: competence, relatedness, and autonomy, writing that "people must not only experience competence or efficacy, they must also experience their behavior as self-determined for intrinsic motivation to be in evidence." A game with a clear objective, a way to succeed at it, and other kids playing alongside them touches all three needs at once. A line waiting for a turn touches none of them. This theory has its own dedicated research applying it specifically to coaching style in youth sport, not just to psychology in general.

It is even in the nameThe "FUNdamentals" stage

The Long-Term Athlete Development model, the framework behind how Canada and many national sport bodies structure youth athletic development, names its foundational stage for roughly ages six through nine "FUNdamentals," with the explicit objective to "learn all fundamental movement skills and build overall movement skills," structured to be "well structured, positive and FUN." That is not a coincidence or a marketing choice. It is a deliberate statement that fun and skill development are the same project at that age, not two separate things a coach has to trade off against each other.

The stakesFun is not a nice to have, it is why kids stay

Research from Michigan State University's Institute for the Study of Youth Sports, cited directly by the Aspen Institute's Project Play, found that "kids report that they play sports to have fun, do something they are good at, be with their friends, improve their athletic skills, and feel the excitement of competition," and separately that "top reasons kids stop playing sports include a lack of fun and parental pressure to perform." A dedicated research program at George Washington University, led by Dr. Amanda Visek and funded by the National Institutes of Health, mapped this out in far more detail, identifying 81 distinct fun determinants across 11 fun factors in youth sport, and that research is cited by US Youth Soccer's own coaching materials, which put the share of kids dropping out of organized sport by adolescence at 43 percent.

Myth check: you may have heard that "70 percent of kids quit organized sports by age 13." That specific number does not hold up. A review of the claim traced it through a chain of secondary sources with no single, verifiable study behind it, and real data, including the CDC's own youth survey, shows organized sports participation among U.S. high schoolers has stayed above 50 percent for years. Use the 43 percent figure above instead if you want a number with a real, checkable source behind it.

Being straight about itThe one part of this idea that is coaching judgment, not a study

Everything above supports the broad idea strongly: games with a felt purpose beat isolated drilling and standing in line, for motivation, for touches on the ball, and for keeping kids in the sport. One specific piece of the philosophy, that a coach should avoid even calling something a "drill" out loud and should instead always frame it verbally as a game, is not something the research separately tested. Nobody has run a study isolating the effect of the word "drill" versus the word "game" while holding the actual activity identical. That piece is this site's own reasoned extrapolation from the broader games based learning research above, not a cited finding of its own. It is a sensible extrapolation, but it deserves to be labeled honestly as one.

In practiceWhat this actually looks like at training

At every format, the same three swaps apply. Replace a single ball line drill with a small sided game or a paired activity where every player has a ball, so the youngest players in their very first season are moving and touching the ball constantly instead of waiting. Replace a long verbal explanation with a short demonstration and a game that starts within thirty seconds, since a young attention span spends itself during the explanation, not during the activity. Replace a plain repetition count with a stake the player can feel: a point for every clean turn away from a defender, a small team competing against another small team for total completed passes, a challenge to beat your own number against the clock. None of this changes what skill is being trained. It changes whether the player experiences training the same way they experience the parts of soccer they actually chose to show up for.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • One ball, one line, ten kids waiting, which is the single biggest killer of touches on the ball in youth practice.
  • A long explanation before a short activity, burning the attention span before the game even starts.
  • Calling everything "fun" without a real objective, which is just chaos with a positive label. The purpose still has to be real and understandable to the player.
  • Overcorrecting into constant games with zero technical focus, when the games based approach works best paired with a clear skill objective for that day, not as a replacement for having one.
  • Assuming older, more competitive players have outgrown this, when a felt purpose and real stakes keep mattering well past the FUNdamentals age range, just in a more tactical form.
