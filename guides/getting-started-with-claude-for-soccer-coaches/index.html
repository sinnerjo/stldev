How to Get Started With Claude: From Zero to Your First Coaching Task

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · October 18, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 13 min read

If you have never used AI before and are not sure where to click first, start here. This guide takes you from creating an account to a real practice plan you have tested, checked, and improved yourself, in about an hour spread over a week. The goal is not to learn buttons. It is to learn how to work with an assistant that is fast, tireless, and eager to please.

Who this guide is for

A volunteer coach, club coach, or team manager who keeps hearing that AI can save time on practice plans and parent emails but has never tried it. Every example uses Claude, the assistant made by Anthropic, though the ideas carry over to ChatGPT and others. When you finish, our guide to training AI on your coaching philosophy and our detailed guide to using AI as a coach are the next two stops.

The short version
  • Go to claude.ai and create a free account. You do not need to pay anything to start.
  • Brief it like a new assistant coach: real details, a clear job, and a stated format for the answer.
  • Treat every answer as a draft from a stranger. It can be confidently wrong, and it tends to agree with you, so ask it to find the problems.
  • Keep children's identities out of the chat. Use jersey numbers or initials, or nothing identifying at all. Never put full names, photos, addresses, birthdates, or medical notes into a general AI chat.
  • Judge a plan as a coach, not as a reader. The test is what your players will be doing, not how good the page sounds.

The basicsWhat Claude is, and the two flaws to plan around

Claude is an AI assistant. You type a message, it writes back, and you keep the conversation going as long as you like. You can ask it to draft an email, explain a rule, build a practice plan, shorten something, or turn a messy list into a clean one. It is not a search engine, and it does not know your team. It knows only what you tell it, plus what it learned in training.

That comes with two flaws worth knowing on day one. First, it can be fluently wrong: a guess sounds exactly like a fact. Second, and subtler, assistants lean toward agreeing with you. Researchers at Anthropic and elsewhere found that five leading assistants consistently leaned toward agreeable answers, and that people sometimes rate a convincing, agreeable answer above a correct one. So the habits here come down to two moves: give it real details so it has less to guess, and invite it to disagree so it does not just flatter your plan.

Step oneSet up and look around, in ten minutes

Open a browser, go to claude.ai, and follow the prompts to sign up. Anthropic's own getting started tutorial treats claude.ai/new as the starting point. The free plan includes chat on web, mobile, and desktop, and paid plans add higher usage limits. Start free. Plans and prices change, so check Anthropic's pricing page for current numbers. One rule to know first: Anthropic's consumer terms say "you must be at least 18 years old... to use the Services." This is a tool for you, the coach, not a project to hand a player.

The screen is simpler than it looks: one big box for typing, your conversation above it, and a new chat for a fresh page. A plus button opens extras such as attaching a file or switching web search on and off, which Anthropic's tutorial describes as on by default for Free, Pro, and Max plans. Menus change, so trust your own screen over any guide.

Step twoYour first conversation, with no soccer in it

Start by learning how it feels to talk to it, not by asking for something that matters. Anthropic's tutorial says to write "in plain language" and not worry about perfect wording, because you can say when an answer misses. If you do not know what to ask, let it interview you. Paste this into a new chat:

"I am brand new to using AI and I am not sure what to ask. Ask me a few questions, one at a time, about what I am trying to get done this week, then suggest three things I could try with you." Paste this into your very first chat if you do not know where to start

Answer honestly and let it lead. A good prompt, which is just the message you type, often comes out of a short back and forth.

How you will know it worked: you have one reply you would actually use, whether that is an email, a list, or a plan. If you do not, do not decide AI is useless. Do the next experiment, because the problem is almost always the request.

Step threeFeel the difference between a vague and a specific request

In a fresh chat, first send something vague about a real soccer problem, like "How do I teach my players to pass?" Read what comes back. Now start another new chat and send the same question with a real observation from your last game:

"I coach a U10 team. In our last game, my players kicked the ball away under pressure instead of passing to a teammate, and most of our passes went straight to the other team. Give me three ways to work on this at practice, and tell me what you would watch for to know it is improving." Swap in what you actually saw at your last game; the more specific, the better

Here is the kind of difference to expect. These excerpts are illustrative examples written to show the shape of each answer, so your wording will differ.

Example answer to the vague request

"Here are some tips for teaching passing. Start with the basics, use fun drills, encourage communication, and be patient. Practice makes perfect, so repeat the skills often."

Example answer to the specific request

"Three ways to work on this: (1) a 4v1 keep away in a 10 by 10 grid, where the player in the middle starts with only light pressure, so passing feels safe; (2) passing gates, where pairs earn a point for each pass through a gate to a teammate; (3) a 4v4 game where a team scores a point for five passes in a row. Watch for: does the player look up before the ball arrives, and does the pass go to a teammate's feet or just away from the defender?"

The first answer could be about any team. The second is about yours: it fits your age group, names your problem, and tells you what to look for. That gap is the whole skill. Three habits produce it every time:

  • Give real details and a real observation. Age group, numbers, space, and what actually happened last time.
  • Say what you want back. The length, the tone, and the format: under 100 words, warm but direct, a short list, a table.
  • Follow up instead of starting over. Say "shorter," "try again for half a field," or "I only have eight cones." Within a conversation, Claude sees everything above, so each follow up builds on the last. Attach a source, like a schedule or a rules document, when there is one.

Step fourTwo settings worth a look

The first is privacy. Open your account settings and find Privacy. As of this writing there is a toggle labeled "Help improve Claude." Anthropic says that when its model training setting is on, it can train new models on your new and resumed chats and may keep them for up to five years instead of 30 days. Switching it off stops that for new chats and for older ones you open afterward. Whether to leave it on is your decision, but leaving it on may lengthen how long your chats are kept.

The second is memory. Claude can save useful context from your chats, such as your role and communication preferences, and carry it into future conversations. Anthropic's help center says memory is on by default for Free, Pro, and Max plans, and that you can view and edit what it saved. This is optional on day one, and our guide to training AI on your coaching philosophy shows how to use it on purpose.

Whichever way you set the privacy toggle, one rule does not depend on it. Use jersey numbers or initials, or nothing identifying at all. Never put full names, photos, addresses, birthdates, or medical notes into a general AI chat, and never paste parents' phone numbers or emails. Describe situations in general terms. Our detailed AI guide explains where this rule comes from.

The one habitCheck it, and make it argue with itself

Anthropic says so plainly about its own product: "even the most advanced language models, like Claude, can sometimes generate text that is factually incorrect or inconsistent with the given context." So a rule, a statistic, or a safety guideline should never reach a parent just because the answer sounded sure. Draft with Claude. Verify before you repeat.

Be careful with the obvious fix. If you ask "are you sure?", a people pleasing assistant may just say yes. Ask for work you can audit instead. Anthropic's guidance on reducing made up answers recommends giving the model explicit permission to say it does not know and making it back each claim with a source or quote. Turn that into a prompt:

"List every factual claim in that answer as a numbered list. For each one, say whether you are certain, fairly sure, or guessing, and name a source I can open to check it. If you do not know, say 'I don't know' instead of filling the gap." Paste this after any answer you plan to repeat to a parent, a player, or a team page
Example: what the answer looks like

"1. U10 teams usually play 7v7: fairly sure. Check your league's rules. 2. Players this age need about 60 minutes of practice: guessing, because it varies by club. 3. A 4v1 grid works best at about 10 by 10 yards: guessing, this is a common starting point, not a rule."

Anything marked "guessing" is where you slow down. Then open the sources yourself. Claude can be wrong about a source too, and checking takes a minute. Our detailed guide has a longer version of this routine.

Step fiveYour first real coaching task: a practice plan, judged like a coach

A practice plan is the best first task: useful the same night, and low risk because you read it before running it. U.S. Soccer's play practice play model, which opens and closes a session with game like play, is a good structure to ask for by name. Fill in the brackets with your real team and use the observation from your last game:

"I coach [age group] [format, such as 7v7] soccer with [number] players and [number] minutes of practice time on [describe your space]. In our last game I saw [one specific thing, such as players kicking the ball away under pressure]. Build one practice plan in a play practice play shape that works on that one thing. For each activity give me: the objective in one sentence, how it is set up, what the players are doing the whole time, two questions I can ask players instead of telling them, and one way to make it easier and one way to make it harder. Keep every player active, with no long lines waiting for a turn and no elimination games. List the equipment I need." Fill in the brackets, keep the one objective, then read the plan before you use it

Here is what the first part looks like filled in by a real coach, followed by the rest of the request exactly as written above:

"I coach U10 7v7 soccer with 11 players and 60 minutes of practice time on half of a grass field with 12 cones and 11 balls. In our last game I saw players kicking the ball away under pressure instead of passing. Build one practice plan in a play practice play shape that works on that one thing. ..." A filled in example; the dots stand for the rest of the request above

One activity in the plan you get back might look like this, again as an illustration:

Example: one activity from the plan

Passing gates, 12 minutes. Objective: look up and pass to a teammate before pressure arrives. Setup: six gates two yards wide in a 20 by 20 yard area, one ball per pair. Players: move constantly, passing through any gate to their partner and then finding a new gate. Ask players: "Where was your teammate before you got the ball?" and "What did you notice just before you passed?" Easier: no defender. Harder: add one defender, or limit players to two touches.

Now read the plan the way a coach reads it, not the way a reader does. A plan can sound polished and still run badly. Ask yourself:

  • Is every player moving and touching a ball most of the time, or do some wait in a line? Waiting is where attention and learning disappear.
  • Does it fit your real numbers and space? A drill for twelve on a full field changes completely with eight players on half of one.
  • Does it work on one clear thing, the thing you saw last game, or five at once?
  • Could you explain each activity in thirty seconds to a nine year old standing in the cold?
  • Would you run it tomorrow as written? If not, name what you would change.

Then make Claude work against its own plan, since an eager to please assistant will not volunteer the weak spots:

"Act as a skeptical, experienced youth coach reviewing this plan. Where will it break down with real nine year olds? Which activity has the most standing around, and what would you change first?" Paste this after you have made your own list; compare what it finds with what you found
Example: what the skeptic might say

"The 4v1 keep away leaves three players waiting while one defender chases, so some of your kids will stand around and get bored. With 11 players, run two small groups at once, and rotate the defender every minute so nobody stays in the middle."

If you are newer to coaching and cannot yet judge a plan on your own, ask one more question: "Explain why each activity works for this age group, and what I should see if it is going well." Then compare it with what you read in our practice planning guide. You know your field, your players, and which kid melts down when a drill involves a line. Claude does not, so the plan you run should be the edited one. Our practice planning guide lays out a strong session structure.

Next stepsYour first week

Skills like this stick through short repeated practice, not one long session:

  • Day one: create the account, run the interview prompt, and do the vague versus specific experiment.
  • Before your next practice: build the plan, check it yourself, run the skeptic prompt, and edit it.
  • After practice: write one line about what worked, give it to Claude without any child's name, and ask it to adjust next week's plan.

That loop, plan, run, observe, adjust, is where the real value is, because your observations are what Claude cannot get on its own. When you are ready for more, ask it directly:

"I coach youth soccer and I am new to using AI. Here is my situation: [age group, format, season length, and the one task that eats too much of your time]. Suggest five coaching or team admin tasks you could help me with this week, ranked from lowest risk to highest, and tell me what details you would need from me for each one." Use this when you are not sure what to try next

From there, the guides build in order. First, train AI on your coaching philosophy, a ten minute setup. Second, read the detailed guide to using AI as a coach. Then pick what you need: a season long practice plan, small sided game variations, a parent handbook, or a sponsorship letter.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Giving up after one generic answer. A generic answer usually means a generic request. Add a real observation or ask a follow up first.
  • Repeating a fact without checking it, especially a rule, a statistic, or anything about safety.
  • Putting a child's personal information into the chat, even for something that feels harmless.

Give Claude something real to work with

The best material for an AI assistant is your own team's real numbers. SubGo tracks playing time and MatchTap tracks match events, and both are free with no account. SubGo's CSV includes player names, so switch your roster to jersey numbers or initials, or delete the player column, before you give the file to any AI tool. MatchTap exports one game at a time, so combine the files yourself. Then ask Claude to summarize them.
