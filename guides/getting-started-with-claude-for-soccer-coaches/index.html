How to Get Started With Claude: From Zero to Your First Coaching Task

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · October 18, 2026 · 11 min read

If you have never used AI before and are not even sure where to click first, this guide is for you. It starts at the very beginning, creating an account and typing your first message, then covers the handful of settings and habits worth knowing on day one, and ends with a real soccer coaching task you can finish in about ten minutes. No technical background is needed, and nothing here assumes you already know what a prompt is.

Who this guide is for

A volunteer coach, club coach, or team manager who keeps hearing that AI can save time on practice plans and parent emails but has never actually tried it. Every example here uses Claude, the assistant made by Anthropic, because it is the one we use to run this site. The same basic ideas carry over to other assistants like ChatGPT. When you finish, our guide to training AI on your coaching philosophy and our detailed guide to using AI as a coach are the next two stops.

The short version
  • Go to claude.ai and create a free account. You do not need to pay anything to start.
  • Write to it the way you would brief a new assistant coach: plain language, real details, and a clear statement of what you want back.
  • Treat every answer as a first draft. Claude can be confidently wrong, so check any fact before you repeat it to a parent or a player.
  • Never put a child's name, photo, address, or medical details into an AI chat. Describe situations in general terms instead.
  • Start with something low risk, like a practice plan, then build from there.

The basicsWhat Claude actually is, in plain terms

Claude is an AI assistant. You type a message, it writes back, and you can keep the conversation going as long as you like, the way you would text a very fast, very well read assistant who is awake at any hour. You can ask it to draft an email, explain a rule, build a practice plan, rewrite something shorter, or turn a messy list into a clean one. It is not a search engine that hands you a list of links, and it is not a person who knows your team. It only knows what you tell it in the conversation, plus whatever it learned in training about the world in general.

That one fact explains most of what goes well and most of what goes wrong. When you give it real details, the answers get useful quickly. When you give it nothing, it guesses, and a confident guess can sound exactly like a correct answer. Hold two ideas together as you read the rest of this guide: it is useful for a first draft of almost anything, and it is never the last word on anything that matters.

Step oneCreate your account

Open a web browser, go to claude.ai, and follow the prompts to sign up. Anthropic's own getting started tutorial treats claude.ai/new as the starting point for a new user. Claude has a free plan, which includes chat on the web, mobile, and desktop, and paid plans above it with higher usage limits. Start on the free plan. The main thing you may notice is that it has the tightest usage limits, and you can decide later whether that ever matters enough to pay. Plans and prices change, so check Anthropic's pricing page instead of trusting any number we could print here.

One rule to know before you sign up: Anthropic's consumer terms say "you must be at least 18 years old... to use the Services." That makes this a tool for you, the coach, not for the players. Do not hand it to a child for a team project. There is also a phone app and a desktop app, and either is a fine addition later, especially for a quick question at the field. Starting in a browser is perfectly good.

Step twoFind your way around in two minutes

The screen is simpler than it looks. There is one big box for typing. Type a message, press enter, and Claude answers beneath it. Everything you say and every answer stays in one conversation, and starting a new chat gives you a fresh page. Near the message box is a plus button that opens extra options, such as attaching a file or turning web search on or off. Anthropic's tutorial describes web search as on by default for Free, Pro, and Max plans, and lists the plus menu as the home for extras like connectors and web search.

You can ignore almost all of that for now. Menus and button names change as the product changes, so if something here does not match your screen exactly, look for the closest match. Learning where things live is a five minute job, and nothing in this guide depends on a specific button.

Step threeYour first conversation, with no soccer in it yet

Do not start with a high stakes request. Start by learning how it feels to talk to it. Anthropic's tutorial says to write to Claude "in plain language" and not to worry about getting the wording perfect, because if an answer misses, you can say so and Claude will adjust. If you are not even sure what to ask, there is an easy move: have Claude interview you first. Paste this into a new conversation:

"I am brand new to using AI and I am not sure what to ask. Ask me a few questions, one at a time, about what I am trying to get done this week, then suggest three things I could try with you." Paste this into your very first chat if you do not know where to start

Notice what happened. You did not need a perfect prompt. A prompt is just the message you type, and a good one usually comes out of a short back and forth, not from one perfectly worded sentence. Anthropic's tutorial suggests the same trick for any vague request: ask Claude to question you about what you are trying to do, then have it write a better prompt.

Step fourFour habits that make every answer better

  • Give real details. "Write a parent email" gets you something generic. "Write a short, friendly email to the parents of my U10 team about moving Saturday's game to 9 a.m." gets you a draft you can actually send after a quick edit.
  • Say what you want back. Tell it the length, the tone, and the format: under 100 words, warm but direct, as a short list, as a table.
  • Follow up instead of starting over. Say "shorter," "warmer," "put that in a table," or "try again, but for a rainy day." Within a single conversation, Claude can see everything said above, so each follow up builds on the last.
  • Attach the source when there is one. A schedule, a past email, or a rules document gives Claude something real to work from. Anthropic's tutorial recommends attaching documents for context, and turning web search off when an answer should come only from your own documents.

Step fiveTwo settings worth a look before you go further

The first is privacy. Open your account settings and look for Privacy. As of this writing there is a toggle labeled "Help improve Claude," and when it is on, Anthropic can use your chats to train its models. Switching it off stops that for new chats and for older conversations you open afterward. Whether to leave it on is your decision, so read the exact wording on screen yourself, since settings like this get reworded over time.

The second is memory. Claude can save useful context from your chats, such as your role and your communication preferences, and bring it into future conversations. Anthropic's help center says memory is on by default for Free, Pro, and Max plans on the web, desktop, and mobile apps, and its tutorial notes you can read or edit what Claude has saved at any time. That is a real time saver for a coach, because it means you do not retype the same team details in every new chat. Our guide to training AI on your coaching philosophy shows how to use it on purpose.

Whichever way you set the privacy toggle, one rule does not depend on it. Do not put a child's personal information into any general AI chat: no full names, photos, home addresses, birthdates, or medical notes. Use first names only, initials, or jersey numbers, and describe situations in general terms. Our detailed AI guide explains where this rule comes from.

The one habitCheck anything you plan to repeat

Claude writes fluently even when it is wrong. Anthropic says so plainly about its own product in its developer documentation: "even the most advanced language models, like Claude, can sometimes generate text that is factually incorrect or inconsistent with the given context." For a coach, that means a rule, a statistic, or a safety guideline should never go to a parent just because the answer sounded sure of itself. Draft with it. Verify before you repeat it.

A simple way to build the habit is to ask Claude to point at its own weak spots. It will not catch everything, but it is a useful prompt for a second look:

"How confident are you in that answer? Tell me which parts you are least sure about, and what I should double check before I share it with anyone." Paste this after any answer you plan to repeat to a parent, a player, or a team page

For a rule or a statistic, go one step further: ask for the exact source, then open it yourself. Our detailed guide has the exact prompt for that, and it is the one habit worth keeping even if you take nothing else from any of these guides.

Step sixYour first real coaching task: a practice plan

A practice plan is the best first coaching task. It is useful the same night, it is low risk because you will read it before you run it, and it shows you right away how much better an answer gets when you give real details. Fill in the brackets with your actual team:

"I coach [age group] [format, such as 7v7] soccer with [number] players and [number] minutes of practice time on [describe your space]. We worked on [last practice's focus] last time. Build me one practice plan with a warm up, technical work, small sided games, and a scrimmage, focused on [this week's focus]. Keep each part short and list the equipment I need." Fill in the brackets with your real team, then read the plan before you use it

Then use the follow up habit from step four. Ask for a rainy day version, a version for half a field, or a version that works with only eight cones. Each change takes seconds, and that back and forth is where most of the value is. Read the finished plan the way you would read one handed to you by a stranger. You know your field and your players. Claude does not, and it cannot tell which of your kids melts down when a drill involves standing in a line.

If you want a stronger structure to ask for, our practice planning guide lays out a one hour session you can name in the prompt.

Next stepsWhere to go from here

Once that first plan works, you will probably want to know what else is worth handing off. Ask Claude directly:

"I coach youth soccer and I am new to using AI. Here is my situation: [age group, format, season length, and the one task that eats too much of your time]. Suggest five coaching or team admin tasks you could help me with this week, ranked from lowest risk to highest, and tell me what details you would need from me for each one." Use this when you are not sure what to try next

From there, the guides on this site build in a rough order:

  • First, train AI on your coaching philosophy. A ten minute setup that stops you from re explaining your team in every conversation.
  • Second, read the detailed guide to using AI as a coach. Practice plans, schedule imports, parent communication, the verification habit, and the privacy rules in full.
  • Then pick what you need. Build a season long practice plan, generate small sided game variations, draft a parent handbook, or write a sponsorship letter.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Starting with a high stakes request instead of a low risk one, and getting discouraged when the first result needs work.
  • Giving up after one generic answer. A generic answer usually means a generic request. Add real details, or ask a follow up, before deciding it does not work.
  • Treating the first answer as final. Almost every useful result comes after at least one round of "shorter," "warmer," or "try again."
  • Repeating a fact without checking it, especially a rule, a statistic, or anything about safety.
  • Putting a child's personal information into the chat, including a name, photo, address, birthdate, or medical detail, even for something that feels harmless.
  • Retyping the same team details in every new chat instead of letting memory or a saved coaching profile carry them.
  • Assuming screens and settings stay the same. Names and menus change, so trust what you see on your own screen over any guide, including this one.

Give Claude something real to work with

The best material for an AI assistant is your own team's real numbers. SubGo tracks playing time and MatchTap tracks match events, and both are free with no account. Export a season of either and hand it to Claude to summarize.
