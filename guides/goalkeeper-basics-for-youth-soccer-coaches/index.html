Goalkeeper Basics for Coaches Who Never Played: What to Actually Teach First

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 25, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 13 min read

Most volunteer coaches never played goalkeeper, and almost all goalkeeper coaching content assumes you did. This is a starting curriculum for the rest of us: the stance, two ways to catch, footwork before diving, a safe way to fall, and the handling rule that most coaches still explain wrong.

Who this guide is for

A coach with a young goalkeeper, roughly ages 9 to 12, and no goalkeeping background yourself. This covers the first few sessions, not a full goalkeeper coaching course.

The short version
  • A keeper who holds the ball more than eight seconds gives away a corner kick. The old six second rule and indirect free kick are gone.
  • Teach in order: stance, catching, footwork, then a safe fall before any real dive.
  • Start with confidence, not technique, and keep goalkeeper work to part of a practice.
  • Do not lock one child in goal. US Youth Soccer says goalkeeping in games before age nine is inappropriate and that younger children should be exposed to goalkeeper style movements in play. From about U10, rotate willing players through the position.
  • Check your league's rules. For example, 9U and 10U goalkeepers may not punt or drop kick under U.S. Soccer's standards.

Know this firstThe handling rule most coaches explain wrong

If you learned the "six second rule" at some point, it is worth unlearning. Starting in the 2025/26 season, IFAB, the body that writes the actual Laws of the Game, changed both the number and the penalty, and the 2026/27 Laws keep the change. The current wording is direct: "A corner kick is awarded if a goalkeeper, inside their penalty area, controls the ball with their hand(s)/arm(s) for more than eight seconds before releasing it." That is a real change from the old rule, where holding it too long gave the other team an indirect free kick instead of a corner. The referee counts down the last five seconds with a raised hand, so a young keeper can actually see it coming rather than being surprised by a whistle. Also new for 2026/27 under the IFAB Laws is a five second countdown for a delayed goal kick, with a corner kick to the opponents if it runs out. Youth leagues often adapt the Laws, so ask your league or referee assignor whether yours applies either change.

The other rule worth knowing before you ever mention it to a kid is the back pass rule. A goalkeeper cannot pick up the ball with their hands after a teammate deliberately kicks it to them, or after receiving it directly from a teammate's throw in. IFAB's Law 12 lists exactly those two situations as offenses, with an exception if the keeper has clearly kicked or tried to kick the ball to release it into play. In plain terms for a young player: feet only if a teammate passes it back with their foot or throws it in, hands are fine for everything else inside the box.

Start hereThe stance everything else builds from

Before catching, footwork, or diving, a goalkeeper needs a ready position that lets them move in any direction immediately. US Youth Soccer's Skills School Manual describes it this way: feet about shoulder width apart, weight forward and up on the balls of the feet, knees bent with the center of gravity lowered, and arms in front. That is the entire first lesson. A kid standing flat footed with their weight back cannot react to anything, no matter how good their hands are. The same manual treats this stance as something to teach from U10 up, so with a nine year old keep it playful and short.

Have them hold the position, then react to a rolled ball, then a thrown ball, before a single shot is taken. The stance is boring to teach and easy to skip, which is exactly why most young keepers never really learn it.

The core skillCatching what comes at them

For a ball above the waist, most coaches teach a hand shape that gets called the "W technique," fingers spread and thumbs close together behind the ball. Worth knowing as you teach it: that specific name is common shorthand in coaching circles, not an official term from a published curriculum, so do not be surprised if you cannot find it in a rulebook. The mechanic itself is what matters: hands behind the ball, not underneath it, so a miscatch still gets smothered against the body instead of squirting through.

For a ball on the ground, a common technique is the basket catch: hands and arms parallel with the fingers pointing toward the ground, then the ball is scooped into the body to secure it. Picture a scoop that closes into a hug. Teach the low one first, because a scoop that closes into the body is a more forgiving mistake than a stiff armed stab at a bouncing ball. Coaching terms for these techniques vary between organizations, so do not worry if your club uses different names.

Before divingFootwork, not diving, comes next

The instinct is to jump straight to diving saves because they look like real goalkeeping. Footwork is what actually gets a keeper to the ball in time, often without diving at all. US Youth Soccer's manual puts it simply: it is the feet that get the hands to the ball. Three movements are worth teaching by name, and here is how they are commonly taught. The shuffle is a quick sideways step for short distances in the goal, keeping the hips level. The crossover covers a greater distance across the goal, with the keeper crossing the feet in front, never behind. The drop step is a step backward with one foot for balls played toward the crossbar, keeping the eyes on the ball. Teaching these three by name gives a young keeper a specific answer to "how do I get there" instead of just "get there."

PositioningCutting the angle

A young keeper's instinct is to stand on the goal line, which leaves the most net exposed. The idea to teach is cutting the angle: as a shooter approaches or the ball moves toward the middle of the field, take a few steps off the line toward the ball, which shrinks the amount of open net the shooter can see. US Youth Soccer's Skills School Manual lists repositioning to an angle facing the ball as a core goalkeeper skill. For young keepers, use one simple cue, such as "step out toward the ball," and do not expect it to be consistent until they are older.

Safety firstTeach a safe fall before a real dive

This is the step most impatient coaches skip, and it is the one most likely to prevent an actual injury. A common teaching progression is to start with the keeper on both knees reaching for the ball, then move to one knee and one foot down, and only then finish with a standing dive. Repeat each stage until it looks controlled before moving to the next one.

The falling technique itself matters as much as the save: the ball should hit the ground first with the body following, and the elbows should stay tucked and relaxed in front of the chest instead of bracing out to catch the fall. These are common coaching practices, and we could not link a public source for the exact steps, so ask your club's goalkeeper coach if their approach differs.

A sports medicine practice writing on youth goalkeeper injuries (ActiveKidMD) adds a related caution: wrist fractures are commonly seen in children or adolescents who have their hands driven backwards towards the wrist when trying to make a save on a direct shot. That is one more reason to teach technique patiently. One thing worth knowing before you tell parents that better gloves fix this: the same page states that there is no solid evidence that goalkeeper braces or gloves provide adequate injury reduction. Good technique, taught in this order, is the better bet, and a good glove is comfort and grip, not a safety device.

One more safety line: keepers hang on crossbars, so anchor any movable goal (see the CPSC guidelines for movable soccer goals) and never practice diving on hard or frozen ground.

Getting it back outThe four ways a keeper restarts play

A young keeper who only knows how to punt the ball as far as possible is giving up half the position. Keepers commonly restart play four ways: a goal kick, a punt, a throw, and a roll along the ground to a nearby teammate's feet. Check your league before teaching the punt. Under the U.S. Soccer Player Development Initiatives, as summarized by AYSO, goalkeepers in 9U and 10U games may not punt or drop kick the ball, and build out lines apply. U.S. Soccer's 2017 standards put the line 14 yards in front of each goal for the games that use it, and some leagues place it halfway between the top of the penalty area and the midfield line, so check yours. At those ages, the roll or throw to an open teammate is worth teaching first and praising when it happens, since it keeps possession instead of trading it for field position.

FirstStart with confidence, not technique

If your keeper is afraid of the ball, a perfect stance will not fix it. Start with a lighter ball, short distances and rolled balls, praise every stop however it happens, and let the child decide when to stop. Keep goalkeeper work to about ten or fifteen minutes of a practice, ideally next to work with the ball at their feet, so they do not feel singled out. These are our coaching suggestions, not research findings.

ExampleA sample three session plan

Here is one way to spread the order over three short sessions of about 15 minutes each. It is our suggestion, built from the progression in this guide.

SessionFocusIdeas
1Stance and handlingHold the ready position, react to a rolled ball, then a thrown ball. Basket catch on the ground, hands behind the ball above the waist.
2Footwork and anglesShuffle and crossover between two cones. Drop step to a lofted toss. Step out toward the ball as a teammate dribbles at goal.
3Safe falling and restartsFalls from both knees, then one knee, then standing, on grass, to a rolled ball. Roll and throw to a teammate. Practice goal kicks.

The bigger pictureDo not lock one kid into the position too early

It is tempting to find the one kid who is not afraid of the ball and leave them in net all season. The Official US Youth Soccer Coaching Manual is direct about the age to even start: "the authors believe that initiating goalkeeping in games prior to age nine is inappropriate," and once players reach that age, "Players at the U-10 level and older should be encouraged, not forced, to be exposed to goalkeeping roles in practice." Encouraged and exposed, not assigned permanently.

US Youth Soccer's Player Development Model makes the same point from a different angle. A goalkeeper statement quoted in it says players in the U-10, U-12 and U-14 age groups "should not begin to specialize in any position at this time in their development." Its "Development of a Goalkeeper" progression chart lists "All team members play goalkeeper" for U8 to U10, which clashes with the manual's advice against goalkeeping in games before age nine. We follow the manual: goalkeeper style movements in play for the youngest, then rotating willing players from about U10. Our guide to positions by format covers rotating every player through different roles as players move from small sided to full sided games, and goalkeeper belongs in that rotation, not outside it.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Skipping the falling progression and putting a young keeper straight into diving saves.
  • Training the goalkeeper alone, away from the rest of the team, instead of folding them into regular technical work with the ball at their feet.
  • Praising the save count instead of the stance, angle, or footwork that made the save possible in the first place.
  • Assigning one player as "the goalkeeper" for an entire season without rotating other willing players through the role, or putting any child in goal in games before age nine.
  • Telling parents better gloves prevent injuries, when the one source we found says there is no solid evidence that they do.
  • Making goalkeeper work the whole practice, or starting with technique before a nervous child feels safe.
