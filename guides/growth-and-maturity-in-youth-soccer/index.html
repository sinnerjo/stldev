Growth Spurts, Not Talent: Why Some Players Look a Year Ahead

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of MatchTap · September 23, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 15 min read

Look down your roster at any age group and you will see it. Two players born five months apart can look like they are playing different sports, one already has an adult sized frame and a long stride, the other still has a kid's body and gets knocked off the ball. Our tryouts and evaluations guide flags this as a scoring risk. This guide goes further into what is actually happening, what real research says about it, and how to coach both players fairly while it sorts itself out.

Who this guide is for

Coaches evaluating, selecting, or building playing time plans for players roughly ages 9 to 15, which is our rule of thumb for when growth timing varies the most. The maturation studies cited here are of male players, and timing differs between girls and boys, so do not apply the numbers to girls. Only the principle of scoring physical and technical traits separately carries over. Pair this with our tryouts guide for scoring mechanics and our equal playing time guide for why minutes matter regardless of size.

The short version
  • Two different things get confused. Relative age is which month a player was born in relative to the selection cutoff. Biological maturation is how far along puberty a player is at the same chronological age. Both create real physical gaps that have nothing to do with skill.
  • The gap can be large. In pooled data on male soccer players at age 13, the average height of boys at different pubertal stages differs by roughly 17 centimeters, and the average weight by roughly 19 kilograms. Individual players vary even more.
  • Early maturers get picked more, and get more minutes. That is documented in several studies, though some are single academy or single tournament samples. Whether late maturers reliably catch up or overtake by 16 to 18, the popular version of this story, is weaker evidence than most coaching content suggests.
  • Real bio banding needs staff and measurement most teams do not have. The underlying idea, though, mixing training groups by physical stage instead of only by age, is something you can borrow informally.
  • Score physical traits and technical traits separately. A smaller player's first touch should not get marked down because they are not winning the ball as often this year.
  • Do not forget the early maturer, and do not collect body data. Size can hide technical gaps, and a volunteer coach should not measure or post children's heights or weights.

Two problems, not oneRelative age and biological maturation are not the same thing

Relative age effect is the more famous of the two, and the numbers are stark. A 2025 perspective article by Roger Barnsley in Frontiers in Sports and Active Living, looking back on four decades of relative age research, reports that "about 40% of elite young adult players were born in the first quarter of the age cohort, compared to just 10% in the last quarter." It also reports that soccer players born in the first quarter were five times more likely to be selected to play International Youth Elite Soccer than players born in the last quarter, though that figure comes from 1989 youth World Tournaments, not recent data. A separate 2025 study of 360 male under 14 players at a national tournament found the pattern just as sharply: 26 of the 28 players later selected to the national U15 pool were born in the first half of the year, and none of the 28 players born in the last quarter of the year made the pool.

Relative age is about the calendar. Biological maturation is a separate variable layered on top of it, and it is easy to miss because it does not follow a birth certificate at all. Two players born on the same day can be at completely different points in puberty, one already past their adolescent growth spurt, the other a year or two from starting theirs. Sports scientists measure this against age at peak height velocity, the point of fastest growth during the adolescent spurt, and classify players at a given age as early, average, or late maturers relative to that curve.

The hidden gapHow much difference maturity timing actually creates

This is not a small effect. A review by Malina and colleagues in the British Journal of Sports Medicine compiles pooled data on Portuguese, Spanish, and Italian male soccer players. Grouped by pubic hair stage, a standard measure of puberty, boys of the same chronological age differ a lot in average size. At age 13, with more boys in each stage, the stage averages run from about 154 to 171 centimeters in height and from about 43 to 62 kilograms in weight. That is a spread of roughly 17 centimeters and 19 kilograms between players who are, on paper, the exact same age. At age 11 the picture is less clear. Almost all boys in the data were at stage 1 or 2, and those stages differed by only a few centimeters, so the larger gap at that age rests on four boys at later stages. These are differences between stage averages, so individual players vary even more. The data are pooled from six studies, and the authors say stage is not the same thing as maturity timing. None of it reflects a difference in soccer ability, and all of it is visible the moment two players line up next to each other.

This is the gap a coach is actually looking at during a tryout, even when the explanation that comes to mind is talent. A study by Hill and colleagues of nine male academy coaches and 98 boys at one English Premier League academy found size mixing with perception in real time. One coach, describing an early maturing player, said "he gets away with a few things because of his size," and described the player "just stepping on the ball" in a 1v1 drill. The coach was describing a technical weakness that size was covering for, not a technical strength.

Over timeWhat actually happens to early and late maturers

The selection bias against late maturers is documented across several studies, though many are single academy or single tournament samples. In the 2025 under 14 tournament study, late maturing players "played significantly fewer minutes" than their peers, and among the selected players, about 90 percent were early or on time maturers, with only about 10 percent late. In the Hill study of one academy, a coach described grading performance differently depending on how physically developed a player looked. If an early maturing player had a slight off game, he said, "he goes down to a 2." Of a late maturing player with exactly the same performance, he said, "I would give him a 4, because my bias is clearly going on." That was one coach's self reported, hypothetical example, not a measured comparison of grades, but it shows how the bias can feel from the inside.

Where the evidence gets thinner is the popular follow up claim, that late maturing players systematically catch up or overtake the early maturers by 16 to 18. A 2018 study by Cumming and colleagues of 171 male academy players aged 11 to 16 found that later maturation was associated with better self regulation, concluding that "in partial support of the underdog hypothesis, later maturing players appear to possess a psychological advantage." That is a real, specific, honest finding, but it is about self regulation, not a guarantee of overtaking anyone in ability. We did not find a well powered study tracking the same players from age 12 through a professional career that confirms late bloomers reliably win out in the end. Treat that part of the story as plausible, not settled, when you talk to a parent about it.

Bio bandingWhat clubs try, and what a small team can actually borrow

Some academies address this with bio banding, grouping players for some training or competition by biological maturity rather than birth year. A 2019 paper in Sports Medicine defines it as grouping athletes "into 'bands' or groups based on characteristic(s) other than" chronological age, while being direct about its limits, "bio-banding reduces, but does not eliminate, maturity-associated variation." A 2026 systematic review of 13 studies covering 861 youth players found real value in short term settings but was honest about the ceiling on that evidence, concluding the current research "relies heavily on short-term match responses and proxy outcomes," with little direct evidence of better selection accuracy or long-term development. One academy practitioner interviewed about actually running it put the practical reality plainly, "bio banding is maybe more of a luxury tool as opposed to something that sort of needs to happen."

That last quote matters for a rec or travel team. Real bio banding needs staff, cross club scheduling, and physical measurements most volunteer coaches cannot arrange, so do not treat it as a program to build. What you can borrow is the underlying idea, not the method. Rotate small sided game groupings periodically by size and physical stage instead of only by roster age. Build a few sessions around constraints that neutralize a size advantage, two touch limits, no shoulder to shoulder challenges, tight spaces that reward a quick first touch over a long stride, so you can actually see the technical picture underneath the physical one. This is a reasonable extension of the bio banding idea, not a validated program on its own, and it is worth being upfront with yourself about that difference.

PracticeCoaching and evaluating players fairly across the gap

The clearest fix starts on your evaluation sheet. If speed, size, and physical presence sit in the same category as first touch and decision making, a bigger player wins the category by default and a smaller player looks worse than their actual technical level. Split them. Score technical and tactical categories on their own, and treat physical traits as a separate line you weigh deliberately rather than let bleed into everything else, exactly the caution our tryouts guide recommends when building a scoring rubric.

The same discipline applies during the season, not just at tryouts. If a smaller or later developing player is quietly getting fewer minutes because they lose more physical battles right now, that is the pattern the research above documents, not a fluke on your team. A deliberate policy of minute equity, tracked rather than assumed, is a direct countermeasure. When you talk to that player's parents, the honest version is not a promise. It is closer to, they are not behind, they have not hit their growth spurt yet, here is specifically what we are doing with their reps in the meantime, and here is how we will know if that is working. That framing is accurate and still genuinely reassuring, without overselling an outcome nobody can guarantee.

Do not forget the early maturer

The early maturer needs coaching too. Size can hide technical gaps, as the Hill coach noticed, and a player who can win every ball on strength may never need to develop the touch or the decision making they will need when everyone else catches up. These are our suggestions, not research findings. Give the early maturer a technical target of their own, such as using their weaker foot or finding the pass before the tackle. Do not talk about them as the big kid or the problem. And do not let a team lean on their size, which can leave them playing a narrow role instead of developing a full game.

What you see and what to try

What you seeWhat to try
One player wins most 50/50 balls on strengthAdd a two touch limit or no shoulder challenges so technique decides the contest.
A smaller player keeps losing the ball to bigger opponentsUse tight space games that reward a quick first touch over a long stride.

An early maturer coasting on sizeSet a technical target that size cannot solve, like weaker foot or quick passing.
A later developing player who seems frustratedPraise specific touches and decisions, and track their minutes.

Mismatched size is also a safety question. We did not review injury research for this guide, so this is a coaching judgment: in drills or games with heavy contact, avoid pairing a player who is much further along physically with one who is not, and use the constraints above to keep contact fair.

Should you measure itFormulas exist, but most teams do not need them

There are published methods for estimating where a player sits on the maturity curve without imaging. One is the Khamis-Roche method, which predicts adult height using a player's current height, weight, age, and both parents' heights. In a 2023 narrative systematic review, Sullivan and colleagues report that the Bayley-Pinneau, Tanner-Whitehouse 2, and Khamis-Roche methods produced estimates of adult height that were typically off by about half a centimeter to two centimeters in the studies reviewed. That helps researchers, but it does not tell a coach which kids are early or late. The review also says non invasive methods showed "only fair to moderate agreement" with invasive methods such as skeletal age from imaging. In other words, these are useful estimates, not diagnoses.

Our recommendation for a volunteer coach is plain: do not collect, record, or post children's heights or weights, or ask for their parents' heights. That is sensitive information about a child's body and a family, and it can feed body shame. Measurement belongs with qualified staff and the family, with consent, and it stays out of team conversations. You do not need a precise maturity number to coach well through this gap. You need the awareness that it exists and is temporary, and a habit of separating what you are watching, physical development or actual skill, every time you evaluate a player. Eyeballing who "looks more advanced" has no validation in the sources we reviewed, so hold that read loosely. It is an observation, not a measurement. Never comment on a child's body in front of teammates or parents, and if a parent worries about their child's growth, send them to the family pediatrician.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Scoring size and speed in the same breath as skill, instead of on a separate line you can weigh on purpose.
  • Assuming today's bigger player will still be the better player at 16, when the evidence for that reversal is thinner than most coaching content implies.
  • Promising a parent their child will "catch up and be better later" as a guarantee rather than a real, honest possibility.
  • Letting playing minutes drift toward whoever is currently bigger, without tracking it, so the gap becomes a season long pattern instead of a passing one.
  • Trying to run a formal bio banding or maturity offset program on a volunteer team that does not have the staff or measurement tools it actually requires.
  • Treating an evaluation made at age 11 as permanent, instead of revisiting it once growth spurts start changing the picture.
  • Letting an early maturer coast on size, instead of coaching their technique too.
  • Collecting or posting children's heights, weights, or parents' heights, which is sensitive data a volunteer coach should not hold.
  • Applying boys' growth numbers to girls, when timing differs and the studies cited here are of male players.

Ask your AI assistant coachDraft the parent conversation, and adjust training across the group

AI is useful here for finding words and for planning sessions. It cannot estimate a child's maturity, predict how tall a player will be, or tell you whether a player is a late developer, and you should not give it any heights, weights, or ages to try. If you are new to AI, start with our beginner guide, and for answers that sound like you, set up a coaching profile first.

1. The parent conversation. Good output is a few calm, honest sentences with no promises.

"I coach [age group] [format]. One of my players looks smaller and less physical than most of their teammates, and it may be because they are still earlier in their growth. Help me write three or four calm, honest sentences I could say to their parent explaining that players the same age can be at different stages of growth, without promising they will catch up, plus one or two specific things I am doing to keep their reps and confidence up in the meantime." Paste this before the conversation comes up, not during it

2. Practice adjustments. Good output is a few constraints and groupings, with no player named.

"I coach [age group] [format], and my roster has a wide range of physical size this season. Suggest a few practice adjustments, such as constraints like two touch limits or no shoulder challenges, small sided game groupings, or drill variations, that let players at different physical stages compete on more even footing while still developing real technical skill. Do not reference any specific player." Paste this when planning training, not about any one player

3. A pressure test. Once you have a plan, ask the AI to find where it might hurt a child.

"Here is my plan for handling size differences on my team: [paste it, with no names]. Act as an experienced youth coach and point out anything that could embarrass a smaller or bigger player, single someone out, or lean too hard on size. List the problems only." Fix what it finds before you use the plan

Describe a player in general terms only, such as "one of my players" or "a ten year old on my team," and leave out names, heights, weights, and any detail that would let a stranger identify them. The AI can help you find your own words for a general situation, or plan a session for the group, not evaluate or make promises about a specific child's future development. AI can also invent statistics and citations, so check any number it gives you before you repeat it.
