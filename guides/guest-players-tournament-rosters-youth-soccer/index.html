Guest Players and Tournament Rosters: Staying Fair When You Borrow Players for a Weekend

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 27, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 10 min read

A tournament weekend has a way of exposing a thin roster. Someone is on vacation, someone is injured, and suddenly you are three players short of a full squad for three games in two days. Borrowing a guest player from another team solves the immediate problem, but it creates two new ones if you are not careful, a guest player who does not know what to expect, and a group of your own players whose minutes just got squeezed by someone who was not even on the team a week ago.

Who this guide is for

Coaches adding one or more guest players to a tournament roster, who want the weekend to be fair to their own team and to the player they borrowed. Our equal playing time guide covers the underlying standard this guide is protecting during an unusual weekend. If you are deciding how much to lean on guest players versus your own roster depth all season, our roster size math guide covers that tradeoff.

The short version
  • Check the actual tournament's rules and deadlines first. Guest limits and paperwork are set by the tournament host and your state association, not one national rule.
  • Ask the other club first, not the family. Approaching a player directly can break association rules, and the player's home club has to approve the loan.
  • Use guest players to fill a real gap, an injury or a scheduling conflict, not to stack a roster for a trophy.
  • Hold every game to your normal playing time floor, guests included. Use the weekend view to catch and fix imbalance, not to excuse a bad game.
  • A guest player is a teammate for the weekend, not a body to plug a hole. Give them a real role and tell them early what to expect.
  • Start the paperwork weeks ahead. Some tournaments want guest names and rosters submitted two weeks before the event.

Rules firstKnow the actual rule before you build the roster

U.S. Youth Soccer's national policy (the December 2025 edition we checked) offers a suggested definition for state associations: a guest player is "a registered player participating in a competition for a team to which the player is not rostered," and the full wording adds "for purposes of league play." League games are different. The same policy says every player listed on the game roster must be on the team roster, "except for tournament competitions that may accept guest players." State associations add their own rules on top. Oklahoma's 2014 policy, for example, also allows guests in friendlies, does not allow them in league play unless a regional competition permits it, and allows no guests at all in its state competitive tournaments.

There is no single national number for how many guests a team can carry. The tournament host decides, and the numbers vary. Seacoast United's spring showcase rules (year not stated) permit five guest or loan players per team. The Target USA Cup's 2026 rules allow up to six, with double rostered players capped at two. Oklahoma's 2014 policy caps guests at one for U6 and U8 teams and two for U10. These examples show the kinds of rules to expect, not current limits for your event. The tournament you are entering this weekend may allow two guests, or five, or none, and the only way to know is to read that tournament's own rules rather than assuming last year's limit still applies.

Before you askAsk the other club, not the family

The natural instinct is to message a family you know and ask whether their child can play. Resist it. Florida Youth Soccer's guest playing rule (Rule 208, as posted in 2017) says no player is guaranteed the right to guest play, that the home association can deny it, and that coaches must get permission from the registering association before contacting the player, or it may be treated as poaching. Your association may word it differently, but the safe order is the same everywhere: talk to the other club's coach or registrar first, let them decide who is available, and let the family hear about it from their own club. Also ask about the player's own schedule, since a guest who has a game of their own that weekend will not help you.

PurposeBe honest about why you are doing it

There is a real difference between guest playing to solve a genuine shortage and guest playing to build a stronger team than the one you actually coach all season. A team that is short two players because of a family vacation and a minor injury has a legitimate reason to guest a couple of players. A team that regularly stacks its tournament roster with outside talent to chase a bracket is doing something different, and the cost lands on your own regular players, who end up on the bench at a tournament they trained for all season. A good test: would you still borrow this player if the tournament had no bracket?

Your own teamProtect your rostered players' minutes first

Use two levels of fairness. First, hold every game to the same floor as your regular season: every available rostered player, guests included, plays at least half of each game, as our equal playing time guide sets out. Second, use the whole weekend to catch drift. Tally minutes after every game, and use the games still ahead to bring anyone who fell behind back toward equal. The weekend view is for correcting a problem early, not an excuse to let one game go sideways because the average will work out later.

Here is what that looks like. Say you have 9 regulars and 2 guests at 7v7, so 11 players, playing three 48 minute games. Each game has 7 players on the field for 48 minutes, which is 336 player minutes. Equal is about 31 minutes each per game, or about 92 over three games. The floor is 24 minutes per game for every player, which accounts for 264 of the 336 minutes. That leaves 72 minutes per game that you can give to whoever the game needs, as long as nobody drops below 24. If a guest has to play a position you lack, that is fine. If the guest plays 40 minutes while a regular sits at 20, that is not. For a ready made rotation, see the worked chart in our substitution patterns guide. A guest exists to fill a gap, not to quietly take development time from the players who show up to training every week.

The guest playerBe fair to the player you borrowed too

Fairness runs in both directions. A family agreeing to guest their child for a weekend deserves clarity up front, not surprises once the tournament starts. Settle cost expectations early. A coach may cover uniforms and tournament fees but still expect a contribution toward travel, and a text message that spells it out is enough to keep everyone on the same page. Check your association's rules on any payment or reimbursement before offering one. The same applies to playing time. If you are borrowing a player specifically because you need real minutes from them, say so. If they are depth who may only see one half all weekend, say that too, before they show up expecting a starting role.

Game daySet them up to succeed in one weekend

A guest player does not have the benefit of a full season learning your team's habits, so do not hand them a complicated system and expect it to click by kickoff. Introduce them to the group before the first game, tell them plainly what position or role you need from them, and keep any tactical instruction simple and specific. Pairing them with one regular who can give them a quiet cue during the game, where to stand on a throw in, who covers what on a corner, does more for a guest's first game than any amount of pregame explanation. The goal is for them to feel like a real teammate, not a part bolted on to fill a gap.

A welcome checklist

  • The position or role you need and roughly how many minutes to expect.
  • Their jersey number. Many tournaments require unique numbers on the roster, so decide who supplies it.
  • Game times, field locations and when to arrive.
  • A parent contact and a signed medical consent form, which many tournaments require for guests. Confirm the guest is covered by their home club's registration and insurance, and that a parent will be at the event or has signed a medical release.
  • A buddy from your regulars for the weekend.

LogisticsGet the paperwork right, weeks before you travel

Guest play usually needs approval from the player's home club, and often from the state association as well, before the player takes the field. Florida's rule describes a guest player form completed by the primary club's registrar and listing the dates and competition covered, plus an interstate permission form when the player crosses state lines. Oklahoma's 2014 policy requires an interstate permission form approved by both associations before play can begin, and traveling guests there need a current player pass, a signed medical release and a roster from their primary team.

Tournaments then add their own requirements. The Target USA Cup asks for a loan form for guests from outside its association, puts guests on the roster with unique jersey numbers, and freezes the roster at final team check-in. Seacoast United requires each guest's name and date of birth on the roster, medical consent forms, and rosters uploaded two weeks before the event. The USA Cup also limits a player to guesting for one team in addition to their primary team. The practical lesson is to read the tournament's guest rules the day you register and work backward from its deadlines, not the morning of the event, since a missing form can mean a player is not eligible to take the field no matter how good the arrangement sounded.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Assuming a guest player limit from a past tournament applies to this one, instead of checking its specific rules.
  • Contacting a player's family before their club, which can break association rules and creates an awkward situation for everyone.
  • Stacking a roster with guests to chase a bracket instead of filling a genuine, specific gap.
  • Letting a guest player's minutes quietly come out of your own rostered players' development time, or using the weekend average to excuse a bad game.
  • Leaving a guest player's role unclear until they are already standing on the sideline at kickoff.
  • Waiting until the week of the tournament to start release forms and roster paperwork.
