In Game Coaching: What to Say From the Sideline, and When to Stay Quiet

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 23, 2026 · 5 min read

Our parent sideline guide asks parents to cheer instead of coach during games. This is the other half of that same deal. If a coach is calling out every touch a player takes, asking parents to stay quiet is a little rich. Here is what a coach's own voice should actually be doing during play, and how often the right answer is nothing at all.

Who this guide is for

Coaches thinking about their own sideline voice during games, as the companion piece to our guide for parents on the same topic.

The short version
  • Do not joystick your players, calling out every pass, dribble and shot as if you were holding a controller.
  • Coach the players off the ball more than the one who has it, and do it sparingly.
  • Save detailed corrections for the next stoppage, halftime, or practice, not the middle of a play.
  • Catch them being good, loudly and specifically, more often than you correct them.
  • Sit down. Standing and pacing feeds the urge to narrate every second of the game.

Why it mattersDecisions are the skill you are actually teaching

Researcher Rianne Kannekens' work on player development found that the players who go the farthest are the ones who developed strong decision making skills early, not the ones who were told the right answer the most often. Trevor Brooking, former technical director of England's Football Association, describes the shift this way: "the old vision of the coach shouting do this or do that has gone," in favor of letting players discover the answer themselves.

Landon Donovan has made a similar point about American development specifically, noting that some players have been "told their whole life exactly what to do in what situation," instead of learning to read the game on their own. His observation about the countries that produce the best players is blunt: they let youth players "be decision makers, and they're able to do it without coaches and parents yelling constantly at them on the sidelines."

What not to sayDo not joystick your players

One coaching guide describes the pattern to avoid clearly: coaches who "tell their players when to pass, who to pass to, when to dribble, when to shoot, when to tackle" end up controlling the game like a joystick rather than coaching it, and the result "robs the enjoyment of the sport" while earning little respect from the players themselves.

A simple test for whether you are about to cross that line: if the instruction you are about to shout has a verb in it, telling a specific player to do a specific thing right now, it is probably an instruction that belongs to the player, not to you. Ask yourself whether you would stand over a student's desk narrating every answer during a math test. A soccer game, for the player on the ball, is closer to that test than it is to a rehearsal.

What to say insteadA few things worth your voice

Silence is not the same as having nothing useful to offer. A few things are genuinely worth saying out loud during play.

  • Coach the players without the ball. A reminder to a defender to step up, a midfielder to check their shoulder, or a fullback to get forward is far less disruptive than coaching the ball carrier, and it still shapes the game. Use it sparingly rather than constantly.
  • Keep cues to the ball carrier short and open ended. Something like "heads up" invites a player to look for their own options rather than handing them the answer.
  • Catch them being good, loudly. A specific, enthusiastic "great vision, way to get your head up" after a good decision teaches at least as much as any correction, and it costs you nothing.
  • Save the detailed feedback for after the play. A note like "you missed a chance to go forward because your body was not open when you received it" is genuinely useful, delivered at the next stoppage or at halftime rather than shouted in the middle of play when the player cannot act on it anyway.

A physical trickSit down

One piece of practical advice worth taking literally: sit during the game instead of pacing the touchline. Standing and moving with the play feeds the urge to narrate every moment of it. Sitting keeps you calmer, makes the occasional comment you do make land with more weight, and models exactly the composure you are hoping to see from your players when things go wrong on the field.

HalftimeLet players talk first

Before giving your own halftime analysis, ask the players what they think is working and what needs to change, then fold your own observations in around theirs. It is a small habit that turns halftime into something players participate in rather than something that happens to them, and it fits naturally alongside the individual check ins described in our player development guide.

On mistakesLet mistakes stay the currency of learning

A player who is corrected loudly in front of the field the moment they make a mistake tends to remember the embarrassment more than the lesson. The same correction, delivered privately at the next stoppage or after the game, tends to actually change something, the same private over public principle covered in our guide on discipline and effort. Mistakes made in real time, under real pressure, are how players actually learn the game. Narrating every one of them as it happens does not speed that process up. It mostly just adds noise.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Coaching every touch of the ball carrier, which leaves no room for the player to actually decide anything.
  • Correcting mistakes loudly, in the moment, instead of privately at the next natural break.
  • Pacing the touchline in a way that makes constant commentary feel almost automatic.
  • Asking parents to stay quiet while coaching every play yourself, which undercuts the standard before you have even set it.
  • Doing all the talking at halftime, instead of asking players what they noticed first.
