In Game Coaching: What to Say From the Sideline, and When to Stay Quiet

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of MatchTap · September 23, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 10 min read

Our parent sideline guide asks parents to cheer instead of coach during games. This is the other half of that same deal. If a coach is calling out every touch a player takes, asking parents to stay quiet is a little rich. Here is what a coach's own voice should be doing during play, and how to tell when speaking up helps and when it just adds noise.

Who this guide is for

Coaches thinking about their own sideline voice during games, as the companion piece to our guide for parents on the same topic.

The short version
  • Do not joystick your players, calling out every pass, dribble and shot as if you were holding a controller.
  • Coach the players off the ball more than the one who has it, and do it sparingly.
  • Save detailed corrections for the next stoppage, halftime, or practice, not the middle of a play.
  • Catch them being good, specifically and with energy, more often than you correct them.
  • Sit down if you can. It makes constant narration harder.
  • Do not go silent either. The goal is less noise, not no coaching.

Why it mattersDecisions are the skill you are actually teaching

A 2010 University of Groningen study by Rianne Kannekens surveyed 389 talented players aged 14 to 18 and 37 national youth team players. Scores on choosing position and decision making were strongly related to later playing level: a high score correctly predicted, in 70 percent of cases, whether an 18 year old would become a professional. It was a questionnaire study, so it shows that decision making goes with success, not that coaching style causes it, and it did not test how coaches talk. It also does not mean you can spot future professionals at U10. It is still a good reason to treat decisions as the thing you are teaching.

On the coaching side, a 2015 Deseret News column quotes England's Trevor Brooking describing how Manchester United now works with young players, who are meant to discover things for themselves: "The old vision of the coach shouting do this or do that has gone."

Landon Donovan made a similar point about American development in an interview with Sports Illustrated's Grant Wahl, as quoted by Soccer America. He said you can tell some American players have been "told their whole life exactly what to do in what situation," and that the best countries focus on "Allowing their youth players to be decision-makers, and they're able to do it without coaches and parents yelling constantly," as he put it.

What not to sayDo not joystick your players

SoccerDrive's coaching course describes the pattern to avoid: joystick coaches "tell their players when to pass, who to pass to, when to dribble, when to shoot, when to tackle," and in its words they "rob the enjoyment of the sport, and are not respected by anyone." That is the author's blunt opinion, but the picture is familiar to anyone who has stood on a sideline.

A simple test comes from a Soccer America article written for parents that works just as well for coaches: would you shout this at a child in a classroom, or at one practicing piano? If the instruction you are about to call out is a command with a verb in it, aimed at a specific player on the ball, it is probably a decision that belongs to the player.

The opposite mistake is just as real. The SoccerDrive author describes a stretch of staying silent until halftime, believing that was the goal, and now sees it as holding players back. Quiet is a tool, not a philosophy.

What to say insteadA few things worth your voice

Silence is not the same as having nothing useful to offer. A few things are genuinely worth saying out loud during play.

  • Coach the players without the ball. A reminder to a defender to step up, a midfielder to check their shoulder, or a fullback to get forward is far less disruptive than coaching the ball carrier, and it still shapes the game. Use it sparingly rather than constantly.
  • Keep cues to the ball carrier short and open ended. Something like "heads up" invites a player to look for their own options rather than handing them the answer.
  • Catch them being good, with energy. A specific "great vision, way to get your head up" after a good decision teaches at least as much as a correction. Praise the decision rather than the result, and remember that some quieter players prefer a nod or a thumbs up to a shout, which our guide on quiet or anxious players covers.
  • Give feedback at the least disruptive moment. SoccerDrive suggests brief feedback right after a pass, shot or dribble, or at the next stoppage, whichever interrupts the player the least. Longer notes such as "you missed a chance to go forward because your body was not open when you received it" belong at halftime or at practice.

PracticalAdjust for age

This part is general coaching judgment rather than something from the sources above. With the youngest players, roughly U6 to U8, a little simple, friendly direction is normal and fine, because many of them are still learning what the game is. As players get older, about U10 and up, shift toward short open cues, praise for decisions, and letting them solve problems. If your team has a mix of ages or experience, coach the group you have, not the one in a book.

A physical trickSit down if you can

Soccer America's article suggests that coaches sit during games, because it makes shouting harder and keeps you from showing nervous energy. If your league or field makes that unrealistic, the idea still works: pick one spot, stay out of the pacing, and let the game happen in front of you. Instead of calling out each mistake, the article suggests noting it, perhaps with an assistant, and talking about it at halftime or practice. A small notebook works, and so does the Add note button in MatchTap, covered at the end of this guide.

HalftimeLet players talk first

SoccerDrive gives the players about five minutes to answer two questions before sharing the author's own view, then combines their answers with those thoughts to plan the second half. A simple version of that structure:

  1. What did we do well? There is almost always something true to point to, whatever the score, and starting there keeps the conversation constructive from the first sentence.
  2. What is the one main thing we need to change in the second half? This is the same question as "what did we do wrong," asked from a constructive angle, so there is no need to ask both. Ask for one thing, not a list.

Asking the team to name the problem out loud, rather than announcing your own diagnosis, gives them practice at noticing what is not working and adjusting, which is part of reading the game. Once players have had the first word, fold your own observations in around theirs. Keep it short, since halftime is limited and players also need water and a rest. With the clock and water breaks, many coaches will have less than five minutes, so two or three minutes is a workable floor. With very young players, one concrete question ("what was your favorite thing we did?") works better than two abstract ones. The habit also fits naturally alongside the individual check ins described in our player development guide.

On mistakesLet mistakes stay the currency of learning

Correcting a player loudly in front of teammates and parents the moment they make a mistake is likely to feel embarrassing and rarely helps them act on it right then, since they are in the middle of play. A short, calm word at the next stoppage, or a longer one after the game, gives the correction a better chance to land. Private here means what it means in our guide on discipline and effort: away from teammates' ears, but in view of other adults.

Mistakes made in real time, under real pressure, are how players learn the game. Narrating every one as it happens does not speed that up. It mostly adds noise.

Short cues for the hard moments

After a goal against or a visible mistake, a few steady phrases are enough: "next play," "good try, go again," "reset, we are okay." Say them in your normal voice, and pick two or three you can say without thinking, so the same words are not a surprise to the players when they hear them.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Coaching every touch of the ball carrier, which leaves no room for the player to actually decide anything.
  • Correcting mistakes loudly, in the moment, instead of at the next natural break.
  • Pacing the touchline in a way that makes constant commentary feel almost automatic.
  • Asking parents to stay quiet while coaching every play yourself, which undercuts the standard before you have even set it.
  • Doing all the talking at halftime, instead of asking players what they noticed first.
  • Going completely silent, which leaves players without the praise and short cues that help them.

Ask your AI assistant coachBuild your sideline phrases, and plan both halftimes before kickoff

Deciding what is worth saying out loud is easier to plan calmly beforehand than to figure out mid game. An AI assistant that knows your coaching priorities from your coaching profile can help you build a short list of go to phrases, and prepare for both kinds of halftime before the game starts, since a team that is comfortably ahead needs a different conversation than one that is down and frustrated. It will not know how you naturally talk unless you show it, so paste in a few things you really say.

Do not paste real player names or identifying details about a child into a general AI tool. Describe situations in general terms instead.

"I coach a [age group] team. My biggest sideline priorities this season are [one or two things, such as players checking their shoulder or trying a pass instead of always dribbling]. Here are a few things I really say on the sideline, with any child's name replaced by "Player": [paste two or three examples]. Give me five short, open ended things I could say to coach players off the ball, and five short, specific praise phrases for when a player makes a good decision, in a voice that sounds like mine, not scripted lines." Use this before a game, not during one
"I coach a [age group] team. Using a what did we do well, what is the one main thing to change structure, give me three or four open ended halftime questions for a game where my team is behind and the players are frustrated, plus a short, calm way to frame the second half, without making any promises about the result." Plan this before the game, then keep it on a card
"I coach a [age group] team. Using the same what did we do well, what is the one main thing to change structure, give me halftime questions for a game where my team is comfortably ahead, that keep the players focused on the habits that got them the lead rather than easing off because the score feels safe." Plan this before the game too

Practice saying the sideline phrases out loud once before game day. A phrase that reads naturally on a screen can still feel stiff coming out of your mouth on the sideline until you have said it a couple of times.
