Planning a Practice Session: Fitting Warm Up, Technical Work, and Small Sided Games Into One Hour

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 26, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 12 min read

Most coaches do not lose practice time to any one bad decision. They lose it in small pieces, five minutes setting up cones while players stand around, a long explanation before a drill anyone could have picked up in one demonstration, a line of six kids waiting for one ball. None of that shows up as a mistake in the moment. It just adds up to a session that feels rushed by the time the actual game at the end arrives. A little structure, decided before you ever get to the field, fixes almost all of it.

Who this guide is for

Coaches building a repeatable structure for a one hour practice, so warm up, technical work, and small sided games all fit without the session running long or feeling rushed. If you are new to coaching entirely, our first season guide covers the broader basics, and our formations by age guide covers what to actually coach once players are on the field. Our positions explained simply guide gives you the plain language version to actually say to players during a drill.

The short version
  • Start with the ball moving, not a line or a lap, from the very first minute.
  • Set up your grids and cones, and anchor any movable goals, before players arrive, not while they wait on you.
  • Match the session to the age. Younger players need shorter blocks and more play, and the one hour plan below is built for about U10 to U12.
  • Move from technical work to pressure to a real game, in that order, every session.
  • End with something that looks like the actual sport, a small sided game, not a drill.
  • Cut explanations short. A quick demonstration teaches faster than a long one ever will.

The frameworkTwo respected ways to structure it, and where they agree

U.S. Soccer's grassroots curriculum is built around a model called Play, Practice, Play. According to U.S. Soccer, the session opens with small sided, pickup style games that the players lead, while the coach creates a "safe, engaging and fun" environment, observes, and guides players toward their own solutions rather than telling them what to do. The middle is targeted practice: repetition, activities that resemble the game, decisions to make, and room for creative problem solving. The session closes with an uninterrupted game that mirrors the real one, where the coach observes, ideally with minimal talk, to see whether players can carry out the session's goal. Coach Toph, a youth coach who writes about the model on Substack, adds a useful caution that it is meant to be flexible, calling it "a conceptual tool meant to help players play and learn more." That author lists no formal coaching credentials, so we use the line for the idea and not as an authority.

A more traditional structure, shown in CoachTube's guide for youth coaches, moves the other direction: individual technique first with no defensive pressure, then the same skill applied under pressure, starting with light resistance and building up, then small sided games with constraints, and a short recap. Both approaches agree on far more than they disagree. Both keep players touching the ball constantly instead of waiting in line, both limit coach talk, and both end the session with something that actually resembles the real game. Which one you pick matters less than making sure your own session reflects one of them on purpose, instead of drifting into whatever feels easiest to plan that week.

TemplateStart from your age group

The one hour table below is adapted from iCoachFootball's 60 minute plan, which is written for U10 to U12 players. We added a short recap at the end. Treat the minutes as a starting point to adjust for your own team, not a rule to follow to the second.

TimeWhat is happening
0 to 10 min Arrival and warm up as a small sided game, 1v1 or 2v2, so players are touching the ball and moving from the moment they step on the field, not standing in a line stretching.
10 to 25 min Technical work on one specific skill, in a low pressure setting at first, then with some light competition added once the group looks comfortable.
25 to 40 min The same skill applied under real pressure, an opposed, game like scenario such as a 3v2 overload, where players have to use the skill to solve an actual problem.
40 to 55 min A full small sided game, with a light coaching constraint tied to the day's theme if it fits naturally, otherwise just let them play.
55 to 60 min A short water break and a quick, two sentence recap of one thing that went well, not a long postmortem.

Younger and older players need a different shape

Younger players cannot hold one activity for fifteen or twenty minutes, so the session has to be cut into shorter blocks with more play. Coach Toph's Play, Practice, Play shapes for each age are a good starting point, and Coach Toph suggests 8 to 10 minute blocks for the youngest group. The block lengths in the table are our own rough suggestions, not an official standard.

AgeShape of the sessionBlock length
U6 to U8Play, Practice, Play, Practice, PlayAbout 6 to 8 minutes each
U9 to U10Play, Practice, Play, sometimes Play, Practice, Practice, PlayRoughly 10 to 12 minutes per activity
U10 to U12The one hour table above10 to 20 minutes
U12 and upAn organized warm up first, then Play, Practice, PlayLonger blocks, adjusted to the group

U.S. Soccer's U9 to U10 coaching page points the same direction, with training games up to 7v7, and one of its practice clips aims, in its words, to "finish with what they're going to see on the weekend."

A warm up that helps prevent injuries

The 10 minute warm up can do more than get kids moving. In a cluster randomized trial of children on under 9, under 11 and under 13 teams (Rossler et al., Sports Medicine), teams that used the full FIFA 11+ Kids warm up had 48 percent fewer injuries than teams that warmed up as usual, and 74 percent fewer severe injuries. The trial tested the whole program, so follow the published routine rather than picking a few exercises.

If weather, roster size, or field space forces a last minute change, the AI prompts near the end of this guide can rescale the plan in about a minute instead of improvising cold in front of the team.

Attention spanMatch activity length to age, not ambition

A common rule of thumb for how long any single activity should run before a young player's focus starts to drift is a child's age in years plus one minute. For six year olds that means roughly six to seven minutes, for eight year olds about eight to ten, and for ten year olds ten to twelve. We could not find a research source for the rule itself, so treat it as a starting point and watch your players. When kids start wandering, chatting, or stopping mid drill, the activity has run long, even if the drill itself is a good one.

Before they arriveDo your setup before practice starts, not during it

Every minute spent setting up cones or explaining a drill at length comes directly out of the time your players get to play. iCoachFootball puts the math plainly: spend 15 minutes setting up cones and 10 minutes lecturing, and you have "wasted nearly half your development time." In a one hour session, 25 minutes is about 42 percent. Show up with your grids marked and your equipment staged before the first player arrives, and save the explaining for a thirty second demonstration rather than a speech.

Before the first ballSafety checks that take two minutes

  • Anchor every movable goal. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission says movable soccer goals must always be anchored properly, and that net pegs should not be used as anchors because they only secure the net. It also says never to allow anyone to climb on or hang from the net or goal frame, and that only trained, authorized people should move goals.
  • Warm up properly. See the 11+ Kids note above.
  • Plan the water break. It is built into the template, and in hot weather make it more frequent.
  • Walk the field first for holes, glass, and anything a kid could trip on.

What actually wastes timeLines, laps, and lectures

Skye Eddy of Soccer Parenting sums up what to avoid with a line she calls "the standard" answer for recreational coaches: "no lines, no laps, no lectures." She adds that stretching and sprint drills are poorly suited to 8 year olds, and recommends small sided games that progress from 1v1 to 4v4 instead. A line of players waiting for one ball is time nobody on that line is developing. A lap around the field does very little for a young player's coordination or touch on the ball, which is what actually needs the repetition. And a long explanation loses a group of kids well before it finishes, no matter how useful the point being made actually is. Our guide on why practice should feel like a game goes deeper on the research behind this, including why a felt purpose matters as much as the activity itself.

ExampleWhat this looks like with a real theme

Say the theme for the week is first touch with a group of ten year olds. Warm up is a simple 2v2 in a tight grid, nothing structured yet, just movement and touches. Technical work is receiving the ball on the half turn, first alone against a wall or a partner's throw, then with a passive defender jogging in to add just enough pressure to matter. The pressure phase becomes a 3v2 to a small goal, where a clean first touch is what actually creates the scoring chance, so the skill is doing real work rather than being practiced in isolation. The session closes with a regular small sided game, no constraint needed, since the whole point was to let that first touch show up naturally once the pressure is real.

With six year olds the same theme looks different. Everyone has a ball and dribbles in open space as the warm up. Then a short block, about six to eight minutes, of stopping a rolled ball with the sole of the foot in a small grid, then a 3v3 game to small goals, another short block of stopping the ball and moving off with it, and a last game. This is our own example, not a published session plan.

When the numbers are offToo many players, too few players

  • Too many players for one game: run two smaller games side by side instead of one big one with a bench, so nobody waits in a line.
  • Too few players: use 2v2, 3v1 with a neutral player, or a possession game with a target.
  • A parent helper: if your club allows it and the helper has passed your club's background check and SafeSport training, they can run a grid in plain view of other adults. Never leave a helper alone with one child.

Did it work?A two minute check after practice

  • Did every player touch the ball almost the whole time, and did anyone stand in a line for more than a few seconds?
  • Did you ever talk for longer than about thirty seconds in one go?
  • Did the closing game show the skill you worked on?
  • Which block ran too long, and which one was too short?

These checks are our own coaching suggestions, not published standards. Write down the answers in a sentence or two and use them to plan the next session.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Starting with a lecture or a lap instead of the ball moving in the first minute.
  • Setting up equipment while players wait, instead of having it ready before anyone arrives.
  • Leaving a movable goal unanchored, or letting kids hang on it.
  • Using the one hour plan for every age, including six year olds who cannot hold fifteen minute blocks.
  • Running one activity far longer than a young player's actual attention span for it.
  • Skipping straight from a drill to a full game, with no pressure phase in between to bridge the two.
  • Ending practice with more technical work instead of something that actually looks like the sport.
  • Explaining a drill at length instead of showing it once and letting players learn by doing it.

Ask your AI assistant coachBuild the plan, check it, then rescale it

An AI assistant that already knows your age group and roster size from your coaching profile can fill in the content for a specific practice in a minute. Two cautions. It can invent drills that need more equipment, space, or players than you have, and it can run blocks far too long for young players, so read the plan before you use it. Our guide to using AI as a coach covers the basics if you are new to this.

"Using a warm up, technical work, pressure phase, small sided game, and recap structure, build me a practice plan for [age group] [format] with [number] players and a theme of [skill, such as first touch or shielding]. Give me one specific activity for each phase, a short equipment list, and the minutes for each block." Prompt 1: build the plan, the night before or morning of practice
"Check this plan for [age group] players: [paste the plan]. Flag any block longer than a typical attention span for this age, any activity where players would wait in a line of more than three, any drill that needs more players, space, or equipment than I have ([describe]), and anything that involves moving or climbing on movable goals." Prompt 2: have it check its own plan
"My practice today is only [length] minutes, and I have [number] players instead of [usual number]. Rescale the timing of each phase and adjust the activities to fit, keeping the same theme of [skill]." Prompt 3: rescale for a shorter slot or a different roster

Keep players anonymous in anything you paste, and do not rely on the AI for facts like safety rules or statistics. Check those against the sources linked below.
