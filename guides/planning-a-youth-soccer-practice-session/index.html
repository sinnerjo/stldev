Planning a Practice Session: Fitting Warm Up, Technical Work, and Small Sided Games Into One Hour

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 26, 2026 · 7 min read

Most coaches do not lose practice time to any one bad decision. They lose it in small pieces, five minutes setting up cones while players stand around, a long explanation before a drill anyone could have picked up in one demonstration, a line of six kids waiting for one ball. None of that shows up as a mistake in the moment. It just adds up to a session that feels rushed by the time the actual game at the end arrives. A little structure, decided before you ever get to the field, fixes almost all of it.

Who this guide is for

Coaches building a repeatable structure for a one hour practice, so warm up, technical work, and small sided games all fit without the session running long or feeling rushed. If you are new to coaching entirely, our first season guide covers the broader basics, and our formations by age guide covers what to actually coach once players are on the field.

The short version
  • Start with the ball moving, not a line or a lap, from the very first minute.
  • Set up your grids and cones before players arrive, not while they wait on you.
  • Match activity length to attention span, which is shorter than most coaches assume for younger ages.
  • Move from technical work to pressure to a real game, in that order, every session.
  • End with something that looks like the actual sport, a small sided game, not a drill.
  • Cut explanations short. A quick demonstration teaches faster than a long one ever will.

The frameworkTwo respected ways to structure it, and where they agree

U.S. Soccer's own grassroots curriculum is built around a model called Play, Practice, Play. Rather than starting with drills, the session opens with a small sided game that the players largely run themselves, while the coach mostly observes. The middle of the session becomes targeted practice built around whatever the coach actually saw players struggle with in that opening game, and the session closes with another small sided game where players apply what they just worked on. As the model's own description puts it, the goal is not a rigid formula to repeat every time, but "a conceptual tool" aimed at one thing, keeping players moving and engaged instead of standing in lines.

A more traditional structure, used widely across youth coaching guides, moves the other direction: technical fundamentals first, in a low pressure setting, then the same skill applied under some opposition, then a full small sided game that puts it all together. Both approaches agree on far more than they disagree. Neither one wants a long lecture up front, both want players touching the ball constantly rather than waiting in line, and both end the session with something that actually resembles the real game. Which one you pick matters less than making sure your own hour actually reflects one of them on purpose, instead of drifting into whatever feels easiest to plan that week.

TemplateA one hour structure you can start from

Blending both approaches into a simple hour looks something like this. Treat the minutes as a starting point to adjust for your own team's age and attention span, not a rule to follow to the second.

TimeWhat is happening
0 to 10 min Arrival and warm up as a small sided game, 1v1 or 2v2, so players are touching the ball and moving from the moment they step on the field, not standing in a line stretching.
10 to 25 min Technical work on one specific skill, in a low pressure setting at first, then with some light competition added once the group looks comfortable.
25 to 45 min The same skill applied under real pressure, an opposed, game like scenario such as a small overload, where players have to use the skill to solve an actual problem.
45 to 58 min A full small sided game, with a light coaching constraint tied to the day's theme if it fits naturally, otherwise just let them play.
58 to 60 min A short water break and a quick, two sentence recap of one thing that went well, not a long postmortem.

Attention spanMatch activity length to age, not ambition

A useful rule of thumb for how long any single activity should run before a young player's focus genuinely starts to drift, is a child's age in years plus one minute. For a group of six year olds, that means an individual activity holding real attention for roughly six to seven minutes before it is time to switch to something new, even if the drill itself is a good one. For eight year olds, that is closer to eight to ten minutes, and for ten year olds, ten to twelve. The broader principle worth remembering below that age range is that frequency beats length, more, shorter practices generally beat fewer, longer ones for actual development, which is worth knowing before assuming a longer practice is automatically a better one.

Before they arriveDo your setup before practice starts, not during it

Most youth coaches only get sixty to ninety minutes of field time in a week, and every minute spent setting up cones or explaining a drill at length is a minute that comes directly out of that budget. One coaching guide puts the math on this plainly: spend fifteen minutes setting up cones and ten minutes lecturing, and you have already lost nearly a third of the entire session before a single ball has been kicked with intent. Show up with your grids marked and your equipment staged before the first player arrives, and save the explaining for a thirty second demonstration rather than a speech.

What actually wastes timeLines, laps, and lectures

One long running piece of advice aimed specifically at volunteer coaches sums up what to avoid in three words: "no lines, no laps, no lectures." A line of players waiting for one ball is time nobody on that line is actually developing. A lap around the field does very little for a young player's coordination or touch on the ball, which is what actually needs the repetition. And a long explanation loses a group of kids well before it finishes, no matter how useful the point being made actually is. Almost everything about a well run hour comes down to eliminating those three things wherever they show up in your plan.

ExampleWhat this looks like with a real theme

Say the theme for the week is first touch. Warm up is a simple 2v2 in a tight grid, nothing structured yet, just movement and touches. Technical work is receiving the ball on the half turn, first alone against a wall or a partner's throw, then with a passive defender jogging in to add just enough pressure to matter. The pressure phase becomes a 3v2 to a small goal, where a clean first touch is what actually creates the scoring chance, so the skill is doing real work rather than being practiced in isolation. The session closes with a regular small sided game, no constraint needed, since the whole point was to let that first touch show up naturally once the pressure is real.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Starting with a lecture or a lap instead of the ball moving in the first minute.
  • Setting up equipment while players wait, instead of having it ready before anyone arrives.
  • Running one activity far longer than a young player's actual attention span for it.
  • Skipping straight from a drill to a full game, with no pressure phase in between to bridge the two.
  • Ending practice with more technical work instead of something that actually looks like the sport.
  • Explaining a drill at length instead of showing it once and letting players learn by doing it.
