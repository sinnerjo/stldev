Recognizing Burnout in a Young Athlete: The Signs Before They Quit

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 24, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 13 min read

By the time a player tells you they want to quit, burnout has usually been building for months. The behavior changes show up earlier, if you know what you are looking at. This guide covers what real sport psychology research says burnout actually is, what it looks like before a kid walks away, what causes it, and what a coach or parent can actually do about it.

Who this guide is for

Coaches and parents who have noticed a player pulling away from a sport they used to love, and want to know if what they are seeing is ordinary tiredness, a real warning sign, or something that deserves more than a coaching fix. A coach is not a clinician. This guide helps you notice, ask, and adjust, and it tells you when to hand the situation to a parent and a pediatrician.

The short version
  • Burnout has three parts: exhaustion, a feeling that effort is not paying off, and losing the value the sport used to have.
  • The signs are not specific. The American Academy of Pediatrics says they often overlap with other problems, so treat them as a reason to ask, not a diagnosis.
  • Risk goes up with early single sport focus, little say in decisions, a winning first climate, and no real time off.
  • What helps: real choices for the player, praise for effort, other sports, scheduled breaks, and a calm private check in with a parent in the loop.
  • If the change reaches beyond soccer, or a child mentions self harm, that goes to a parent the same day and to a pediatrician, with 988 and 911 as your plan for a crisis.

Before the signsThe real dropout numbers, not the famous one

You have probably heard that 70 percent of kids quit organized sports by age 13. We went looking for where that number comes from, and it does not hold up well. A USA TODAY story, carried on Tucson.com, reports that Joseph Janosky of Lasell University found the claim may not have a clear primary source, and calls the pattern citation drift. Marty Fox of the Aspen Institute argued the better framing is that kids quit most sports by 13. A physician quoted in the story cites CDC Youth Risk Behavior Survey data showing organized sports participation among U.S. high school students has stayed above 50 percent since 2000, apart from the COVID years, and Tom Farrey of Project Play puts the average age at which kids quit a sport at about 12.

The more careful numbers come from the Monitoring the Future survey, as analyzed in a report from D'Youville University's Center for Research on Physical Activity, Sports and Health. Using surveys from roughly 2006 to 2012, it found the share of students playing a given sport was on average 32 percent lower in 12th grade than in 8th grade. For soccer it was 31 percent lower for boys and 53 percent lower for girls. Two cautions apply. These compare different groups of students, not individual kids followed over time, the data are more than a decade old, and a drop in one sport does not mean a child left sport altogether, since many switch. Even so, the number is not nothing, and it is not evenly spread between boys and girls.

The frameworkWhat burnout actually is, in three parts

The most used model in sport psychology research comes from Thomas Raedeke and Alan Smith, and it is measured with a tool called the Athlete Burnout Questionnaire. It breaks burnout into three parts: emotional and physical exhaustion, a reduced sense of accomplishment, and devaluation of the sport. Translated out of research language, that is a player who is worn out, a player who no longer feels like their effort is getting them anywhere, and a player who has quietly stopped caring about the thing they used to care about. Real burnout usually involves some mix of all three, not just one bad week of tiredness.

What to watch forThe behavior signs before a kid quits outright

The American Academy of Pediatrics, in its 2024 clinical report on overuse injuries, overtraining, and burnout in young athletes, lists these possible signs: fatigue, depressive symptoms, loss of interest or motivation, sleep disturbances, irritability, anxiety symptoms, poor concentration, unexplained musculoskeletal complaints, weight changes, decreased performance, and decreased enjoyment of sports. The report is also clear that the symptoms are often not specific to burnout. That matters. This list is a reason to look closer and ask a question, not a way to diagnose anyone. On a soccer field it tends to look like this:

  • Dread before practice or games, not simple reluctance to leave the couch, but a pattern of complaints, stalling, or a mood shift specifically tied to soccer.
  • Vague physical complaints with no clear cause, a sore leg or a stomach ache that never quite becomes a diagnosable injury and tends to show up before practice more than during it.
  • Irritability that is specific to the sport, a kid who is otherwise fine at school or at home but short tempered around anything soccer related.
  • Performance dropping despite continued or increased effort. The AAP says athletes with "decreased performance despite adequate recovery, nutrition, and hydration may require further evaluation for burnout." That evaluation belongs to a clinician, not the sideline. The player is still showing up and trying, and it is not working.
  • Losing interest in the parts of the sport they used to love, skipping the extra reps they used to ask for, no longer wanting to watch soccer at home, going quiet about a team they used to talk about constantly.

Age changes how it looks

This part is our coaching rule of thumb, not a research finding. Younger players, roughly under 10, rarely say they are burned out. It tends to show up as stomach aches, clinginess, or a sudden refusal to go, and the best first step is a word with a parent. Teenagers can usually tell you directly, but they may hide it, and school load, other sports, and friends are often part of the story.

A related but different thingBurnout is not the same as overtraining syndrome

These two get used interchangeably, and they are related but not identical. The AAP describes overtraining syndrome as "an accumulation of stress" that results in "a persistent performance decrease" and "requires weeks or months of rest to restore," a mostly physical and performance based problem. Burnout is more about motivation and emotion than the body's ability to recover. The AAP places them on the same spectrum, stating plainly that "burnout is part of a spectrum that includes overreaching and overtraining syndrome." In practice, a player can be burned out without being physically overtrained, and a player recovering from overtraining syndrome is also at real risk of becoming burned out along the way.

What actually causes itThe research backed risk factors

One honest caution before the list. Most of this research is observational, meaning it finds associations. It does not prove that any one factor causes burnout in an individual child.

  • Specializing in one sport too early. A 2016 American Academy of Pediatrics report, as summarized by MinnPost, lists burnout, anxiety, depression, and attrition as increased in early specializers. The AAP's 2024 clinical report covers burnout too, and AAP reports can lapse, so check the AAP site for the current status of the 2016 one. A 2020 systematic review and meta analysis by Giusti and colleagues pooled 8 studies covering 1,429 adolescent athletes and found specializers scored higher than samplers on all three burnout dimensions, with the biggest gap in reduced sense of accomplishment. The comparison group was small, though, only 58 samplers, so treat it as an association.
  • Low autonomy. The AAP 2024 report says young people who "primarily identify themselves as an athlete" or who feel they have "limited control over their sports decisions" are at higher risk of burnout.
  • A climate built around winning instead of improving. In a 2020 East Carolina University master's thesis by Jordan Barbee, advised by Thomas Raedeke, a survey of 150 high school athletes found that coach and peer "mastery" climates, focused on effort and improvement, were linked to lower burnout and higher engagement. A parent mastery climate was not linked to lower burnout. It is a small, survey based study, so it shows a link and not a cause.
  • Too many structured hours with no real rest. The National Athletic Trainers' Association recommends that athletes "not participate in organized sport and/or activity more hours per week than their age," that they have "a minimum of two days off per week" from organized training and competition, and that they not play a single sport more than eight months a year.

What to actually doWhat the research says helps

  • Give the player real say in their own soccer decisions. The AAP's recommendation is to "encourage athletic autonomy and intrinsic motivation, measure success on participation and effort." Our guide on why practice should feel like a game covers the autonomy and intrinsic motivation research in more depth, and how it should change what a practice looks like.
  • Talk and coach in terms of effort and improvement, not just results. This has the clearest link to lower burnout in the research we found, though it is an association and not a guarantee.
  • Support more than one sport. The 2016 AAP report, as summarized by MinnPost, advises delaying specialization in a single sport until about age 15 to 16 to minimize risks of overuse injury, and NATA says to delay specializing as long as possible.
  • Build in real time off. The AAP 2024 report recommends at least 2 to 3 months away from any specific sport during the year, which can be split into 1 month blocks, plus at least 1 to 2 days off per week from competition and sport specific training. Time away from soccer can still include other sports and unstructured play.
  • Lighten the load, not only the conversation. A skipped extra session, a choice of position, or a lower stakes few weeks costs a coach little and can matter a lot to a tired kid.

How to run the check in conversation

  • Tell a parent first. Say you have noticed a change and plan a short, friendly chat. It keeps them in the loop and avoids a surprise.
  • Keep it away from teammates but in view of other adults. The U.S. Center for SafeSport describes safer one on one interactions as "observable and interruptible," for example a corner of the field while another coach or parents are nearby, never a closed room or a private message thread.
  • Ask open questions and mostly listen. "What has soccer felt like lately?" works better than "Why don't you try harder?"
  • Offer one small change and agree to check back in about two weeks.
  • Set your plan before you talk. If a child mentions self harm, tell the parent the same day, give them 988 (call or text), and call 911 for immediate danger. Do not keep anything serious between the two of you.

A careful noteWhen it might be more than burnout

Sport burnout and a broader mental health concern can look similar from the sideline, and a coach cannot sort out which one is which. HealthyChildren.org, the American Academy of Pediatrics parent site, lists signs of depression that deserve more than a coaching adjustment: appearing sad, tearful, or irritable, loss of interest in friends, school, or activities, changes in appetite or weight, sleeping more or less than usual, trouble concentrating, and any thoughts of self harm or suicide.

If you see a pattern like that, or a change that reaches well beyond soccer, talk to a parent and encourage a visit to the child's pediatrician. If a child talks about hurting themselves, tell a parent the same day. In the U.S., anyone can call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. In an emergency, such as a child saying they intend to harm themselves or others, call 911 or go to the emergency department. This is not something to diagnose, manage, or keep private from the sideline.

If what a child says involves harm by an adult, including at home, do not route it only to the parent. Tell your club's safeguarding contact or the U.S. Center for SafeSport, and follow your state's mandatory reporting rules.

Ask your AI assistant coachPrepare for a check in conversation, not a diagnosis

Noticing dread before practice or a drop in enjoyment is the easy part. Finding calm, open ended questions that do not feel like an interrogation is harder in the moment. An AI assistant can help you prepare, as long as you describe the player in general terms and treat its output as drafting help. It cannot tell you whether a child is burned out or depressed, and it can invent studies and statistics, so check any claim it makes before you repeat it. If you are new to this, start with our beginner guide to using Claude as a soccer coach.

"I coach [age group] [format]. One of my players seems less interested in soccer lately, showing some general signs like low energy and less enthusiasm than before, but nothing I would call a mental health concern. Help me write four or five open ended questions I could ask this player in a corner of the field, in view of other adults, in a tone that feels like I am checking in, not confronting them." Prompt 1: prepare the questions
"Here are the questions you suggested: [paste them]. Act as a skeptical youth sports psychologist. Which of these might make a kid feel interrogated or pressured, and which might lead them to a particular answer? List the problems only. I already have a plan if a child says something serious: tell the parent the same day, give 988, and call 911 for immediate danger." Prompt 2: pressure test them
"Help me write a short, calm message to a parent. I want to say I have noticed a change in their child's enthusiasm at practice, that I plan to have a friendly chat, and that I would like to hear what they are seeing. No diagnosis, no blame." Prompt 3: bring the parent in first

Describe the player only in general terms, never by name or with identifying detail. These prompts are for preparing your own words, not for diagnosing anyone. If what you are seeing looks like more than ordinary burnout, that goes to a parent and a pediatrician, never to an AI tool.
