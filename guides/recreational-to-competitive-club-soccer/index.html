Moving From Recreational to Competitive Club Soccer: What Actually Changes

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 23, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 11 min read

A parent asks a rec coach some version of the same question every season: is my kid good enough to play club? The honest answer usually has less to do with talent than with four things that are easy to underestimate ahead of time: cost, time, how playing time works, and whether the player still loves the game once the answer is yes.

Who this guide is for

Families weighing a move from recreational to club soccer, and the coaches, recreational or club, who end up in that conversation with them.

The short version
  • Cost jumps a lot, from a couple hundred dollars a season to well over a thousand a year, and travel can add more.
  • Time commitment multiplies, from one practice and one game a week to several practices, tournaments, and travel.
  • Playing time can become earned, especially at 11v11, but a healthy floor should still exist.
  • Coaching gets more structured, though club coaches vary, so ask who the coach is and how they are trained.
  • Ask the player, and read the commitment terms before you sign anything.
  • There is no deadline. The right time to move is when a player is challenged and still having fun, and trying out is not a commitment.

MoneyThe cost difference is real

Published figures vary, and the sources below are clubs or club marketing sites, so treat them as ranges, not quotes. Play Club Soccer puts recreational leagues at about $50 to $400 a year and entry level competitive programs at around $1,500 to $3,500 a year, with elite national programs "often" past $20,000 once all expenses are included. Arkansas Rising lists $175 to $250 per season for recreational and $1,000 to $3,000 or more for club. San Francisco Youth Soccer says club play can "often exceed" $2,000 per year per child. Your own area may differ a lot, so ask the clubs you are considering for a written fee schedule.

Registration is often not the biggest piece. Play Club Soccer says travel is the main cost driver and over a season can add up to more than the base club fees. Uniforms and training gear add hundreds of dollars more.

None of that is a reason to assume club is out of reach. Many clubs offer scholarships, payment plans, or fee reductions for volunteering, and families can cut real costs by buying secondhand gear, carpooling to tournaments, and renting a home instead of booking hotels. It is also worth judging a program on its coaching and development, not its logo or ranking. Play Club Soccer says regional competitive clubs "frequently offer comparable coaching quality to national leagues at a fraction of the cost," though that is a club marketing site's claim, so check it by watching a practice.

ScheduleThe time commitment multiplies

A recreational season is usually one practice and one game a week for two or three months, mostly local. Competitive club soccer usually means multiple practices a week, weekend tournaments, and travel that can run regional or even national depending on the level, often across most of the year rather than one season. This is worth sitting with honestly before committing, since it affects the whole family's schedule, not just the player's.

On the fieldPlaying time can be earned, but should not disappear

This is the change families feel most directly. At the recreational level, and through 9v9 in club as well, our own equal playing time guide holds that minutes should stay close to equal regardless of skill. At 11v11 and older, more competitive levels where roster spots are earned through tryouts, roles and starting spots can be earned, and minutes become tied to performance and role rather than an automatic rotation.

That said, our standard still applies even here: no healthy, available player should get less than half a game at any age, including 11v11 club soccer. Earned does not have to mean unpredictable or all or nothing. San Francisco Youth Soccer suggests asking whether a minimum playing time is expected. A club whose players regularly sit an entire game on the bench is a fit question worth raising before signing, not after. If you are already past that point, our guide on talking to a coach about playing time covers the conversation itself, and how differently it goes at this level.

On the sidelineCoaching gets more structured

Recreational teams are usually organized by age and neighborhood, coached by a volunteer parent focused on basic skills and enjoying the game. Competitive teams are built through tryouts, and Arkansas Rising describes club coaches as experienced and trained in player development and tactics. That is not universal. Club coaches range from licensed, paid professionals to volunteers, so ask who will actually coach your child and how they are trained. Our guide on coaching licenses explains what the common ones mean. Names for levels (select, travel, premier, and so on) also vary by state, so ask each club what its level means.

Structure can genuinely accelerate a player's growth. It can also feel like a big shift for a family used to a more relaxed, fun first environment, which is exactly why it is worth watching a practice or two before committing rather than judging a club by its website alone.

TimingThere is no deadline, only fit

San Francisco Youth Soccer's tryout guidance puts it plainly: "Players who love the game that are in a good environment where they are challenged but still having fun will keep playing and keep getting better." Its other article says this move "can happen at any age" and that the biggest shift tends to come around ages 10 to 12. A player who is bored or coasting in a recreational setting might be ready earlier. A player who is already overwhelmed and enjoying the game less every week is not ready simply because a birthday says so. Trying out is not a commitment, and families can revisit the decision each year.

It is also fair to warn against rushing. Our burnout guide covers the research in more detail. The American Academy of Pediatrics' 2016 clinical report on sports specialization states: "Burnout, anxiety, depression, and attrition are increased in early specializers," and it advises delaying specialization in a single sport until at least age 15 to 16. The National Athletic Trainers' Association recommends that organized hours per week not exceed a child's age and that kids have a minimum of two days off per week. Those studies show associations, not proof for any single child. The AAP's own news summary adds a balanced point: whether training is specialized or multisport, it becomes a problem when an athlete has no free play time. So ask any club how many months a year it expects and how much real rest is built in.

Our first season guide cites a 2025 Aspen Institute survey of 3,827 young people aged 10 to 17, in which former players most often named feeling "not good enough" (29 percent) and "bad coaching" (21 percent) as their least favorite part of playing. That is not the same as why they quit, but a club environment that is too intense too soon can feed both.

The playerAsk the player, not just the family

The decision belongs to the whole family, but the player should have a real voice. Ask open questions: "What do you like most about soccer right now?" "What would you want to be different?" "How would you feel about practicing more often?" Listen for enthusiasm and not just agreement. If a player says yes only to please a parent, that is worth a gentler conversation, not a signed contract.

For coachesWhat changes if you are the one in this conversation

If a parent asks you, as their recreational coach, whether their child is ready for club, the honest path matters more than the encouraging one. Braddock Road Youth Club has written guidance for coaches on cutting players at tryouts. It is a different situation, but its advice carries over: tell parents promptly and privately, say plainly "you haven't seen what you are looking for" if that is the case, and do not tell families a player will "probably survive tryouts, even if you believe it." Where possible, talk to a coach at another level first and help the family find a place where the player will be challenged and enjoy the game. That last part is our adaptation, not BRYC's wording. The goal is what is genuinely in the best interest of the child, not what is easiest to say in the moment.

If you are the one making the jump to coaching at the club level, expect the structure to change around you too. You will likely be working inside a set curriculum rather than building practices entirely on your own, and parents will expect more formal communication about development and playing time from day one. Our preseason parent meeting guide and player development guide both apply directly here, since club parents are paying more and expecting more transparency in return.

Before you signQuestions worth asking a club

  • What is the coaching philosophy, and does it match what your family wants for your player, development and enjoyment or results and rankings?
  • What does a realistic season cost, once travel, tournaments, and gear are included, not just the registration fee? Ask for it in writing.
  • How is playing time decided, and is there any guaranteed minimum for a healthy, available player?
  • What is the actual weekly and seasonal time commitment, including tournaments, travel distance, and how many months of the year?
  • What are the commitment terms? How early do you have to commit, and what are the refund and withdrawal rules if it is not a fit?
  • Who is the coach, and how are they trained?
  • Can you watch a practice first, to see the coaching style and team culture before committing a season to it?

Be cautious of pressure to commit before you have had time to decide, and of promises of college scholarships or guaranteed results. A club that welcomes your questions is a good sign.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Choosing a club by its name or ranking instead of watching how it actually coaches and treats players.
  • Underestimating the time commitment until the first tournament weekend arrives and the whole family's schedule is gone.
  • Signing without reading the terms, including fees, refunds, and how early you must commit.
  • Assuming earned playing time means no floor at all. A club with a reasonable minimum still exists and is worth seeking out.
  • Pushing a young player into intense specialization before they have shown they want it, rather than letting their own enjoyment lead.
  • A coach softening an honest assessment to avoid a hard conversation, which usually costs more trust later than it saves now.
