The Season Long Practice Plan: Turning Weaknesses Into a Real Progression With AI

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 27, 2026 · 7 min read

Most youth practice plans get built one week at a time, the night before or the morning of, in response to whatever went wrong at the last game. That is not a bad instinct, reacting to what you actually saw matters, but it rarely adds up to anything bigger than a string of disconnected sessions. A team that struggles to keep possession under pressure in September is often still struggling with the exact same thing in November, because no single practice was ever asked to build toward it on purpose. An AI assistant is well suited to the part of this that is tedious rather than creative, turning a short list of real weaknesses into an actual six to eight week arc, one theme building on the last.

Who this guide is for

Coaches who already have a solid single session template, ours is in the one hour practice guide, and want to string several weeks of it into a real progression aimed at specific weaknesses, instead of picking a fresh, unrelated topic every week. This pairs well with a coaching profile already in place, so the plan reflects your actual format and priorities from the first prompt.

The short version
  • Name two or three weaknesses, not ten. A plan trying to fix everything at once fixes nothing in particular.
  • Ask for a progression, not a list. Each week should build on what the last one covered, not repeat it or start over.
  • Expect four to eight weeks before a skill visibly improves, which is what makes a season long view worth building in the first place.
  • Keep every week in your existing one hour structure, so only the theme changes, not the whole shape of practice.
  • Revise the plan when real life interrupts it, a missed week, bad weather, or a skill clicking faster than expected, instead of quietly abandoning the rest of it.

The problemWhy one good session rarely fixes anything on its own

A single well run practice can absolutely improve a specific moment, a cleaner first touch that day, a better decision in one small sided game. What it cannot do is create the kind of lasting change coaches actually want, because skill development runs on a longer clock than one Tuesday night. U.S. Soccer's own coaching education materials on periodization state it plainly: "it typically takes from four to eight weeks to see significant improvements in skills such as strength, speed, power or endurance," organized into what the same framework calls a microcycle, one week of training, nested inside a longer mesocycle of about a month, all built around dividing the season into distinct phases rather than treating every week the same.

None of that requires a sports science degree to apply. It just requires deciding on a theme before the season gets away from you, and building several weeks toward it on purpose, which is exactly the part an AI assistant is good at once you hand it real information instead of a blank prompt.

Step oneName two or three real weaknesses, not everything at once

The instinct to fix every problem at once is understandable and almost always backfires. One youth coaching framework for structuring a season recommends assessing the team early and identifying "2 to 3 development priorities" to build toward, rather than a long list nobody will actually track. If you already log stats with MatchTap or track individual development over time, that data is the fastest way to get specific, a completion rate under pressure, a pattern of losing the ball in the same third of the field, a set piece that never quite executes. If you do not have numbers, your own honest memory of the last few games works fine, specificity matters more than the source.

"I coach [age group] [format] soccer. Based on the last few games and practices, here is what I have noticed: [describe two or three specific weaknesses in plain language, for example late runs into the box, losing composure under pressure near our own goal, or a slow first touch that gives defenders time to close down]. Ask me clarifying questions if you need more detail, then help me narrow this to the two or three priorities most worth building a multi week plan around." Step twoTurn the priorities into an actual progression

Once you have settled on real priorities, the next prompt is the one that does the actual work of this guide, asking for a multi week arc rather than a single session. Anchor it to whatever single practice structure you already trust, so the AI is filling in a theme each week rather than reinventing the whole shape of practice from scratch.

"Using the one hour practice structure of warm up, technical work, pressure phase, small sided game, and recap, build me a six week practice progression for [age group] [format] focused on these priorities, in order: [list your two or three priorities]. Each week should build on the last rather than repeating it, moving from an isolated version of the skill toward a version under real game pressure by the final week. For each week, give me the specific theme and one sentence on how it connects to the week before it." Paste this once you have named your real priorities

Read the six week overview before asking for any single week's full session detail. It is much faster to catch a progression that jumps around or repeats itself at the outline stage than after you have already generated six full practice plans. Once the arc looks right, ask for one week's plan in full detail at a time, the same way you would generate any single session, so you are not carrying six weeks of content into your notes before you actually need it.

The shape of a seasonMatching a progression to where you are in the year

What a six to eight week block should emphasize changes depending on where it falls in your season, and it is worth saying so explicitly in your prompt rather than assuming the AI knows. One youth season planning framework breaks a typical year into a preseason block weighted heavily toward technical work and team habits, an early season block that reacts to what shows up in actual games, a longer midseason stretch, and an end of season block that shifts toward evaluation and tapering volume down. You do not need to follow that exact structure, but naming which part of your season you are in, preseason, midseason, or a final stretch before playoffs, changes what a sensible six week plan should actually contain, and is worth a single sentence in your prompt.

Step threeRevising the plan when real life interrupts it

No six week plan survives a season untouched. A rained out week, a skill that clicked in three weeks instead of six, a tournament that eats a normal practice slot, all of it is completely normal, and none of it means starting over. Go back to the same conversation and ask for an adjustment rather than a fresh plan, so the AI keeps the context of what you already covered.

"We are now on week [number] of the six week plan. [Describe what actually happened, for example we missed week 3 to weather, or the team picked up the pressing theme faster than expected and is ready to move on]. Adjust the remaining weeks of the plan to account for this, keeping the same overall priorities and end goal." Paste this whenever the plan needs to bend to what actually happened

Our detailed guide to using AI as a coach covers the broader habit worth applying here too, checking anything the AI states as settled fact, a claimed timeline for skill improvement or a specific training ratio, against a real source before repeating it to parents or other coaches as established science.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Naming too many priorities at once, which produces a plan that touches everything and builds toward nothing in particular.
  • Asking for a full six weeks of detailed sessions before reviewing the overall arc, making a jumbled progression far more expensive to fix.
  • Ignoring where you actually are in the season and asking for a generic plan that does not match a preseason, midseason, or tapering stretch.
  • Abandoning the plan entirely after one missed week instead of asking for a quick adjustment to the remaining weeks.
  • Expecting a visible change after one or two sessions, when four to eight weeks is the realistic timeline for most skills to actually show improvement.
