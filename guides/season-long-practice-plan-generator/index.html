The Season Long Practice Plan: Turning Weaknesses Into a Real Progression With AI

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 27, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 10 min read

Most youth practice plans get built one week at a time, the night before or the morning of, in response to whatever went wrong at the last game. That is not a bad instinct, reacting to what you actually saw matters, but it rarely adds up to anything bigger than a string of disconnected sessions. A team that struggles to keep possession under pressure in September is often still struggling with the exact same thing in November, because no single practice was ever asked to build toward it on purpose. An AI assistant is well suited to the part of this that is tedious rather than creative, turning a short list of real weaknesses into an actual six to eight week arc, one theme building on the last.

Who this guide is for

Coaches who already have a solid single session template, ours is in the one hour practice guide, and want to string several weeks of it into a real progression aimed at specific weaknesses, instead of picking a fresh, unrelated topic every week. This pairs well with a coaching profile already in place, so the plan reflects your actual format and priorities from the first prompt.

The short version
  • Name two or three priorities, not ten. A plan trying to fix everything at once fixes nothing in particular.
  • Ask for a progression, not a list. Each week should build on what the last one covered, not repeat it or start over.
  • Treat six to eight weeks as a planning window, not a promise. How long a skill takes to improve varies by skill and by player, so check by watching games.
  • Keep every week in a session shape that fits the age. Our one hour structure is built for about U10 to U12, and younger players need shorter blocks and more play.
  • Revise the plan when real life interrupts it, a missed week, bad weather, or a skill clicking faster than expected, instead of quietly abandoning the rest of it.

The problemWhy one good session rarely fixes anything on its own

A single well run practice can absolutely improve a specific moment, a cleaner first touch that day, a better decision in one small sided game. What it cannot do is create the kind of lasting change coaches actually want, because skill development runs on a longer clock than one Tuesday night. The idea of planning across weeks has a name in coaching: periodization. U.S. Soccer's Learning Center page on periodization for youth soccer describes organizing training into one week periods, called microcycles, inside monthly time periods, called mesocycles.

One caution about timelines. The same page says it takes about four to eight weeks to see significant improvements in strength, speed, power, and endurance. That is a statement about physical fitness, and the page names no age group. No published timeline we could find says how many weeks a technical or tactical skill takes to improve. So treat six to eight weeks in this guide as a planning window that gives each theme room to build, not as a promise of when a skill will click.

None of this requires a sports science degree. It just requires deciding on a theme before the season gets away from you, and building several weeks toward it on purpose, which is exactly the part an AI assistant is good at once you hand it real information instead of a blank prompt.

Step oneName two or three real priorities, not everything at once

The instinct to fix every problem at once is understandable and almost always backfires. FlickTec, a company that sells a youth training app, recommends identifying "2 to 3 development priorities per player" as a pre-season step. Its blog cites no research, so treat that as one trainer's practical advice. We apply the same limit to the team, because a list nobody will actually track does not help anyone. If you already log stats with MatchTap or track individual development over time, that data is the fastest way to get specific: a pattern of losing the ball in the same third of the field, a set piece that never quite executes. If you do not have numbers, your own honest memory of the last few games works fine. Specificity matters more than the source.

"I coach [age group] [format] soccer. Based on the last few games and practices, here is what I have noticed: [describe two or three specific team level priorities in plain language, for example late runs into the box, losing composure under pressure near our own goal, or a slow first touch that gives defenders time to close down]. Do not use any player names. Ask me clarifying questions if you need more detail, then help me narrow this to the two or three priorities most worth building a multi week plan around." Paste this first, before asking for the actual plan

Step twoTurn the priorities into an actual progression

Once you have settled on real priorities, the next prompt is the one that does the actual work of this guide, asking for a multi week arc rather than a single session. Anchor it to whatever single practice structure you already trust, so the AI is filling in a theme each week rather than reinventing the whole shape of practice from scratch.

"Using a warm up, technical work, pressure phase, small sided game, and recap structure that fits [age group] players, build me a six week practice progression for [age group] [format] focused on these priorities, in order: [list your two or three priorities]. Each week should build on the last rather than repeating it, moving from an isolated version of the skill toward a version under real game pressure by the final week. For each week, give me the specific theme and one sentence on how it connects to the week before it. Show it as a table, and do not add more practice time than the one hour I have." Paste this once you have named your real priorities

Read the six week overview before asking for any single week's full session detail. It is much faster to catch a progression that jumps around or repeats itself at the outline stage than after you have already generated six full practice plans. Once the arc looks right, ask for one week's plan in full detail at a time, the same way you would generate any single session.

What a good arc looks like

Here is an example for U10 players whose priority is keeping the ball under pressure. This is our own illustration, not a published plan, but it shows the shape to look for: one skill, building from no pressure to game pressure.

WeekThemeHow it builds
1Receiving with an open body shape, alone and in pairsThe isolated skill, no pressure
2Passing and moving in trianglesSame skill, now with movement
33v1 keep away with a passive defenderLight pressure, then a checkpoint
44v2 in a small gridReal pressure
55v3 to a targetPressure plus a decision about where to go
6Small sided game with a two touch limitGame pressure, then review the plan

It also matches the Play, Practice, Play idea from our practice planning guide, where U.S. Soccer describes practice that resembles the game and includes decisions for players to make.

The shape of a seasonMatching a progression to where you are in the year

What a six to eight week block should emphasize changes depending on where it falls in your season, and it is worth saying so explicitly in your prompt rather than assuming the AI knows. FlickTec's season plan, which again comes from a vendor and cites no sources, divides a year into a preseason of four to six weeks before the first game, an early season of the first six to eight weeks of competition, a midseason of eight to ten weeks, and an end of season of the final four to six weeks. It leans technical early, adds more tactical work later, and lowers volume at the end. You do not need to follow that exact structure, but naming which part of your season you are in, preseason, midseason, or a final stretch before playoffs, changes what a sensible six week plan should contain, and is worth a single sentence in your prompt.

Is it working?A checkpoint after week three

A multi week theme is only useful if you can tell whether it is landing. After the third week, watch a game or the closing small sided game and keep a simple tally tied to your priority. For a possession theme, count how often the team strings together five passes in a row. For a first touch theme, count touches that let the player turn and face forward. Compare it with what you saw in week one. Then ask a few players what they enjoyed, because a plan that feels like a chore has stopped working no matter what the tally says. These checks are our own suggestions, not published standards.

Know your teamKeep it light for younger and recreational teams

Periodization comes from fitness training, and a six week arc is most useful for older players. For teams under about 10, and for recreational teams with uneven attendance, keep the themes playful and game based, skip the word weakness with the players, and build in a review week. Share the theme in positive terms, for example "this month we are getting better at keeping the ball." The plan changes what you do in practice, not how much practice there is. This is our coaching rule of thumb, not a research finding.

Step threeRevising the plan when real life interrupts it

No six week plan survives a season untouched. A rained out week, a skill that clicked in three weeks instead of six, a tournament that eats a normal practice slot, all of it is completely normal, and none of it means starting over. Go back to the same conversation and ask for an adjustment rather than a fresh plan, so the AI keeps the context of what you already covered.

"We are now on week [number] of the six week plan. [Describe what actually happened, for example we missed week 3 to weather, or the team picked up the pressing theme faster than expected and is ready to move on]. First restate the plan so far in a short table, then adjust the remaining weeks to account for this, keeping the same overall priorities and end goal." Paste this whenever the plan needs to bend to what actually happened

Keep the AI honest

  • Long chats drift. Asking it to restate the plan in a table before each change keeps you both on the same page.
  • Check what it can actually run. Tell it your players, space, and equipment, and ask it to flag any activity that needs more.
  • Check anything stated as fact. A claimed timeline for skill improvement or a specific training ratio can sound official and be wrong, so verify it against a real source before repeating it to parents. Our guide to using AI as a coach covers the habit in more depth.
  • Keep players anonymous. Describe team level patterns, not named children.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Naming too many priorities at once, which produces a plan that touches everything and builds toward nothing in particular.
  • Asking for a full six weeks of detailed sessions before reviewing the overall arc, making a jumbled progression far more expensive to fix.
  • Ignoring where you actually are in the season and asking for a generic plan that does not match a preseason, midseason, or end of season stretch.
  • Abandoning the plan entirely after one missed week instead of asking for a quick adjustment to the remaining weeks.
  • Treating six to eight weeks as a promise, when how long a skill takes to improve varies by skill and by player.
  • Turning practice into a fix it project for young players, instead of keeping the themes playful.

Check whether the plan is working
