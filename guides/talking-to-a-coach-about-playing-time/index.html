Talking to a Coach About Playing Time: A Parent's Game Plan

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · October 4, 2026 · 8 min read

Your kid is getting well under half the game, week after week, and you want it to change. The instinct is to say something the moment the final whistle blows. That is almost always the wrong moment, and for a lot of parents it is also unclear who to even say it to, the coach, the club, both, in what order. This guide is the actual game plan, in order, from checking your facts to knowing exactly who to talk to and what to say if the first conversation does not fix it.

Who this guide is for

Parents whose child is regularly playing well under half of games at the recreational or club level and want a productive way to raise it, not a confrontation that burns the relationship for the rest of the season. Our own equal playing time guide lays out the coaching standard we believe in, and our recreational to competitive guide covers how much that standard actually shifts once tryouts are involved.

The short version
  • Get the facts first. One rough game is not a pattern. Track minutes across three or four games before you act on a feeling.
  • Check what your league actually promises. Some leagues genuinely require a minimum. Many recreational leagues, honestly, leave it entirely to the coach.
  • Wait at least 24 hours after a game before raising it, and never in the parking lot right after the final whistle.
  • Talk to your child first, then go to the head coach directly and privately, before anyone else.
  • Ask, do not accuse. Find out the plan for your child's development instead of comparing them to a specific teammate.
  • Know the level changes the conversation. Earned minutes at a competitive level are a real, different situation than recreational soccer.
  • If the private conversation does not work, there is a real next step, and it is almost never the team group chat.

Before anythingGet the facts before you get the meeting

A single bad game, your kid on the bench for a stretch while the score got out of hand, feels urgent in the moment, but it is not evidence of a pattern by itself. Before you request a conversation, track actual minutes across three or four games, not your memory of how the last one felt. If the team already uses a tracking app, most coaches can pull up a season summary in a minute or two, and that is a completely reasonable thing to ask for. SubGo, the free tracker we built for coaches, keeps exactly this kind of record, every player's total minutes by game, exportable as a simple list, specifically so a playing time conversation can start from real numbers instead of two different memories of how a game went.

Know your leagueCheck what your league actually promises, honestly

The honest answer here is more mixed than most parents expect, and it matters a lot for how you frame the conversation. AYSO's own national rulebook is explicit: "all eligible team members in attendance at AYSO matches must play at least half of the match, excluding overtime." If your league is AYSO, that is not a coaching philosophy, it is a written rule, and a coach who is not meeting it is not following the league's own regulations.

Plenty of other recreational leagues do not go that far in writing. When we researched this for our own equal playing time guide, we checked the actual playing rules published by our own regional US Youth Soccer affiliate and found no minimum playing time requirement anywhere in them, which leaves the decision to the coach. That does not mean a 50% floor is a bad ask, it is the standard we coach to ourselves and the one plenty of respected programs use, it means you should know going in whether you are pointing to an actual rule or making a fairness argument, because those are two different conversations with two different amounts of leverage behind them. Find your own league's actual published rules before the meeting, not after.

FirstTalk to your child before you talk to the coach

Before you request anything, find out how your child actually experiences this. Some kids are genuinely bothered by limited minutes. Others are having a great time regardless and would be embarrassed to learn a parent is lobbying on their behalf. Ask what they think is going on, whether there was a specific moment that stuck with them, and what they actually want, more playing time specifically, or just to understand why things are the way they are. What you hear should shape what you ask for, and it keeps the conversation from becoming about your read on the situation instead of your child's actual experience.

TimingWait a day, then request a real conversation

Positive Coaching Alliance, a national nonprofit focused on youth sports culture, recommends a cooling off period before raising anything with a coach, describing the boundary directly: "we can't talk until 24 hours after a competition so any emotional build up dissipates." That window exists for a reason. A conversation started in the parking lot minutes after a tough game runs on adrenaline for both people, and almost nothing productive gets decided in it.

Once the day has passed, ask for an actual conversation rather than trying to squeeze it in during a pickup line or a group text. A short message works fine for this part: something like "could we find 10 minutes to talk this week about my child's playing time" gets you a real, private conversation instead of a rushed one, and it signals you are approaching this as a discussion, not an ambush.

The conversationHow to actually open it

The way you frame the opening matters as much as anything you say after it. Skye Eddy of the Soccer Parenting Association, a youth soccer education organization, suggests starting plainly and without accusation: "I'd like to talk with you about the amount of time my child has been playing in the games." From there, she recommends naming your actual priority rather than a demand, "my number one priority is that my child has fun and continues to develop," and then asking directly instead of guessing, "please be very honest with me, why is my child playing less than half of a game."

Notice what is missing from that script: any other player's name. Comparing your child directly to a specific teammate turns a conversation about development into a competition the coach cannot referee in front of you, and it is the fastest way to make the conversation defensive on both sides. Positive Coaching Alliance draws the line on scope the same way, noting plainly that a parent can raise "your athlete's attitude or psyche," but that "the coach is in charge of decisions of playing time, lineups, and strategy." You are there to understand the decision and advocate for your child, not to make the lineup call yourself.

ContextRecreational and competitive are different conversations

How much this conversation can actually change depends heavily on the level your child plays at, and being honest with yourself about that going in saves everyone frustration. At the recreational level, and generally through the 9v9 age range even in club soccer, our own coaching philosophy holds that minutes should stay close to equal regardless of skill, and that is the standard worth advocating for. Our recreational to competitive guide covers this shift directly, once real tryouts are involved, playing time becomes earned through performance and role rather than automatically rotated, and a good club will still hold a reasonable floor, but it will not be equal minutes for every player regardless of form.

That changes what you should actually ask for. At the recreational level, the fair question is close to "why isn't my child getting close to equal time." At a competitive level, the more useful question is "what specifically does my child need to work on to earn more minutes," since that is a question a coach can actually answer and act on, and it opens a real development conversation instead of a debate about fairness that the level was never built to guarantee.

If nothing changesThe actual next step, and who it is not

If the private conversation does not lead anywhere and the pattern continues, most clubs do have a real chain of command, and it is worth following in order rather than skipping straight to the top. One club's own parent handbook lays it out plainly: talk to the head coach first, then, if unresolved, the program director, then the director of coaching, then the board. That same handbook is also honest about a hard limit worth knowing in advance, it lists "playing time that falls within the club guidelines" as a topic that is explicitly not up for board level discussion. In plain terms, the people above the coach can often address how a concern was handled, but they will rarely overturn a playing time decision that does not actually break a written rule. Bring your notes, the dates and minutes you tracked, and what was said in the first conversation, to whichever step you reach.

Watch outWhat not to do

  • Confronting the coach in the parking lot right after the game, while everyone is still running on adrenaline.
  • Raising it in the team group chat or on social media, which puts the coach on the defensive in public before you have even had a private conversation.
  • Comparing your child by name to a specific teammate, instead of focusing on your own child's development.
  • Letting your child feel like a project being negotiated, rather than finding out what they actually want first.
  • Skipping the head coach and going straight to the club director or board, before giving the person actually making the decision a chance to respond.
  • Assuming a written minimum exists when your specific league may leave the decision entirely to the coach.
