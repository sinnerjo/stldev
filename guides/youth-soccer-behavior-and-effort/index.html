Handling a Player Who Is Struggling With Behavior or Effort: Discipline That Teaches

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of MatchTap · September 23, 2026, updated October 21, 2026 · 15 min read

Every coach eventually gets a player who is acting out, or one who has visibly checked out and stopped trying. The easy response to both is the same, benching them until the problem goes away. It rarely teaches anything, and it often makes a struggling kid feel like the season is already over. There is a better way, and it treats behavior problems and effort problems as two different things that need two different responses.

Who this guide is for

Coaches with a player who is misbehaving, checked out, or visibly not trying, who want a response that changes something rather than one that just removes the player from view for a while. If your player is going quiet and freezing up instead of acting out or checking out, that is a different problem with a different fix, covered in our quiet or anxious player guide. Setting expectations before this ever comes up is covered in our preseason parent meeting guide. If a language gap could be the real explanation, check our multilingual team guide before you assume it is attitude.

The short version
  • Set expectations before problems start, not after, so a correction is enforcing something the player already agreed to.
  • Frame it as a consequence to learn from, not a punishment to endure.
  • Correct quietly, praise publicly, except with a shy or anxious player, where a quiet word often works better. Keep a correction out of earshot of teammates but never out of sight of other adults.
  • Low effort is usually a signal, not laziness. Find out what changed before you discipline it.
  • Never use running or exercise as punishment. It teaches players to resent the very activity you want them to love.
  • Match the response to the age. A U6 player needs a quick redirect, not a four step ladder.
  • Get curious about differences like ADHD or being autistic, quietly and with the family's help, before assuming a player is being difficult on purpose.

BehaviorTreat it as a consequence, not a punishment

The word you use in your own head changes how the conversation goes. One coach training resource puts it directly: calling something a punishment makes an athlete "guarded and ready for the fight," while framing the same moment as a consequence supports learning instead of triggering denial. A simple version of that framing sounds like this: behaviors have consequences so we can learn from our actions, and the consequence for what happened is this. Said calmly, like a conversation rather than a sentencing, it tends to land as fair rather than as an attack.

The Rutgers Youth Sports Research Council's list of 20 positive discipline strategies backs up a few habits worth building on purpose. It advises minimizing public criticism and using public praise, which is why we suggest correcting quietly. A shy or anxious player is the exception, because public praise can embarrass them, and a quiet word often works better. It says to be predictable, meaning you react to behavior consistently so players know what to expect. And it says to discipline quickly and briefly without holding a grudge, then welcome the athlete back as an equal.

A workable escalation, adapted from a coaching guide built specifically for soccer, looks something like this. A first issue gets a private reminder of the team rules. A second gets a sterner reminder that explains exactly how the rule is being broken. A third gets a short time away from the distraction, with you deciding when the player returns, followed by a brief talk about changing the behavior. A fourth means the player sits out the rest of practice, followed by a conversation with the player and a parent. None of those steps need to be dramatic, and most issues resolve well before you reach the end of the list.

This ladder is for ordinary issues. Fighting, bullying, harassment, or anything that puts someone at real risk does not start at step one. Those go to your club or league, which is also the right call when a pattern keeps repeating despite your best efforts.

Private means out of earshot, not out of sight

One safeguard belongs in every version of this. The U.S. Center for SafeSport's one on one interactions policy says contact between an adult and a minor athlete must be "observable and interruptible," meaning other people can easily see it and someone could step in. Its own example is a head coach talking to a struggling player off to the side of practice while the team and an assistant coach stay in view. So when this guide says private, it means away from teammates' ears, not behind a closed door or out of sight. A player on a short break should stay within view of an adult too. Check the policy your club or league follows, because local rules and exceptions can differ.

Match the response to the age

The same ladder does not fit every player. This table is our suggestion, based on coaching experience, not a research finding.

AgeWhat worksWhat to adjust
U6 to U8A quick, calm redirect in the moment, then a fresh start.Skip the full ladder. Keep any break to a minute or two next to an assistant, and use simple words.
U9 to U12The full ladder, with a short talk after each step.Ask the player to say what happened in their own words before you explain the consequence.
U13 and upStandards the players helped write, and the full ladder.Bring in the parent sooner, and expect more questions about why.

One line worth building your whole approach around: it is close to impossible to start loose on discipline and tighten up later once behavior has already gotten worse. Set the standard clearly at the start of the season, ideally in the same preseason meeting where you cover everything else, and it is far easier to enforce all season.

What not to doNever make exercise the punishment

Running laps or doing push ups as a consequence is common and worth avoiding entirely. The goal all season is for players to enjoy training, including the physical parts of it, and turning movement into a punishment teaches the opposite lesson. Alternatives that do not carry that cost include a direct verbal warning, a brief removal from the activity or the game, a small task such as helping pick up equipment, or, for anything serious enough to raise real safety concerns, involving your league or club rather than handling it alone. TrueSport's version of the task idea is cleaning the field. SHAPE America, the national association of health and physical educators, also takes the position that physical activity should not be used as punishment.

A removal from the game should also respect your league's rules. Many leagues set minimum playing time, so check yours before you plan to sit a player for part of a match. Our equal playing time guide covers how those rules work.

EffortA drop in effort is usually a signal, not laziness

A player who stops trying is a different problem than a player who is misbehaving, and it deserves a different first move. Vanta Sports, a youth sports software company, writes that motivation usually fades through pressure, boredom, confusion, or a feeling that the sport no longer belongs to the child, and that declining effort is a signal to look into before disciplining. That is a practitioner's view, not a study, but it matches how many coaches describe the pattern. Before assuming a kid simply decided to be lazy, it is worth quietly ruling out the first three.

The sport psychology side of this is consistent with that. The Association for Applied Sport Psychology contrasts athletes whose motivation is controlled by outside rewards, who show less interest and effort, with athletes who feel in control of their own behavior, who show greater interest, effort, and positive coping. To support the second kind, it suggests specific verbal and nonverbal praise tied to actions, acknowledging each athlete's role, and setting stretch but attainable goals together with the athlete. Vanta Sports adds small choices that are genuinely the player's, as part of building a learning focused climate. Picking a warm up activity or naming their own focus for a practice are our examples of that.

In practice this can sound like noticing something better than before, not just a good outcome: your first touch improved when you opened your body up, let's try that again under a bit more pressure. That kind of specific, process focused praise is worth building into a habit, described in more depth in our own player development guide, which is really the long term version of the same idea, a player who sees their own honest progress on something specific tends to bring more effort on their own, without needing to be pushed into it.

Getting to know playersSome players experience the game differently

Everything above assumes a coach can tell the difference between a player who is choosing not to try and a player who is struggling for a reason that has nothing to do with attitude. For some players, that reason is a difference like ADHD or being autistic, and it is worth staying genuinely curious rather than assuming defiance or disinterest, especially since these differences can show up as either a behavior moment or a drop in effort.

A player with ADHD who looks unfocused during a water break or a long explanation is not always disengaged. CHADD, the national ADHD organization, makes this point about information sharing. When a parent tells the coach about a child's ADHD, the coach becomes "less likely to attribute a lack of focus to limited interest in participating." CHADD also lists staying focused during periods of inactivity as a common struggle, and it suggests giving kids a task during downtime so they stay occupied. In soccer, a small job during a long explanation or a water break, like handing out pinnies, is our version of the same idea.

U.S. Soccer's resource hub summarizes a guide from Soccer Parenting on coaching children with ADHD, including how it affects focus, communication, learning, and behavior. We could read only the summary page, so we do not quote the guide. If you can open it, it is worth the time. For a fuller plan, see our guide to coaching players with ADHD, which covers practice changes, the family conversation, and AI prompts.

An autistic player may also communicate or process the game differently, and a little adjustment in how you coach goes a long way. The Rutgers Youth Sports Research Council recommends keeping your instructions "short, slow, and precise." It notes that sarcasm, humor, metaphors, and abstract statements will generally not be understood, and it advises warning players about upcoming changes with transition prompts, such as saying plainly that you are about to stop one drill and start the next, rather than expecting an instant transition.

None of this is about labeling a child from the sideline. A coach is not a clinician, and guessing at a diagnosis based on one practice is not the goal here, quietly staying curious is. Many players who seem distracted or low energy on a given day are just having a normal day, tired or bored the way any kid can be, and that does not need an explanation either. The point is simply to leave room for a different reason before jumping to discipline.

Ask the family, gently and privately

The people who already know the answer are usually the parents, and the best time to ask is before it ever becomes a discipline conversation. Our own preseason parent meeting guide is a natural place for this. Instead of singling anyone out, invite every family to share anything that would help you coach their child well, the same open question for the whole team, so no parent feels put on the spot for volunteering something personal. Sharing is always the family's choice to make, never something to ask a child to explain in front of teammates.

The Rutgers autism page includes a sample parent questionnaire that asks things like how the child communicates and how a parent redirects them when they get agitated. Those two questions are a good model for any family: how does your child communicate best, and what helps when they get frustrated. We suggest keeping the ask open to all families, keeping what they share confidential, and using it only to coach that child better. A short, respectful conversation like this is also just good coaching for every player on the roster, not only the ones with a diagnosis behind the moment.

ExampleWhat a private conversation can sound like

For a player who has stopped trying: "I have noticed you have not been getting into the small sided games the last couple of practices. Is everything okay? Is there something about practice that is not working for you right now?" Listen first. The answer might be boredom, might be something happening off the field entirely, and either changes what you do next far more than a lecture about effort would.

For a behavior issue: "Pushing your teammate is not something we do here, even when you are frustrated. You are going to sit out the next few minutes, and then we will go again. I know you can play better than that." Short, specific, and closed with a vote of confidence rather than a lingering punishment.

For the parent, when it reaches step four: "I want to share something I have seen, and I would like your help. At the last three practices, [player] has been pushing teammates when frustrated. We have talked about it each time. What helps [player] reset at home? And here is what I will do on my end." Open with what you have seen, ask before you advise, agree on one next step together, and have the talk away from the players, in view of other adults, not in the middle of a game.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Starting the season loose on expectations, then trying to tighten up once behavior has already slipped.
  • Using running or exercise as a consequence, which teaches players to dread the very activity that should be fun.
  • Benching a player for the rest of the season as the first response, rather than a last one reached step by step.
  • Treating an effort problem like a behavior problem, and skipping straight to discipline before asking what changed.
  • Correcting a player in front of the group, which usually produces embarrassment and defensiveness instead of change.
  • Holding a grudge after the consequence is over, instead of welcoming the player back as a full member of the team.
  • Having a private talk where no other adult can see, when the safe version is away from teammates but in view of others.
  • Letting each coach on the staff enforce a different standard, which players quickly learn to exploit.
  • Sending a serious incident down the ladder, instead of to your club or league.

Try thisDo this before your next practice

Write down your three team expectations in under ten words each, phrased as what players should do instead of what they should not do. Share them with your assistant coaches so everyone enforces the same three, and read them to the players at the start of practice. If you can, let the players add one of their own. This is our suggestion, but it puts the most important idea in this guide into practice: the standard exists before the problem does.

Ask your AI assistant coachTurn this framework into a written plan, and build a praise bank

AI is useful here for the writing and the planning, not for judging a child. It can turn this guide's framework into your own one page plan, help you prepare the questions to ask a family, and build language you can use mid practice. It cannot tell you why a particular player is acting the way they are. If you are new to AI, start with our beginner guide, and for answers that sound like you, set up a coaching profile first.

1. Your written plan. Good output is a one page plan in your words that an assistant coach could follow without asking you anything.

"I coach [age group] [format]. Using a consequence, not punishment framework, help me write a one page plan for behavior issues: a private reminder, then a firmer one, then a brief removal that keeps the player within sight of an adult, then a parent conversation. Use simple language an assistant coach could follow, and adjust the steps for my age group." Paste this before you need it, not during a tense moment

2. Questions to ask, not answers. Good output is a short list of open questions you can ask a player or family. Ask the AI to stay away from guessing a cause.

"I coach [age group] [format]. A player has seemed to stop trying over the last few practices. Do not guess why. Write me five open, friendly questions I could ask the player or the family to understand what changed, and tell me which question to start with." The questions are the point, because only the player and family know the reason

3. A praise bank. Good output is exact phrases you can say without thinking.

"I coach [age group] [format]. Build me a bank of ten specific, effort focused praise phrases I can use during practice and games, tied to things like hustle, recovering well after a mistake, or communicating with teammates, rather than outcome based praise like scoring or winning." Paste this once, then keep the list handy to pull from during a game

4. A pressure test. Once you have a plan, ask the AI to find the holes in it.

"Here is my behavior plan: [paste it]. Act as an experienced youth coach and point out where it is unclear, unfair to a younger player, or likely to cause an argument with a parent. Do not rewrite it, just list the problems." Fix what it finds, then give the plan to your assistant coaches

Never paste a real player's name or enough detail to identify a specific child into a general AI tool, especially for anything touching behavior or a private family situation. Use AI to build your own general approach in advance, not to describe or get advice about an actual player. AI can also invent research and quotes, so check any source it names before you repeat it.
