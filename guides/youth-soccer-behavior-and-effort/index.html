Handling a Player Who Is Struggling With Behavior or Effort: Discipline That Teaches

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 23, 2026 · 9 min read

Every coach eventually gets a player who is acting out, or one who has visibly checked out and stopped trying. The easy response to both is the same, benching them until the problem goes away. It rarely teaches anything, and it often makes a struggling kid feel like the season is already over. There is a better way, and it treats behavior problems and effort problems as two different things that need two different responses.

Who this guide is for

Coaches with a player who is misbehaving, checked out, or visibly not trying, who want a response that changes something rather than one that just removes the player from view for a while. If your player is going quiet and freezing up instead of acting out or checking out, that is a different problem with a different fix, covered in our quiet or anxious player guide. Setting expectations before this ever comes up is covered in our preseason parent meeting guide. If a language gap could be the real explanation, check our multilingual team guide before you assume it is attitude.

The short version
  • Set expectations before problems start, not after, so a correction is enforcing something the player already agreed to.
  • Frame it as a consequence to learn from, not a punishment to endure.
  • Correct in private, praise in public. A public correction usually produces defensiveness, not change.
  • Low effort is usually a signal, not laziness. Find out what changed before you discipline it.
  • Never use running or exercise as punishment. It teaches players to resent the very activity you want them to love.
  • Get curious about differences like ADHD or being autistic, quietly and with the family's help, before assuming a player is being difficult on purpose.

BehaviorTreat it as a consequence, not a punishment

The word you use in your own head changes how the conversation goes. One coach training resource puts it directly: calling something a punishment makes an athlete "guarded and ready for the fight," while framing the same moment as a consequence supports learning instead of triggering denial. A simple version of that framing sounds like this: behaviors have consequences so we can learn from our actions, and the consequence for what happened is this. Said calmly, like a conversation rather than a sentencing, it tends to land as fair rather than as an attack.

The Rutgers Youth Sports Research Council's list of positive discipline techniques backs up a few habits worth building on purpose. Correct privately, but praise publicly, since public criticism tends to embarrass rather than teach. Be predictable, so players know what to expect rather than guessing at your mood that day. And once it is handled, let it go. Discipline briefly, without a grudge, and welcome the player back as a full member of the team, not someone still being punished an hour later.

A workable escalation, adapted from a coaching guide built specifically for soccer, looks something like this. A first issue gets a private, specific reminder of the expectation. A second gets a firmer version of the same reminder, naming exactly what was crossed. A third gets a short removal from the activity, ended on your terms rather than the player's. A fourth gets a longer sit and a conversation with the parent. None of those steps need to be dramatic, and most issues resolve well before you reach the end of the list.

One line worth building your whole approach around: it is close to impossible to start loose on discipline and tighten up later once behavior has already gotten worse. Set the standard clearly at the start of the season, ideally in the same preseason meeting where you cover everything else, and it is far easier to enforce all season.

What not to doNever make exercise the punishment

Running laps or doing push ups as a consequence is common and worth avoiding entirely. The goal all season is for players to enjoy training, including the physical parts of it, and turning movement into a punishment teaches the opposite lesson. Alternatives that do not carry that cost include a direct verbal warning, a brief removal from the activity or the game, a small non physical task such as helping reset equipment, or, for anything serious enough to raise real safety concerns, involving your league or club rather than handling it alone.

EffortA drop in effort is usually a signal, not laziness

A player who stops trying is a different problem than a player who is misbehaving, and it deserves a different first move. One youth sports resource frames it well: motivation fades because of pressure, boredom, confusion, or a feeling that the sport no longer belongs to the child, not because a kid simply decided to be lazy. Before assuming the second, it is worth quietly ruling out the first three.

The research on motivation points at why discipline alone rarely fixes this. The Association for Applied Sport Psychology notes that athletes controlled mainly by outside rewards or pressure show less interest and effort over time, while those who feel a sense of ownership over their own effort show more. Two things build that ownership. Specific, honest feedback tied to something the player actually did, rather than generic praise, and small choices that are genuinely theirs, such as picking a warm up activity or naming their own focus for a practice.

In practice this can sound like noticing something better than before, not just a good outcome: your first touch improved when you opened your body up, let's try that again under a bit more pressure. That kind of specific, process focused praise is worth building into a habit, described in more depth in our own player development guide, which is really the long term version of the same idea, a player who sees their own honest progress on something specific tends to bring more effort on their own, without needing to be pushed into it.

Getting to know playersSome players experience the game differently

Everything above assumes a coach can tell the difference between a player who is choosing not to try and a player who is struggling for a reason that has nothing to do with attitude. For some players, that reason is a difference like ADHD or being autistic, and it is worth staying genuinely curious rather than assuming defiance or disinterest, especially since these differences can show up as either a behavior moment or a drop in effort.

A player with ADHD who looks unfocused during a water break or a long explanation is not always disengaged. CHADD, the national ADHD organization, notes that a coach who understands a child's attention or executive function challenges is "less likely to attribute a lack of focus to limited interest in participating." Sometimes a player has already processed what you said and is simply unsure what to do with the extra standing around time, which can look a lot like not paying attention from the sideline. U.S. Soccer's own guidance for coaches puts the goal simply: individualize support for the player who needs it while keeping things fair for the whole team.

An autistic player may also communicate or process the game differently, and a little adjustment in how you coach goes a long way. The Rutgers Youth Sports Research Council recommends keeping instructions "short, slow, and precise," avoiding sarcasm or figurative language that may not land as intended, and giving a heads up before switching activities, such as saying plainly that you are about to stop one drill and start the next, rather than expecting an instant transition.

None of this is about labeling a child from the sideline. A coach is not a clinician, and guessing at a diagnosis based on one practice is not the goal here, quietly staying curious is. Many players who seem distracted or low energy on a given day are just having a normal day, tired or bored the way any kid can be, and that does not need an explanation either. The point is simply to leave room for a different reason before jumping to discipline.

Ask the family, gently and privately

The people who already know the answer are usually the parents, and the best time to ask is before it ever becomes a discipline conversation. Our own preseason parent meeting guide is a natural place for this. Instead of singling anyone out, invite every family to share anything that would help you coach their child well, the same open question for the whole team, so no parent feels put on the spot for volunteering something personal. Sharing is always the family's choice to make, never something to ask a child to explain in front of teammates.

The Rutgers guidance suggests a short, optional, and confidential question or two covers most of what actually helps: how the player communicates best, what tends to help them reset if they get frustrated, and anything that reliably helps them refocus. U.S. Soccer frames the larger goal the same way, describing real partnerships between coaches, parents, and schools as what actually supports a child well, rather than any one adult guessing alone. A short, respectful conversation like this is also just good coaching for every player on the roster, not only the ones with a diagnosis behind the moment.

ExampleWhat a private conversation can sound like

For a player who has stopped trying: "I have noticed you have not been getting into the small sided games the last couple of practices. Is everything okay? Is there something about practice that is not working for you right now?" Listen first. The answer might be boredom, might be something happening off the field entirely, and either changes what you do next far more than a lecture about effort would.

For a behavior issue: "Pushing your teammate is not something we do here, even when you are frustrated. You are going to sit out the next few minutes, and then we will go again. I know you can play better than that." Short, specific, and closed with a vote of confidence rather than a lingering punishment.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Starting the season loose on expectations, then trying to tighten up once behavior has already slipped.
  • Using running or exercise as a consequence, which teaches players to dread the very activity that should be fun.
  • Benching a player for the rest of the season as the first response, rather than a last one reached step by step.
  • Treating an effort problem like a behavior problem, and skipping straight to discipline before asking what changed.
  • Correcting a player in front of the group, which usually produces embarrassment and defensiveness instead of change.
  • Holding a grudge after the consequence is over, instead of welcoming the player back as a full member of the team.
