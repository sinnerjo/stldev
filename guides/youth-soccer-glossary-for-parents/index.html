The Youth Soccer Glossary for Parents: Every Term You Have Nodded Along To

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of MatchTap · September 24, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 11 min read

Someone yells "overlap" and every parent on the bench nods like it means something. A flag goes up for offside and half the sideline groans without knowing why. You do not need a coaching license to follow a youth soccer game, but you do need the words. This is the glossary we wish someone had handed us at our first game, checked against the actual rule book rather than sideline folklore.

Who this guide is for

Parents who are new to soccer, or who have been coming to games for years and would still like someone to finally explain offside. Where a rule comes from an actual rule book, we say so and quote it. Where a term is just common soccer language with no official definition, we say that too.

The one everyone fakes understandingOffside, actually explained

Here is the real rule, from the International Football Association Board, the organization that writes soccer's actual rule book. A player is in an offside position if "any part of the head, body or feet is in the opponents' half (excluding the halfway line)" and "any part of the head, body or feet is nearer to the opponents' goal line than both the ball and the second-last opponent." In plain terms: an attacking player is offside if they are standing closer to the other team's goal than both the ball and the second to last defender, at the moment a teammate plays the ball to them. The goalkeeper usually counts as the last defender, which is why people say "behind the last defender" as shorthand. Hands and arms do not count: the rule book says "the hands and arms of all players, including the goalkeepers, are not considered."

Standing in an offside position is not actually illegal by itself. The flag only goes up once that player gets "involved in active play," which the rule book breaks into three situations: touching a ball a teammate passed or touched, interfering with an opponent, or gaining an advantage from a ball that rebounded or deflected off a post, the crossbar, or an opponent. A player can be standing offside all game and never be flagged, as long as the ball never actually comes to them while they are in that position.

There is also a set of restarts where offside cannot be called at all. The rule book says there is no offside offence if a player receives the ball directly from a goal kick, a throw-in, or a corner kick. That is why attackers can stand next to the goal on a corner without anyone raising a flag.

In youth soccer, offside is not called at all in the smallest formats. US Soccer's small sided standards, announced in 2017, say that "there will be no offside infraction before the U-9 age group." Before that, in 3v3 and 4v4, there is no offside rule to break at all.

A quick aside, since it made real news in 2026: the Canadian Premier League is trialing "daylight offside" during its 2026 season, where a player is only offside if their head, body and feet are all wholly beyond the last defender (and the ball still has to be involved in the test). That is one competition's trial, run in collaboration with FIFA under IFAB's mandate, not a change to the actual law. If your child's league calls offside differently next season, it will not be because of this.

Every whistle, decodedThe calls that stop play

  • Handball. Not every hand touch is a foul. The rule book calls it an offense when a player "deliberately touches the ball with their hand/arm," or when a touch happens after the player "has made their body unnaturally bigger." A touch that comes from a natural position of the arm is treated differently from one that does not.
  • Direct free kick versus indirect free kick. A direct kick can be scored straight into the goal. An indirect kick cannot, the ball has to touch another player, from either team, before it counts. The referee signals indirect by raising one arm straight up and keeping it there until the ball is touched or leaves play.
  • Penalty kick. Awarded when a foul that would normally earn a direct free kick happens inside the fouling team's own penalty box.
  • Corner kick versus goal kick. Whoever touched the ball last before the whole ball crossed the goal line (outside the goal) decides the call. Last touch by the attacking team means a goal kick for the defense. Last touch by the defending team means a corner kick for the attack.
  • Throw-in. The thrower faces the field with part of each foot on the touchline or on the ground outside it, and throws the ball with both hands from behind and over the head. If the throw-in is not taken correctly, the rule book says it is retaken by the opposing team.
  • Added time versus extra time. These are not the same thing. Added time, also called stoppage time, is the extra minutes the referee tacks onto a half to make up for stoppages like injuries or substitutions. Extra time is a separate mini match played after a tied knockout game, and it only happens in tournaments that require a winner that day.
  • Build out line. A youth only rule in the small sided games that use it. When the goalkeeper has the ball in hand or takes a goal kick, the opposing team must stay behind the line until the ball is in play, so the team in possession can play out of the back without getting swarmed. US Soccer's standards put the line 14 yards in front of each goal and start build out lines at U9. They also say a team cannot be called offside between midfield and the line. Leagues can vary, so ask yours.
  • Yellow card versus red card. A yellow card is a formal warning for things like a reckless foul, arguing with the referee, or delaying the restart, and it stays on the record for that game. A red card ends a player's game immediately, for serious fouls, violent conduct, or a second yellow in the same match, and their team plays the rest of the game a player short.

What the coach is yellingTactical words with no rule book entry

None of the words in this section come from an official rule book. They are just common soccer language, the kind every coach uses and assumes everyone already understands. Coaches use some of them differently, so if your child's coach uses a word another way, trust the coach.

  • Marking. Man marking means a defender follows one specific opponent everywhere they go. Zonal marking means a defender is responsible for a space on the field and picks up whoever enters it.
  • Overlap. A supporting player, often a fullback, runs around and past a teammate who has the ball while staying wide, offering a new passing option further up the field.
  • Press or pressing. A coordinated effort, not just one player, to pressure whoever has the ball and force a mistake or a turnover.
  • Through ball. A pass played into open space behind the defense for a teammate to run onto, instead of a pass played directly to their feet.
  • Possession. Which team controls the ball, often talked about as a percentage of the game.
  • Transition. The moment right after the ball changes teams, before anyone has reorganized. Winning it and immediately looking to attack is an attacking transition. Losing it and immediately sprinting to regroup is a defensive transition.
  • One touch. Passing or shooting the ball on the first touch, without controlling it first.
  • Give and go. Also called a wall pass or a one two. A player passes to a teammate and immediately runs into space to receive the ball right back, as if it bounced off a wall.
  • Third man run. A third player, not the passer and not the first receiver, times a run into space to receive the next pass while the defense is watching the first two players.
  • Offside trap. A defense stepping forward together at the right moment to leave an attacker in an offside position just as the ball is played.
  • Man on. A warning shouted to the player with the ball that an opponent is close behind them, so they know to protect the ball or pass.
  • Switch. A call to change the point of attack by passing the ball from one side of the field to the other.
  • Support. A call for teammates to move closer to the player with the ball, so there is someone to pass to.
  • Turn. A call to the player with the ball, telling them they have space to turn and face forward.
  • Shape. How the team spreads out on the field. "Keep your shape" means stay in your spots instead of bunching around the ball.
  • Recover. A call for players to sprint back toward their own goal after losing the ball.
  • Counter attack. A fast attack launched the instant a team wins the ball, aiming to score before the other defense can get organized.

What ends up on the stat sheetScoring terms

  • Clean sheet. A game in which a team, and its goalkeeper, did not concede a goal.
  • Brace and hat trick. A brace is two goals by one player in a game. A hat trick is three.
  • Own goal. When a player accidentally sends the ball into their own team's goal, and it counts as a goal for the other team.
  • Assist. The final pass or action that directly sets up a goal. There is no single official standard for what counts, different leagues and stat keepers can be more or less generous about it.

Words that only exist in youth soccerClub and league specific terms

  • Guest player. A player who normally plays for one team but plays a tournament for a different team, usually to fill a roster gap. US Youth Soccer's policy defines it as a player "participating in a competition for a team to which the player is not rostered," and it lets tournament competitions accept guest players. We did not find a similar allowance for league play, but state associations can have their own rules, so ask yours. Our guest player guide covers this in more depth.
  • Call up. A player from a younger age group or a lower team getting asked to play up with an older or higher team, often temporarily.
  • Small sided. An umbrella term for any format smaller than the full eleven a side game, generally 3v3 through 9v9 depending on age. Our positions guide walks through what changes at each step.
  • ID camp. Short for identification camp, a camp run by a college program or a recruiting service where high school age players train and get evaluated for recruiting purposes.
  • Showcase. A tournament organized, and attended, specifically so college coaches and scouts can watch players, as distinct from a tournament that exists purely to compete.
  • ODP. Short for the Olympic Development Program, a state by state pathway of tryouts, identification camps, and regional competition run through US Youth Soccer. It is still active as of this writing. Worth knowing honestly: US Soccer itself has also built its own separate talent identification structure for national teams in recent years, so ODP is one pathway into higher level soccer, not automatically the only one.
  • Caps. This one gets used loosely, but its real meaning is an appearance for a national team. You will hear it around state or regional select teams sometimes, but a parent is very unlikely to hear it used for a normal club game, so do not worry if you never encounter it.
