Youth Soccer Sideline Guide for Parents: Cheer, Don't Coach

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of MatchTap · September 20, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 13 min read

The best thing you can do on the sideline is also the simplest. Cheer, stay positive, and let your child play. You do not need to say much, and you almost never need to give directions. This guide covers what to do during the game, why it helps, and what to say on the car ride home.

Who this guide is for

Parents of players in recreational and club soccer. Much of the research here is about older children and teens, so adjust it to your own child's age. Coaches are welcome to send this page to their team parents before the first game. If the terms coaches and referees use still sound like a foreign language, our soccer glossary for parents covers those.

The short version
  • Cheer, do not coach. Leave the instructions to the coach.
  • Stay quiet while your child has the ball. Cheer right after the play, not during it.
  • Cheer for every player. Good parents cheer for their own child. Great parents cheer for all of them.
  • Remember you do not know the plan. Even well meaning help can clash with what the coach asked for.
  • On the car ride home, saying nothing beats saying the wrong thing. Ask if they had fun and if they did their best, and tell them you enjoyed watching.

Rule oneCheer, do not coach

A young player is doing several hard things at once. They are reading the play, controlling the ball, listening for their coach and hearing their teammates. Every extra voice from the sideline competes with all of that.

Soccer Parenting sorts sideline behavior into three types: supportive parents who encourage all players and focus on effort, distracting parents who give instructions from the sideline and undermine the coach, and hostile parents who go after referees, coaches or players. It says shouted instructions interfere with players' focus, confuse them, and get in the way of learning to make their own decisions.

The players say the same thing. In a 2011 study by Camilla Knight, Kacey Neely and Nicholas Holt, 36 female athletes, with an average age of 13.5, described how they wanted their parents to behave. Twelve of them played soccer, and the rest played volleyball, basketball and other team sports. Twenty nine of them said parents should not coach during games, because the instructions were confusing and contradicted what the coach had said. Twenty eight did not want parents drawing attention to themselves. Thirty one found it embarrassing when parents argued with officials. All 36 wanted parents to keep their emotions under control and to focus on effort over outcome. It is a small study of girls across several sports, so treat it as a strong hint, not a law of nature.

A Soccer America column from Northern California offers a real world look. During COVID rules, parents had to sit behind fences, and at one venue the author describes, 40 or more yards from the field. The author, a referee, says the best part was "not seeing children playing nervously, as I often do when there's all that adult noise," and that "when coaches give good advice, the players can actually hear it." That is one person's observation, not research, but it fits what the players in the study reported.

Instead of shouting, try cheering

Words about effort and teamwork are safe. Words that tell a child what to do are not.

Rule twoStay quiet when your child has the ball

The moment a child gets the ball is the moment they have to decide something. Dribble, pass, shoot, turn. They often have only a moment, and they are already listening to their coach and teammates. A parent's voice can pull their eyes and attention toward the sideline at the worst possible time, and it puts a second opinion in their head. Then they are deciding between the coach's plan, your instruction and what they see on the field.

Mistakes are also how players learn. Our equal playing time guide makes the case that game time and repetitions matter more than winning today, and repetitions include the ones that go wrong. A child who hears "Shoot!" on every touch learns to look at the sideline. A child who is left alone is more likely to learn to look at the field.

So save the noise for right after the play. A clap, their name, and "Nice try!" tell them you saw it, without telling them what to do next time.

Rule threeGood parents cheer for their player. Great parents cheer for all players.

In the same 2011 study, all 36 athletes wanted parents to encourage the whole team. One player said of her mom, "she's not just clapping for me, she's clapping for the whole team. That's what I like." Another, comparing her volleyball and basketball teams, said parent support "makes you a closer team and you work harder together," a line MOJO Sports also quotes.

Here is what cheering for everyone looks like in practice:

  • Learn every child's name and use it. It takes a few games and it means a lot to a kid.
  • Cheer the substitute coming on as loudly as the starter.
  • Cheer for the goalkeeper after a goal against. That child feels worse than anyone on the field.
  • Notice the quiet contributions: the hustle back, the pass that led to the pass, the tackle nobody saw.
  • Applaud good play from either team, and never celebrate when a child on the other side makes a mistake or gets hurt.

This also helps your own child. They see what a good teammate looks like, and they learn that their team cares about every player, including the ones who play fewer minutes on a given day.

Rule fourYou do not know the plan

You may see the game clearly. What you cannot see is what the coach asked for this week. Here are a few examples of instructions a coach might have given, any of which a shout from the sideline could undo:

  • "Play the ball out from the back, even when it feels risky." Then a parent yells "Kick it!"
  • "Stay wide and wait for the pass." Then a parent yells "Get in there!"
  • "Try your left foot today." Then a parent yells "Just use your right!"
  • "You are playing a new position this week so you can learn it." Then a parent yells "Get back to where you belong!"

That last one is more common than most parents realize. US Youth Soccer's Player Development Model says "Players must be given the chance to play every position in soccer to deepen their understanding of the game," and that U-10, U-12 and U-14 players "should not begin to specialize in any position." Our formations guide explains why. A child in an unfamiliar spot may be doing exactly what the plan calls for, even when it looks strange from the sideline.

A 2021 study by Babett Lobinger and colleagues, published in the International Journal of Sports Science and Coaching, surveyed children, parents and coaches in youth soccer. Across its samples, which included players aged 8 to 16, children and parents were highly satisfied with parents' support and sideline behavior, while coaches' satisfaction differed significantly. The abstract does not say in which direction. One possible reading is that the help feels helpful from where you stand and can look different from where the coach stands, but that is our interpretation, not the authors'.

If you have a concern or an idea

Bring it to the coach, away from the game, at a good time such as after practice or by message. Try opening with "How can I best support the team?" before you offer an opinion. Do not approach the coach during the game, when both of you are watching the field. Our guide on talking to a coach about playing time covers a common version of this conversation.

Respect the referee

Referees at youth games are often young and still learning, and 31 of the 36 athletes in the Knight study found arguing with officials embarrassing. A Jr. NBA article drawing on Positive Coaching Alliance ideas suggests coaches ask parents to commit to "Honoring the Game no matter what," even when a call goes against the team, and to keep filling their child's "emotional tank." A good reminder is that the child on the field is watching how you handle a call you dislike.

When something is wrong

Cheer, do not coach has one clear exception: safety. If your child is hurt, or you think any child is in danger, step in or get the coach right away. If another adult is abusive toward a referee, a coach, or a child, do not confront them yourself. Tell the coach or a club or league official after the game, or right away if someone is at risk. Your club's own code of conduct comes first.

After the gameThe car ride home: saying nothing beats saying the wrong thing

The ride home is where a good day can turn into a bad one. Your child is tired, maybe disappointed, maybe thrilled, and every word you say carries extra weight. If you are unsure what to say, say nothing. Silence is far better than the wrong words.

When you do speak, you only need two questions and one sentence:

  1. "Did you have fun?"
  2. "Did you do your best?"
  3. "I enjoyed watching you play."

Those questions are the right ones because they match what young players say matters. George Washington University researcher Amanda Visek and her team studied 142 youth soccer players, 37 coaches and 57 parents and found 81 things that make sports fun. The top three fun factors were being a good sport, trying hard and positive coaching. According to the study's press release, winning ranked near the bottom. "Did you do your best?" asks about the second one, and "Did you have fun?" asks about all of them.

What you skip matters as much as what you say. The Aspen Institute's 2025 national survey of 3,827 young people aged 10 to 17 found that former players reported more negative parent behavior than current players. Twenty one percent of former players said they were pressured to play when they did not want to, compared with 9 percent of current players, and 18 percent said they were compared with other players, compared with 13 percent of current players. A survey cannot prove that the pressure caused anyone to quit, but the pattern is clear enough to guide how we talk on the ride home.

John O'Sullivan of the Changing the Game Project makes the same point in Soccer America. He writes that many children say parents' actions and conversations after games made them feel that their value and worth depended on how they played, and he recommends giving kids "the time and space to digest the game." He also warns against questions that seem innocent, such as asking why one teammate gets all the shots, because children hear them as criticism of a teammate or of the coach. In the Knight study, 33 of the 36 athletes wanted positive and realistic feedback after games, and the authors note that any constructive criticism should be provided away from others. The authors also report that athletes wanted some time with their teammates right after games.

If your child does want to talk about the game, be a quiet listener. Ask what they liked best and let them lead. The one exception is behavior. If they were disrespectful to a referee or teammate, speak with them about it as a parent, briefly, and then move on.

A note on "I love to watch you play"

Coach Bruce Brown of Proactive Coaching popularized the idea that this is the sentence young athletes most want to hear from their parents. Several sites repeat the claim that it came from a long running survey of college athletes. We could not trace the original data, so we present it as trusted coaching wisdom and not as a research finding. It is easy advice to follow either way. Say it, and mean it.

BonusGive your energy a job

Many parents who yell instructions are simply full of nervous energy and want to help. Soccer Parenting quotes a club coordinator who says her club encourages parents to take on roles like keeping stats, which keeps them engaged and less distracting. If nerves are the problem, standing with other parents, breathing out slowly, and giving yourself a job all help. Ask your coach whether they would like help. Handing out orange slices, running the scorebook or keeping one simple stat gives your hands and your attention something to do, and it helps the team.

Coaches who want to try this can read our guide on stat tracking with MatchTap, which shows how to give each parent a small set of stats to follow, using real numbers instead of feelings. Coaches can also send this page to team parents before the first game, and set the tone at a preseason parent meeting, so everyone starts from the same expectations. Our in game coaching guide is the coach's half of this same deal, and the guide on handling the sideline coaching parent covers what to do when a parent keeps instructing from the sideline.

Soccer moves fast, and it is normal to see a call or a moment you do not understand, a raised flag, a strange substitution pattern, a foul nobody explained. Asking an AI assistant about it after the game, rather than guessing out loud during the game, keeps rule one of this guide intact: cheer, do not coach. If instead you have a real concern or an idea for the coach, this guide's advice is to raise it away from the game, and an AI assistant can help you draft that message too.
