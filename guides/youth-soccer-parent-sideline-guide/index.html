Youth Soccer Sideline Guide for Parents: Cheer, Don't Coach

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 20, 2026 · 9 min read

The best thing you can do on the sideline is also the simplest. Cheer, stay positive, and let your child play. You do not need to say much, and you almost never need to give directions. This guide covers what to do during the game, why it helps, and what to say on the car ride home.

Who this guide is for

Parents of players in recreational and club soccer, at every age. Coaches are welcome to send this page to their team parents before the first game. If the terms coaches and referees use still sound like a foreign language, our soccer glossary for parents covers those.

The short version
  • Cheer, do not coach. Leave the instructions to the coach.
  • Stay quiet while your child has the ball. Cheer right after the play, not during it.
  • Cheer for every player. Good parents cheer for their own child. Great parents cheer for all of them.
  • Remember you do not know the plan. Even well meaning help can clash with what the coach asked for.
  • On the car ride home, saying nothing beats saying the wrong thing. Ask if they had fun and if they did their best, and tell them you enjoyed watching.

Rule oneCheer, do not coach

A young player is doing several hard things at once. She is reading the play, controlling the ball, listening for her coach and hearing her teammates. Every extra voice from the sideline competes with all of that.

Soccer Parenting sorts sideline behavior into three types: supportive parents who encourage all players and focus on effort, distracting parents who give instructions from the sideline and undermine the coach, and hostile parents who go after referees, coaches or players. It says shouted instructions interfere with players' focus, confuse them, and get in the way of learning to make their own decisions.

The players say the same thing. In a 2011 study by Camilla Knight, Kacey Neely and Nicholas Holt, 36 female athletes aged 12 to 15 in soccer, volleyball, basketball and other team sports described how they wanted their parents to behave. Twenty nine of them said parents should not coach during games, because the instructions were confusing and contradicted what the coach had said. Twenty eight did not want parents drawing attention to themselves. Thirty one found it embarrassing when parents argued with officials. All 36 wanted parents to keep their emotions under control and to focus on effort over outcome. It is a small study of girls, so treat it as a strong hint, not a law of nature.

A Soccer America column from Northern California offers a real world look. During COVID rules, parents had to watch from behind fences more than 40 yards from the field. The author saw players who looked less nervous and coaches who gave calmer instructions, noting that "when coaches give good advice, the players can actually hear it." That is one person's observation, not research, but it fits what the players in the study reported.

Instead of shouting, try cheering

Words about effort and teamwork are safe. Words that tell a child what to do are not.

Rule twoStay quiet when your child has the ball

The moment a child gets the ball is the moment she has to decide something. Dribble, pass, shoot, turn. She has about a second, and she is already listening to her coach and teammates. A parent's voice pulls her eyes and her attention toward the sideline at the worst possible time, and it puts a second opinion in her head. Then she is deciding between the coach's plan, your instruction and what she sees on the field.

Mistakes are also how players learn. Our equal playing time guide makes the case that game time and repetitions matter more than winning today, and repetitions include the ones that go wrong. A child who hears "Shoot!" on every touch learns to look at the sideline. A child who is left alone learns to look at the field.

So save the noise for right after the play. A clap, her name, and "Nice try!" tell her you saw it, without telling her what to do next time.

Rule threeGood parents cheer for their player. Great parents cheer for all players.

In the same 2011 study, all 36 athletes wanted parents to encourage the whole team, and one put it this way: "she's not just clapping for me, she's clapping for the whole team." In a summary of Knight's work, MOJO Sports quotes a player saying it "makes you a closer team and you work harder together."

Here is what cheering for everyone looks like in practice:

  • Learn every child's name and use it. It takes a few games and it means a lot to a kid.
  • Cheer the substitute coming on as loudly as the starter.
  • Cheer for the goalkeeper after a goal against. That child feels worse than anyone on the field.
  • Notice the quiet contributions: the hustle back, the pass that led to the pass, the tackle nobody saw.
  • Applaud good play from either team, and never celebrate when a child on the other side makes a mistake or gets hurt.

This also helps your own child. She sees what a good teammate looks like, and she learns that her team cares about every player, including the ones who play fewer minutes on a given day.

Rule fourYou do not know the plan

You may see the game clearly. What you cannot see is what the coach asked for this week. Here are a few examples of instructions a coach might have given, any of which a shout from the sideline could undo:

  • "Play the ball out from the back, even when it feels risky." Then a parent yells "Kick it!"
  • "Stay wide and wait for the pass." Then a parent yells "Get in there!"
  • "Try your left foot today." Then a parent yells "Just use your right!"
  • "You are playing a new position this week so you can learn it." Then a parent yells "Get back to where you belong!"

That last one is more common than most parents realize. Player development guidance from US Youth Soccer says young players should rotate through positions, and our formations guide explains why. A child in an unfamiliar spot is doing exactly what the plan calls for, even when it looks strange from the sideline.

A 2021 study by Babett Lobinger and colleagues, published in the International Journal of Sports Science and Coaching, surveyed children, parents and coaches in youth soccer. Children and parents were generally satisfied with parents' support on the sideline, while coaches' satisfaction differed significantly. In other words, the help feels helpful from where you stand and can look quite different from where the coach stands.

If you have a concern or an idea

Bring it to the coach, away from the game, at a good time such as after practice or by message. Try opening with "How can I best support the team?" before you offer an opinion. Do not approach the coach during the game, when both of you are watching the field.

Respect the referee

Referees at youth games are often young and still learning, and 31 of the 36 athletes in the Knight study found arguing with officials embarrassing. The Positive Coaching Alliance asks parents to commit to positive conduct even when a call goes against their team, and to keep filling their child's "emotional tank." A good reminder is that the child on the field is watching how you handle a call you dislike.

After the gameThe car ride home: saying nothing beats saying the wrong thing

The ride home is where a good day can turn into a bad one. Your child is tired, maybe disappointed, maybe thrilled, and every word you say carries extra weight. If you are unsure what to say, say nothing. Silence is far better than the wrong words.

When you do speak, you only need two questions and one sentence:

  1. "Did you have fun?"
  2. "Did you do your best?"
  3. "I enjoyed watching you play."

Those questions are the right ones because they match what young players say matters. George Washington University researcher Amanda Visek and her team studied 142 youth soccer players, 37 coaches and 57 parents and found 81 things that make sports fun. The top three fun factors were being a good sport, trying hard and positive coaching. Winning ranked near the bottom. "Did you do your best?" asks about the second one, and "Did you have fun?" asks about all of them.

What you skip matters as much as what you say. The Aspen Institute's 2025 national survey of 3,827 young people aged 10 to 17 found that former players reported more negative parent behavior than current players. Twenty one percent of former players said they were pressured to play when they did not want to, compared with 9 percent of current players, and 18 percent said they were compared with other players, compared with 13 percent of current players. A survey cannot prove that the pressure caused anyone to quit, but the pattern is clear enough to guide how we talk on the ride home.

John O'Sullivan of the Changing the Game Project makes the same point in Soccer America. He writes that many children say parents' actions and conversations after games made them feel that their value and worth depended on how they played, and he recommends giving kids "the time and space to digest the game." He also warns against questions that seem innocent, such as asking why one teammate gets all the shots, because children hear them as criticism of a teammate or of the coach. In the Knight study, 33 of the 36 athletes wanted positive and honest feedback delivered privately, and they wanted time with teammates before talking to a parent.

If your child does want to talk about the game, be a quiet listener. Ask what she liked best and let her lead. The one exception is behavior. If she was disrespectful to a referee or teammate, speak with her about it as a parent, briefly, and then move on.

A note on "I love to watch you play"

Coach Bruce Brown of Proactive Coaching popularized the idea that this is the sentence young athletes most want to hear from their parents. Several sites repeat the claim that it came from a long running survey of college athletes. We could not trace the original data, so we present it as trusted coaching wisdom and not as a research finding. It is easy advice to follow either way. Say it, and mean it.

BonusGive your energy a job

Many parents who yell instructions are simply full of nervous energy and want to help. Soccer Parenting suggests channeling it into a constructive role, such as keeping stats, instead of sideline commentary. Ask your coach whether they would like help. Handing out orange slices, running the scorebook or keeping one simple stat gives your hands and your attention something to do, and it helps the team.
