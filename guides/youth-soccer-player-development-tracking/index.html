Tracking Player Development Over a Season, Not Just One Game

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of MatchTap · September 23, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 12 min read

One game tells you almost nothing about a player. A quiet Saturday can follow a great week of practice, and a big game can follow a rough one. If you want to tell a parent something true about their child's development, you need a trend across several games, not a memory of the last one.

Who this guide is for

Coaches who already track games, with MatchTap or anything else, and want to turn those games into a season long picture for each player instead of a running impression.

The short version
  • One game is a small sample. Do not judge a player's development from a single performance, good or bad.
  • Pick one or two goals per player, tied to something they control, not the scoreboard.
  • Log the same one or two numbers after every game. MatchTap's player tagged events (in Player and Team individual modes) and per game CSV export make this quick for goals it has an event for. Count the rest by hand.
  • Check in every few games, not every game, so normal ups and downs do not look like real changes.
  • Show parents the trend, not a single number, and frame it around development rather than the scoreboard.

Why it mattersOne game does not tell you much

Sports statistics need a number of tries before they mean anything. A well known NFL example is the 2009 Denver Broncos, who started 6 and 0, then lost eight of their final ten games, finished 8 and 8 and missed the playoffs, according to CBC Sports. The hot start was not a preview of the season. It was a small sample that happened to run one direction before it evened out.

Young players are noisier than NFL teams. A single game folds in the opponent, the weather, how much sleep a ten year old got, and plain luck, on top of whatever skill they actually have. A run of check-ins tells you far more than one test day, so judge the trend across several games, not the headline from Saturday.

Step onePick one or two goals per player

Our first season guide covers picking one or two goals for the whole team before a game. This is the same idea aimed at one player across a whole season.

David Feigley of the Rutgers Youth Sports Research Council makes a related point about control. He separates what an athlete does control, where effort makes a difference, from what an athlete does not control, where effort makes no difference, and warns that athletes who miss the difference spend time and effort thinking about things they cannot control. A player cannot control whether the team wins. She can control whether she tries her strong foot, whether she calls for the ball, or whether she gets goal side of her mark before the ball arrives.

TrueSport's guide to setting goals with young athletes has players list their personal strengths and weaknesses before goals are set, and recommends displaying the goals in a locker or at home where the player will see them regularly. Our own advice is to agree on the goal together instead of assigning it. A goal nobody looks at again is not a goal, it is a wish.

Keep it small. One goal for most players, two at most for an older or more experienced one. A list of five things to fix will not fit in a ten year old's head during a game, and it will not fit in yours while you are also watching the whole field.

MatchTap counts a fixed list of events, so choose goals you can measure with one of them or with a simple hand tally. Goals like calls for the ball are worth tracking, but they live outside the app. Pick something you can count from the sideline while you are also coaching.

Do not set a goal that caps mistakes, such as losing the ball fewer than a set number of times. It teaches a child to stop dribbling and take fewer risks.

A note on age. For U6 to U8, the best goal is one the child can say in a sentence, such as "try to dribble past one player," and the numbers stay with you. Sharing counts with very young children can turn play into a score. From about U10, a player who is interested can look at her own trend. These are our coaching suggestions, not research findings.

Step twoLog the same number after every game

Once you have a goal, you need a consistent way to measure it, and this is where a match tracker earns its keep. If you use MatchTap in Team Individual mode, every event gets tagged with the player who did it, and every finished game is saved to Past Games with its own log. The CSV export includes a player column alongside the event and the time, so you can find one player's numbers inside any game you tracked without watching the game again.

Being direct about what MatchTap does and does not do: it gives you clean, player tagged numbers for one game at a time. It does not add those numbers up across a season for you. That part is on you, and it does not need to be complicated. A single page per player, on paper or in a notes app, with one row added after every game, is enough. A basic spreadsheet works too, and lets you paste in a new game's CSV and filter it down to one player's rows in a few seconds. Keep that page private, and use initials or jersey numbers instead of full names.

  • Track it during the game. Use Team Individual mode (or Player mode for a single child) so the event you care about gets tagged with the right player automatically. For a goal MatchTap has no event for, add a Note tied to the player or keep a tally on paper.
  • Pull the number after the game. Export the CSV or just check the Log screen, and count that player's events for the goal you set.
  • Add one row. Date, opponent, the number, and anything you noticed. That is the whole system.
  • Repeat for every game. The value is in the row you add today plus every row before it, not in any single row.

Step threeCheck in every few games, not every game

Reviewing a goal after every single game invites you to overreact to normal noise. TrueSport advises reviewing goals at various points in the season and says the end of season meetings are probably the most important. A good rhythm for most teams is a quick look every three or four games, plus a longer conversation at the midpoint and one at the end of the season.

When you check in, look at the shape of the numbers, not any single point. If a player's completed passes were 2, 1, 4, 3 and 6 over five games, the 1 does not mean she got worse. The overall climb is the story. When the numbers trend in the right direction over time, development becomes visible instead of vague.

Be ready to change the goal too. TrueSport is direct that goals "are not etched in stone," and a goal a player has clearly outgrown should be replaced with a harder one rather than kept just because it was written down first.

Step fourShow parents the trend, not a feeling

This is the payoff for the work above. Instead of telling a parent "she's coming along," you can show them five numbers going in the right direction, or explain honestly that a number has been flat and here is what you are both going to try next. If you also evaluate players for tryouts or team placement, our tryouts and evaluations guide covers how to do that fairly.

A few things make that conversation go well:

  • Set the frame at the start of the season. The Positive Coaching Alliance advises holding a preseason meeting to share your coaching philosophy, including the importance of valuing effort over winning. Add how you track progress to that meeting, before anyone is anxious about playing time.
  • Pick your moment. TeamSnap's advice on playing time conversations is blunt about timing: avoid them right before or after a game, and keep them private, not next to the field where other players or parents can hear.
  • Use the numbers to add context, not to win an argument. Show one child's own trend, never a ranking against a teammate.
  • Point the conversation forward. End with what the player can work on next, not just where they stand today. A good question for the player is "What is the next thing you want to work on?"

None of this requires software built for it. A screenshot of a simple table, or just reading the numbers out loud, is usually enough. What matters is that the numbers are real and collected the same way every time, which is exactly what a consistent per game habit with MatchTap gives you.

ExampleWhat a season of tracking looks like

Here is a simple example for one player with a goal of getting more touches on the ball by calling for it. MatchTap has no event for this, so the coach counts the calls with a Note or a tally on paper.

Game 3 looks like a step back if you only glance at the number. Looked at across all five games, it is a normal dip inside an upward trend. Five games is still a small sample, so keep logging before you declare success, and check the trend, not the last data point.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Tracking too many things. One number per player is plenty. A list of ten stats will not get updated after week two.
  • Choosing a goal you cannot count. If you cannot count it from the sideline or with a MatchTap event, pick a different goal.
  • Reacting to a single game. A bad number in one game is data, not a verdict.
  • Comparing players to each other. This is a record of one player against her own past games, not a leaderboard.
  • Never updating the goal. A goal a player mastered in October should not still be the goal in March.
  • Saving all the feedback for the end of the season. Check in along the way so nothing you say at the final meeting is a surprise.

Ask your AI assistant coachBuild the one page tracking template before the season starts

An AI assistant can help you choose goals with a player, build the tracking page, and draft the check in. The AI cannot see this guide or your app, so each prompt below carries what it needs. Privacy first: use jersey numbers or initials, or nothing identifying at all. Never put full names, photos, addresses, birthdates, or medical notes into a general AI chat. If you are new to prompting, our beginner guide to using Claude for soccer coaching shows how to give an AI enough context.

"I coach a [age group] [format] youth soccer team. A player, identified only as [initials or jersey number], has this strength and this thing to improve: [describe both in your own words]. Suggest three small goals this player could work on this season. Each goal must be something the player controls, not a result like winning, and something I can count from the sideline. For each goal, say how to count it. If it fits one of these app events, name the event: Pass completed, Pass not completed, Possession lost, Turnover, Dribble completed, Shot on goal, Shot missed, Shot blocked, Tackle won, Interception, Header won, Good decision. Otherwise say it needs a hand tally. Then write one sentence I can say to the player in words a [age] year old understands." Paste this at the start of the season, one player at a time
"I coach a [age group] [format] soccer team with [roster size] players. I want to log one number per player after every game, tied to a development goal. Here are the goals, using initials only: [list each player's initials and goal, and say whether it is an app event or a hand tally]. Build me a simple template for a spreadsheet or notes app, with one row per game per player and columns for date, opponent, the number, and a short note. Add a column for how the number was counted, so I stay consistent from game to game. Keep it to what I listed and do not add other stats." Paste this once the goals are chosen, before your first game
"A player on my [age group] team, identified only as [initials], has this development goal: [goal]. Here are the numbers from the last several games, oldest first: [paste the numbers, such as 2, 1, 4, 3, 6]. Work out the average of the first two and the last two games and show your arithmetic. Describe the trend honestly in two sentences, including any dip along the way, and tell me what I cannot conclude from this few games. Then draft a short, friendly summary for the parent that does not compare the child to any other player, and suggest one specific next thing to work on." Paste this every few games, with initials only and the real numbers pasted in

AI tools can miscount, so check the arithmetic against your own sheet before you share anything with a family. The tracking sheet and the check in summaries get reused all season, so keep full names out of both. MatchTap exports one game at a time, so combine the files yourself, and delete the player column or switch to jersey numbers before you give a file to any AI tool.
