Youth Soccer Positions Explained Simply: One Job at a Time, by Format

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · October 5, 2026 · 9 min read

Tell a group of eight year olds they are playing a 4-3-3 and watch what happens. Nothing happens, because the sentence meant nothing to them. Formation talk is built for coaches and grown players who already know what a midfielder does. Young players need something smaller: one job they can actually hold in their head while the ball is moving. This guide gives you that job, in plain words, and shows exactly how it changes as the game grows from 3v3 up through 11v11.

Who this guide is for

Coaches and parents who want a simple way to talk to young players about what to do on the field, without a whiteboard or a formation number. Pair this with our formations by age guide if you also want the coaching side, choosing which shape to teach toward.

Before we startNo one hands you these words

Here is the honest, slightly frustrating truth we found digging into this. US Soccer and US Youth Soccer both have real, official guidance about young players and positions, and it says a lot about what not to do. It does not say what to actually tell an eight year old instead. The US Youth Soccer Small Sided Games Manual will not even let 3v3 and 4v4 have a goalkeeper, stating plainly that those formats are played "with no goalkeepers." The US Soccer Coaching Curriculum says players at the U9 to U12 level "will rotate in two or three different positions to avoid early specialization," and it does not schedule real position specific technical training until U15, when "part of the technical training will be position specific (e.g. defender: passing, center midfield players: receiving to turn and strikers: finishing)."

So the people who write the actual youth development standards are extremely clear that young players should not be locked into named positions. What they never supply is the plain language you use instead. That gap gets filled by individual coaches, all differently, which is exactly why one team's "defender" gets told to never cross midfield while another team's defender is encouraged to join the attack. This guide is our attempt to fill that gap honestly. Nothing below is an official glossary. It is a simple, consistent way to talk about jobs on the field, built to match what the official guidance already tells you about each age and format.

The root ideaBefore any position, there are only two jobs

Underneath every formation, US Soccer's own coaching curriculum states one principle that applies at every age and every format: "all players attack and all players defend." That line is written for coaches, but the idea underneath it is simple enough for a six year old. There are only two jobs on a soccer field, ever. When your team has the ball, help it move forward. When the other team has the ball, help get it back. Everything else in this guide, every format and every named position, is just a more specific version of those same two jobs.

That is worth saying directly to your players, in almost exactly those words, at every age. It is also worth saying to yourself before you hand a young player a more specific job. If the specific job ever contradicts the two jobs underneath it, for example a defender who is told to never cross midfield even when the team desperately needs an extra body forward, the specific job is wrong, not the player.

U6 to U83v3 and 4v4: one rule, not a job

At this age, do not assign positions at all. That is not our opinion, it is what the format is built around. US Youth Soccer's own manual explains why in plain terms, noting that players at this age "are very egocentric" and that "the ball represents a toy that belongs to them" so "they don't share well." There are no goalkeepers in 3v3 or 4v4 for the same reason, every player is simply a player.

The only thing worth teaching here is one rule, not a job: find space. If your team has the ball, get away from the crowd so a teammate has somewhere to pass. If the other team has the ball, get closer to your own goal. That is it. Resist the urge to say "defender" or "forward" to a six year old. At this age, the two jobs from the section above are already enough, and trying to add more will only produce a kid standing still, confused, while eight players chase the ball in a clump.

Coaching cue: one short phrase repeated often works better than a paragraph of instruction. "Find space" or "get open" is something a five year old can act on mid game. A sentence about shape is not.

What to actually do once a player gets that space is its own question, and our dribbling before passing guide lays out the case for individual ball mastery being the priority at this age, with passing arriving later once the field gives players an actual reason to need it.

U9 to U107v7: the first three jobs actually exist

Seven players is the first format with a real goalkeeper, US Youth Soccer's manual lists it as "6 field players + 1 goalkeeper," and it is also the first format where enough players exist to make a defender, a midfielder, and a forward mean something different from each other. Recommended shapes at this stage typically look like a 1-2-3-1 or a 1-3-2-1, according to youth development guidance published by Florida Youth Soccer. This is where the two jobs from before start to split into simple, specific versions.

  • Goalkeeper. Protect the goal with your hands in the box. Once you have the ball, look for a teammate before you kick it far away. You are the first attacker, not just the last defender.
  • Defender. Be the last line before the goalkeeper. Win the ball back first, and only then think about passing forward. If you are unsure, stay goal side of the player you are marking.
  • Midfielder. You help both jobs. Get open for a pass when your team has the ball, and get back to help the defenders when it does not. You will run more than anyone else on the field, and that is the job.
  • Forward. Make yourself an option to score. When the other team has the ball, you are the first one pressuring their defenders, not standing and waiting for the ball to arrive.

Keep rotating every child through all four jobs across the season. Nothing above is a life sentence, it is a job for this game, maybe this half. If you have never played goalkeeper yourself, our goalkeeper basics guide covers exactly what to teach in the first few sessions in net.

U11 to U129v9: the same jobs get a direction

Nine players adds enough bodies for width to actually matter, and this is where recommended shapes shift to something like a 1-3-2-3 or a 1-3-3-2. No official curriculum we found names "wide player" as a taught concept at this age, so treat that specific label as our own addition, not a governing body term, though the shapes themselves come from published youth development guidance. What is official at this stage is a new emphasis on speed of thought. The US Youth Soccer manual says the smaller field and player count at this age "will require more concentration on transition, which a vitally important tactical concept for the players to learn," and it credits players at this age with "increased intellect and improved vision of the field," which is exactly why midfield play becomes a real teaching point here.

  • Wide player (defender or forward). Your job is the same as before, defend or attack, but now you own a side of the field. Stay wide to stretch the game, and get back to your side when the ball is lost.
  • Everyone, new addition: transition. The moment the ball changes teams matters as much as anything else. Win it, and the first thing you do is look forward before the other team can reset. Lose it, and the first thing you do is sprint to get behind the ball, not argue about the call.

The four core jobs from 7v7 do not disappear, they just get sharper. A defender now has a side of the field to own. A midfielder now has more room to actually connect the two ends. Keep talking in jobs, not formation numbers, even as the shapes on paper start to look like real systems.

U13 and up11v11: same two jobs, more specific tools

Eleven a side is the format most adults picture when they say "formation," and it is genuinely the first time a roster is big enough to split jobs like center back, fullback, holding midfielder, attacking midfielder, winger, and striker into meaningfully different work. It is worth knowing, though, that the official curriculum still does not rush this. Position specific technical training is not scheduled until U15, two full years after most players reach 11v11. Tim Bradbury, Director of Coaching Instruction for Eastern New York Youth Soccer, puts it more bluntly, writing that "any parent or coach who wishes to assign a player to a set position before 18 is ultimately hurting their development." Full specialization, in other words, is a high school idea, not a U13 one, even once the field and roster look like the real game.

With that caution in place, here is how we would explain the split jobs once a coach does start teaching them, in plain language rather than professional shorthand. None of this phrasing comes from a governing body, we built it to match the two jobs idea above.

  • Center back versus fullback. A center back protects the middle in front of the goal, wins the ball in the air and on the ground, and rarely gambles. A fullback does the same defending job on one side, but is allowed to run forward when the game opens up, as long as they get back before the other team can punish it.
  • Holding midfielder versus attacking midfielder. A holding midfielder's first job is to stop the other team's attack before it reaches the defenders, and to keep passes simple once they have the ball. An attacking midfielder's first job is to find or create a scoring chance, and their passing can afford to take more risk.
  • Winger versus striker. A winger's job starts wide, stretching the field and beating a defender one on one before delivering the ball centrally. A striker's job is to be the one waiting to finish what the winger, or anyone else, creates.

Every one of those six jobs is still just a specific version of attack or defend. Say that out loud to your players when you hand out a new job for the season. It keeps the number on the whiteboard from feeling like a cage.

Watch outWhat not to do

  • Naming a position before 7v7, when the format itself is not built to support one.
  • Handing a young player a pro team's formation number without translating what the job actually is.
  • Punishing a player for leaving "their" spot, especially before the mid teens, when official guidance still expects rotation.
  • Treating a 9v9 or 11v11 job as permanent, when even the most specialized job on this page is one your player should keep rotating out of for years yet.
  • Explaining a job once and never again. Young players forget under pressure. Repeat the same short phrase from the sideline, calmly, all game.
