Youth Soccer Positions Explained Simply: One Job at a Time, by Format

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 24, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 12 min read

Tell a group of eight year olds they are playing a 4-3-3 and watch what happens. Nothing happens, because the sentence meant nothing to them. Formation talk is built for coaches and grown players who already know what a midfielder does. Young players need something smaller: one job they can actually hold in their head while the ball is moving. This guide gives you that job, in plain words, and shows exactly how it changes as the game grows from 3v3 up through 11v11.

Who this guide is for

Coaches and parents who want a simple way to talk to young players about what to do on the field, without a whiteboard or a formation number. Pair this with our formations by age guide if you also want the coaching side, choosing which shape to teach toward. A note on notation: formations in this guide count the goalkeeper, so 1-3-2-1 means 1 goalkeeper, 3 defenders, 2 midfielders and 1 forward. The formations guide leaves the goalkeeper out, so the same shape is written 3-2-1 there.

The short version
  • Underneath every position there are only two jobs: when your team has the ball, help move it forward, and when the other team has it, help get it back.
  • Through 4v4, there are no positions and no goalkeeper. Teach one rule: find space.
  • At 7v7, four jobs exist: goalkeeper, defender, midfielder and forward. Rotate every child through all of them.
  • At 9v9, the same jobs gain a side of the field, and wide players appear.
  • At 11v11, jobs split further, but U.S. Soccer's age group guides through U16 still encourage players to play different positions.
  • Talk in jobs, not formation numbers, and repeat one short phrase, calmly, all game.

Before we startNo one hands you these words

Official youth guidance says a lot about what not to do with young players and positions, and very little about what to say instead. US Youth Soccer's 2017 standards play 4v4 with no goalkeepers. U.S. Soccer's page for 7v7 says players "should be encouraged to try all positions," and its age group guides for U13 through U16 still say to keep encouraging players to play different positions. What none of them supplies is plain language for an eight year old, so that gap gets filled by individual coaches, all differently. This guide is our attempt to fill it. Nothing below is an official glossary. It is a simple, consistent way to talk about jobs, built to match what the official pages say about each format.

The root ideaBefore any position, there are only two jobs

Underneath every formation there are only two jobs on a soccer field, ever. When your team has the ball, help it move forward. When the other team has the ball, help get it back. U.S. Soccer's 9v9 page says each player is given "a position and roles and responsibilities with and without the ball," which is the same idea in coach language. Everything else in this guide, every format and every named position, is just a more specific version of those two jobs.

It is worth saying directly to your players, in almost exactly those words, at every age. It is also worth saying to yourself before you hand a young player a more specific job. If the specific job ever contradicts the two jobs underneath it, for example a defender who is told to never cross midfield even when the team desperately needs an extra body forward, the specific job is wrong, not the player.

U6 to U83v3 and 4v4: one rule, not a job

At this age, do not assign positions at all. That is not just our opinion, it is what the format is built around. US Youth Soccer's 2012 manual (we could only link an unofficial copy) describes four and five year olds as "very egocentric," with the ball as "a toy that belongs to them," and describes U8 players as starting to share the ball more. The manual plays 3v3 and 4v4 with no goalkeepers, and the 2017 standards list none for 4v4 at U6 to U8, so every player is simply a player.

The only thing worth teaching here is one rule, not a job: find space. If your team has the ball, get away from the crowd so a teammate has somewhere to pass. If the other team has the ball, get closer to your own goal. That is it. Resist the urge to say "defender" or "forward" to a six year old. The two jobs from the section above are already enough, and trying to add more will only produce a kid standing still, confused, while eight players chase the ball in a clump.

Coaching cue: one short phrase repeated often works better than a paragraph of instruction. "Find space" or "get open" is something a five year old can act on mid game. A sentence about shape is not.

What to actually do once a player gets that space is its own question, and our dribbling before passing guide lays out the case for individual ball mastery being the priority at this age, with passing arriving later once the field gives players an actual reason to need it.

U9 to U107v7: the first real jobs exist

Seven players is the first format with a real goalkeeper in the current U.S. Soccer formats, and it is also the first with enough players for a defender, a midfielder and a forward to mean something different from each other. U.S. Soccer's page for U9 to U10 lists a 1-3-2-1, which is one goalkeeper, three defenders (a central back and two wing backs), two midfielders and one forward, and says other formations may be used. Notice that it describes the goalkeeper and the defenders as supporting the attack. The jobs below keep that idea.

  • Goalkeeper. Protect the goal with your hands in the box. Once you have the ball, look for a teammate before you kick it far away. You are the first attacker, not just the last defender.
  • Defender. You are the players closest to your own goal. When the other team has the ball, win it back and stay between the ball and your goal. When your team has it, help it move forward: pass to a teammate and move up with the team.
  • Midfielder. You help both jobs. U.S. Soccer's shape has one midfielder who is a little more attacking and one who is a little more defensive. In kid words, get open for a pass when your team has the ball, and get back to help when it does not. You will run more than anyone else on the field, and that is the job.
  • Forward. Make yourself an option to score. When the other team has the ball, you are the first one pressuring their defenders, not standing and waiting for the ball to arrive.

Keep rotating every child through all four jobs across the season, and never force a child into goal. Ask for volunteers first. Nothing above is a life sentence, it is a job for this game, maybe this half. If you have never played goalkeeper yourself, our goalkeeper basics guide covers exactly what to teach in the first few sessions in net.

U11 to U129v9: the same jobs get a side of the field

Nine players adds enough bodies for width to actually matter. U.S. Soccer's page for U11 to U12 lists a 1-3-2-3: one goalkeeper, a back line of three (one central back and two outside backs), two staggered midfielders, and three forwards (one central and two wide). It says 9v9 adds wing roles compared with 7v7, and that other formations may be used.

  • Wide player (outside back or wide forward). Your job is the same as before, defend or attack, but now you own a side of the field. Stay wide so your teammates have room to play, and get back to your side when the ball is lost.
  • Everyone, one more habit: the moment the ball changes teams. Win it, and look forward before the other team can reset. Lose it, and sprint to get behind the ball instead of arguing about the call. That habit is a coaching idea we add here, not a U.S. Soccer 9v9 term.

The four core jobs from 7v7 do not disappear, they just get sharper. A defender now has a side of the field to own. A midfielder now has more room to actually connect the two ends. Keep talking in jobs, not formation numbers, even as the shapes on paper start to look like real systems.

U13 and up11v11: same two jobs, more specific tools

Eleven a side is the format most adults picture when they say "formation," and it is the first time a roster is big enough to split jobs like center back, fullback, holding midfielder, attacking midfielder, winger and striker into meaningfully different work. It is worth knowing, though, that official guidance still does not rush this. U.S. Soccer's age group guide for U13 to U14 introduces a 1-4-3-3 and still says to continue to encourage players to play in different positions. At U15 to U16 it lists individual skills linked to playing position as a focus, and says the same thing about different positions. Even once the field and roster look like the real game, the official direction is rotation first, specialization slowly.

With that caution in place, here is how we would explain the split jobs once a coach does start teaching them, in plain language rather than professional shorthand. None of this phrasing comes from a governing body. We built it to match the two jobs idea above.

  • Center back versus fullback. A center back protects the middle in front of the goal, wins the ball in the air and on the ground, and rarely gambles. A fullback does the same defending job on one side, but is allowed to run forward when the game opens up, as long as they get back before the other team can punish it.
  • Holding midfielder versus attacking midfielder. A holding midfielder stays in front of the defenders and stops the other team's attacks before they reach them, then keeps passes simple. An attacking midfielder's first job is to find or create a scoring chance, and their passing can afford to take more risk.
  • Winger versus striker. A winger's job starts wide, spreading the field and beating a defender one on one before delivering the ball to the middle. A striker's job is to be the one waiting to finish what the winger, or anyone else, creates.

Every one of those six jobs is still just a specific version of attack or defend. Say that out loud to your players when you hand out a new job for the season. It keeps the number on the whiteboard from feeling like a cage.

When a player loves one position

Sooner or later a child will ask to play the same spot every game, usually the one where they score or the one that feels safe. You do not have to say no outright. Let them play it for part of the game, give them a different job for the rest, and tell them why: every job teaches something that makes them better at the one they love. That keeps the rotation in place without turning it into a punishment.

Watch outWhat not to do

  • Naming a position before 7v7, when U.S. Soccer's 4v4 diamond is there to teach height, width and depth, not fixed jobs.
  • Telling defenders and the goalkeeper to just clear the ball, when U.S. Soccer's own shapes have both of them helping the attack.
  • Handing a young player a pro team's formation number without translating what the job actually is.
  • Punishing a player for leaving "their" spot, especially before the mid teens, when official guidance still expects rotation.
  • Treating a 9v9 or 11v11 job as permanent, when official guidance through U16 still encourages players to try different positions.
  • Explaining a job once and never again. Young players forget under pressure. Repeat the same short phrase from the sideline, calmly, all game.
