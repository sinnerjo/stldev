Roster Size Math: How Many Players to Actually Carry

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 22, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 12 min read

Roster size feels like a simple number until the week you show up with two players sick, one on a family trip, and one still in a boot from last week's game, and suddenly your 11v11 team has nine players for a game that wants eleven, which means playing short handed with nobody to sub. Getting this right before the season starts is mostly arithmetic: your format's field size, how much bench you want for a real rotation, and a buffer for the players who will not show up on a given Saturday, whether you know it yet or not.

Who this guide is for

Coaches building a roster before the season starts, or realizing midseason that theirs is too thin or too thick. This assumes you already have a playing time standard in mind, ours is covered in the equal playing time guide, and a rotation plan, covered in the substitution patterns guide. Roster size is the number that makes both of those actually work on a Saturday.

The short version
  • Lean toward a smaller roster when you can. Every extra player on the roster is real minutes taken from someone else.
  • There is no governing body formula for the absence buffer. Track who actually shows up for three or four weeks and plan for your average number of absences plus one.
  • Know your league's actual minimum to avoid a forfeit, not just the general rule you have heard secondhand.
  • A big club's real advantage is depth, more teams and coaches to lean on, but guest rules are mostly written for tournaments, so do not plan around borrowing a player for a league game.
  • A small club or rec team without that safety net should carry more, since a thin roster with no backup plan is the version of this that actually forfeits games.

The mathThree numbers, not one

Roster size math has three separate inputs, and most of the frustration with a bad roster comes from only thinking about one of them. The first is your format's field size, seven for 7v7, nine for 9v9, eleven for 11v11, which is fixed and not up for debate. The second is how much bench you want for a real rotation, enough players that your sub pattern spreads minutes around instead of running the same seven kids into the ground. The third is a buffer for the players who will not be there on a given day, sick, hurt, out of town, or just done for the season, which is the number coaches most often skip until it costs them a game.

Add the three together and you get a roster size. Here is one example for each format. At 7v7, that is 7 on the field, 3 on the bench and a buffer of 1, so 11 players. At 9v9, it is 9, 3 and 2, so 14. At 11v11, it is 11, 4 and 3, so 18. Skip the third number and you get a roster that works fine for six weeks and then does not. Your own bench and buffer will differ, and the sections below show how to size them.

What the numbers sayPublished roster sizes, where they actually exist

U.S. Soccer's 2017 Player Development Initiatives, as hosted by US Youth Soccer, put numbers on two of the three small sided formats. They say that for 7v7, "Rosters should include no more than 12 players," and for 9v9, "Rosters should include no more than 16 players." These are recommendations, not rules. The document presents them as best practice, not mandated, and they describe a ceiling meant to protect playing time, not a number every team should aim for. Check what your league actually enforces.

For 11v11, U.S. Youth Soccer's national policy (Rule 205) allows up to 22 players on the team roster and a game day roster of no more than 18, with a minimum of 7. State associations can vary these numbers. Utah Youth Soccer's 2016 table, for example, lists up to 12 players at 7v7 (U9 and U10), up to 15 at 9v9 (U11 and U12), up to 18 at 11v11 for U13 to U15, and up to 22 for U16 and older. Your actual ceiling is whatever your own league says it is, so check your own rulebook before you plan a number.

The national policy also separates the full roster from the game day roster. If your league does the same, ask how it works, because a player left off the game day roster plays zero minutes, which collides with any playing time standard. Plan equal time around the number who are active, not the number on the list.

The hard floorWhat it actually takes to avoid a forfeit

The number that matters more than any ceiling is the floor, the point at which a game cannot continue at all. For 11v11, the Laws of the Game state that "a match may not start or continue if either team has fewer than seven players." Seven, for an eleven player format, is the number to know cold. Leagues often add their own rules about the minimum to start, so check yours.

For 7v7 and 9v9, there is no equivalent national rule that we could confirm, so ask your league for its minimum to start rather than assuming a number.

Know the difference between legal and workable, though. Seven players is the point where the game ends, not the point where it is playable. An 11v11 team with nine players is legal, but it has no one to sub, plays two men down, and tires quickly. Treat the floor as the number you must never reach, not a plan.

The real costA bigger roster is fewer minutes for everyone

Here is the part a lot of roster discussions skip. Total playing minutes in a game are fixed: the game length times the number of players on the field. Every player you add to the roster divides that fixed pool into smaller pieces. This is the same math behind our equal playing time guide. The share of the game each player gets is the number on the field divided by the roster size, and a roster padded past what your format and sub plan actually need takes minutes away from every player on the team, not just the ones on the bench.

That is the whole case for leaning smaller when your situation allows it. A 9v9 team with 12 players gives every kid 75% of the game. With 14 players, it is 64%. In a 60 minute game, that is about 6 more minutes per player for the smaller roster. The tradeoff is real, though. A smaller roster has less room to absorb the week two players are out, which is why the buffer matters, and why it depends on what kind of club you coach for.

There is also a ceiling that comes from the arithmetic itself. If every player is to get at least half of every game, the roster cannot be larger than twice the number on the field: 14 for 7v7, 18 for 9v9 and 22 for 11v11. At 7v7 and 9v9, the recommended maximums above (12 and 16) are lower, so they are the ones to plan around.

Honest gapWhy there is no clean formula for the buffer

We looked for real data to justify a specific buffer number, an actual study on how often youth players miss a game or practice, and could not find one. What exists is coach anecdote, not a citable study, so we are not going to dress up a guess as science. Injury data does exist. One review of youth football studies found an overall rate of 5.7 injuries per 1,000 hours for males and 6.8 for females, and the match rate was about five times the training rate, 14.4 against 2.8 for males and 15.0 against 2.6 for females. That tells you injuries cluster in games, but it is an average across many studies and does not convert into "carry two extra players."

So size it from your own team. For the first three or four weeks, write down how many players are missing from each practice and game. Take the average number of absences and add one, so you can still cover two players out at once. Say you coach 9v9, want 3 on the bench, and average 1.5 absences. Then 9 plus 3 plus 1.5 plus 1 comes to 14.5, so round up to 15, or to 14 if you accept a tighter margin. That is a judgment call, but it is grounded in your own attendance and the published numbers above, not a number borrowed from someone else's team.

Club sizeBig club versus small club changes the math

A real advantage of coaching at a medium or large club is depth. More teams in your age group usually means more relationships between coaches, and a realistic chance of borrowing a player from a sister team when someone gets hurt. That is a genuine benefit worth factoring into your roster plan, and it is one of the better reasons to prefer a bigger club if you have the choice.

It is worth being precise about what that actually is before you build a season around it. Guest player rules exist, and we cover them in our guest players and tournament rosters guide, but U.S. Youth Soccer's national policy treats guests as an exception for tournament competitions, and state and league rules vary. Do not count on being allowed to borrow a player for an ordinary league game. Some clubs may arrange something informally, but treat that as a soft advantage of club culture, not a rule you can lean on the way you can lean on your own bench. If you coach at a small club or in a recreational league without that depth to borrow from, carry more of your own buffer. For families weighing whether a bigger club is worth it for reasons beyond roster depth, our recreational to competitive club guide covers what else actually changes.

Real teamsThree things the table leaves out

  • Goalkeepers. Carry at least two players who are willing to play in goal, so an absence or an injury does not leave you without one.
  • Adding a player midseason. Every addition lowers everyone's share. Check the table above before you say yes, and tell the parents of your current players why.
  • Playing short. If you are one or two players short on a given day, you do not forfeit, but you lose your bench. Plan how you will rotate in advance, and our substitution patterns guide shows how to rebuild a rotation with fewer names.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Building a roster around a published maximum instead of your own format, sub plan, and situation.
  • Counting on a guest player who may not actually be available under your league's real rules.
  • Carrying so many players that even a full roster with zero absences cannot hit your own playing time standard.
  • Guessing your absence rate instead of tracking it for a few weeks, and waiting until two players are out at once to discover the roster cannot absorb it.
  • Treating the legal minimum to avoid a forfeit as a comfortable number, rather than the absolute last resort it is.
  • Copying a roster size from a different format or a different club's situation instead of running the actual math for your own.
