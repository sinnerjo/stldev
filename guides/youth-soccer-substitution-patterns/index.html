Substitution Patterns That Actually Work: A Rotation System for 7v7, 9v9, and 11v11

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 21, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 17 min read

Knowing that playing time should be equal is the easy part. Actually delivering it during a live fifty to ninety minute game, while also coaching, watching the ball, and half listening to a parent behind you, is where good intentions fall apart. Either you lose track and one kid quietly gets shorted, or you sub so often and so randomly that the game turns into a substitution circus, both benches waving players on and off every couple of minutes. A written system fixes both problems before kickoff.

Who this guide is for

Coaches who already have a playing time policy, covered in our equal playing time guide, and want an actual system for executing it during games without losing track or disrupting the flow of play. If you are brand new to coaching entirely, our first season guide covers the broader basics first. If your bench never seems big enough to run a real rotation, our roster size math guide covers how many players to actually carry.

The short version
  • Pick one system before the season, not in the middle of your first game.
  • Let your roster size set the stretch length. The time a player spends on the field compared with the bench should match players on the field compared with players on the bench. Ten minutes on and 5 off is a good fit only for a roster about one and a half times the size of your lineup.
  • Adjust for age. Younger players need a quicker way back onto the field. Older players can handle a longer bench stretch if it is paired with a longer turn on the field.
  • Sub at planned checkpoints, in one round, not one player at a time. The size of the round follows the size of your bench.
  • Think ahead about who is coming back in, not just who is going out, especially when two players from the same thin position group come off together.
  • Write your plan down before kickoff, and sub because of the plan, not because of a mistake that just happened on the field.
  • Tell a player before the whistle, and tell parents before the season, so everyone understands why a sub happens when it does.

The problemWhy substitutions turn into chaos

The chaos is not really about fairness. It is about frequency and timing. Soccer America columnist Mike Woitalla describes the common failure mode plainly: without a plan, one team subs a couple of players, then a minute or less later the other team subs back, and the cycle repeats until a game has 20 to 30 sub breaks in a 50 or 60 minute game. Tony Lepore, then U.S. Soccer's director of scouting, is quoted in the same piece and sums up the result: "with constant stoppages, the games have no flow, no pace." Every one of those stoppages also gives you one more moment to lose track of who actually went in, and for how long.

The fix is not subbing less often out of principle. It is subbing on a plan instead of on impulse, so the number of stoppages stays reasonable and every player still gets their share.

RhythmHow long should a stretch on the field actually be

Before you decide when to sub, decide how long a player should stay on before coming off at all. My own default is 10 minutes on the field and 5 on the bench, then the cycle starts over. A pattern like 5 on and 5 off looks fair on paper, but in practice it is too many interruptions. A player barely gets into the game, starts reading where the ball is going to be, maybe links up with a teammate for the first time, and then gets pulled right as it starts paying off. Do that every five minutes for an entire game and nobody ever gets far enough into a stretch of play to build real continuity.

This is not just a personal preference. Tony Lepore, then U.S. Soccer's director of scouting, made the same case publicly, warning that constant substitutions take "away the opportunity for our youngest players to play real soccer," and arguing plainly that "in order to get better at playing, you need to play, for extended, uninterrupted periods." When U.S. Soccer's Development Academy (discontinued in 2020) issued substitution guidelines for U12 teams, it suggested changes around the 15 minute mark, halftime and the 45 minute mark of a 60 minute game, with both teams subbing together, on the idea that less frequent interruptions help development. It did not ask coaches to sub more often. It asked them to sub less. No study I am aware of has directly tested 10 and 5 against 5 and 5, so treat the exact numbers as a starting point, not a law.

Let your roster set the numbers

Here is the part most guides skip. If every player is to get the same time, the ratio of time on the field to time on the bench has to match the ratio of players on the field to players on the bench. Ten on and 5 off is 2 to 1, or two thirds of the game on the field. That fits a roster about one and a half times the size of your lineup, which is roughly 10 or 11 players at 7v7, 13 or 14 at 9v9, and 16 or 17 at 11v11. With a bigger roster the bench stretch gets longer compared with the field stretch, and no pattern can fix that.

Adjust the stretch by age

The right stretch is not the same for a 7 year old and a 13 year old. US Youth Soccer's practice activities guide lists a short attention span as a trait of U6 players and describes U8 players as having a longer attention span that is "still not extended." That is exactly why the youngest players need a quick way back into the game rather than a long wait on the sideline.

In practice, younger teams should keep both numbers small, a short stretch on the field paired with a short turnaround on the bench, so nobody sits long enough to disengage. Older, more focused players can handle a longer bench stretch, but only if you pay it back with a longer stretch on the field once they are back on, not just a longer wait for the same short shift. A trade of more bench time for more field time is fair. A trade of more bench time for the same short shift is not, and it is the version that actually causes complaints.

The systemSplit each half into thirds

A simple rule of thumb is to make the first change about a third of the way through each half and the second about two thirds of the way through, on top of the natural break at halftime. That gives you a repeatable rhythm: each half has three blocks, with two planned changes at about minutes 8 and 17 of a 25 minute half, instead of a constant trickle of one off changes. The same checkpoints repeat in the second half. You do not need to sub every player at every checkpoint. You need a moment, planned in advance, where you look at your rotation and make whatever changes it calls for. Think of the checkpoints as the answer to when you look at your rotation, and the ratio from the table above as the answer to who is due at each one.

FormatTypical half lengthCheckpoints per half
7v7, U9 to U10 25 minutes Roughly the 8 and 17 minute marks, plus halftime.
9v9, U11 to U12 30 minutes Roughly the 10 and 20 minute marks, plus halftime.
11v11, U13 and up Varies by league and age Roughly a third and two thirds of the way through, plus halftime.

Those minute marks are targets, not a whistle you blow yourself. A simple way to track them is to set one timer for the half length and another for the checkpoint. When the checkpoint timer goes off, wait for the next stoppage and make your changes. The timer tells you a checkpoint has arrived. The referee's whistle, not your phone, still decides the exact moment you act on it. If your league plays running quarters instead of two halves, each quarter break is already a built in window, so treat every one as a checkpoint.

ShapeSub in rounds, not one at a time

A single substitution rarely breaks anything. A stream of them, one player at a time over several minutes, is what actually disrupts a team's shape, since every change resets whatever understanding the players on the field just built with each other. Bundling your changes into one round at a planned checkpoint means the team settles into a new shape once every eight to ten minutes instead of adjusting to something different every few minutes all game long. It also uses one stoppage instead of several, and gives the other team fewer chances to make changes in response.

How big should a round be? It follows your bench. With a bench of two or three, a round of two or three is natural. With a bench of five, as in the chart above, a round changes up to five players, and that is fine, because it is still one stoppage. If a round of five feels like too much disruption, the honest answer is a smaller roster or a longer shift, not a smaller round. Rounds of two or three with a bench of five means some players wait much longer than others.

Rounds only work if you also think one step ahead, past the swap you are about to make and into the one it creates. Pulling two midfielders in the same round looks efficient in the moment, but if your roster only carries four midfielders total, you have just committed both of your remaining midfielders to playing the rest of that stretch without a break of their own. If a position group is thin, move one player from it per round instead of two, and use the rest of the round to cover different positions rather than stacking multiple changes on the one spot with the least depth.

CoordinationSub when the other team subs, when you can

Part of what causes a cascade of stoppages is that both benches are reacting to each other instead of working from their own plan. In the Development Academy's guidance for U12, both teams substituted at the same planned points, and opposing coaches were encouraged to coordinate their changes. You cannot control when the other coach decides to sub, but offering to make changes together at a natural stoppage costs you nothing, and many coaches will agree, since it saves them a stoppage too.

Before kickoffWrite it down, do not carry it in your head

A rotation plan that only exists in your head falls apart under the pressure of an actual game, where you are also managing the score, an injury, and whoever is talking to you from the sideline. Write the plan down before you get to the field. It does not need to be complicated. A chart like the one above works. So does a lineup sheet with two names per position, one above the other, where the name on top starts and the name below is the substitute, so at each checkpoint you already know who is due in without reconstructing it from memory.

Bring that sheet to the field, and update it as the game actually unfolds rather than trusting yourself to remember an injury substitution or a change from three checkpoints ago. A rotation plan is only useful if it still reflects reality by the second half.

Real gamesWhen the plan meets the game

  • The goalkeeper. Decide before kickoff who plays in goal in each half, and put it on the sheet. Changing keepers at halftime is easiest, since it costs no extra stoppage. At 11v11 club level, where a dedicated keeper is common, the keeper plays the whole game and your rotation is about the outfield players.
  • An absent player. Rebuild the table before kickoff with one fewer name. The bench shrinks, so everyone plays a little more, and the ratios in the table above change.
  • A late arrival. Slot the player in at the next checkpoint and count the time they missed when you check minutes at halftime.
  • An injury or illness mid game. Replace the player with whoever on your bench has had the least time, then recount at the next checkpoint rather than rebuilding the whole plan. If a head injury is possible, take the player off, do not let them return that day, and follow your league's concussion policy. A rotation plan never overrides that.
  • What your league allows. Rules vary on when substitutions can happen. U.S. Soccer's Player Development Initiatives describe unlimited substitutions at any time in 4v4 and at any stoppage in 7v7 and 9v9. The St. Louis Youth Soccer 2026/27 playing rules we checked allow unlimited substitutions at standard stoppages at the referee's discretion. Confirm your own league's rules and tell your referee your plan if it helps.
  • The score. The plan does not change because you are ahead or behind. A comfortable lead is a good moment to give your lowest minute players more time.

DisciplineSub because of the plan, not because of a mistake

I am a huge believer in this one. Once you have a plan, stick to it, even when a player just gave the ball away or missed an open shot. The temptation in the moment is to pull that player right then, as if the substitution were a consequence. Resist it. If your plan says they come off at the next checkpoint anyway, let the plan run. If it says they still have five more minutes, let them play through the mistake.

The closest research is a small qualitative study of seven youth ice hockey goalies aged 13 to 15. It found that "benching can be detrimental to an athlete's feeling of self-worth and relations with coaches and teammates when experienced as a form of punishment," and players described feeling like an outcast on their own team. It covers one position in one sport and measured feelings, not decisions, so treat it as a caution rather than proof. The coaching logic connects cleanly to it, though. A player who watches a mistake get punished with a hook learns to avoid the kind of mistake that comes from trying something hard, a risky pass, a dribble at a defender, a shot from a tighter angle, because the downside of trying and failing now includes the bench.

Take that fear out of the equation and you get the opposite. A player who knows their time on the field is locked to a plan, not to their last touch, is free to try the difficult thing. Some of those attempts will fail, and that is the point. Failing at a hard pass and staying on the field to see what happens next is how a player learns to read the situation better next time.

CommunicationTell players before the whistle, tell parents before the season

Walk over and tell a player they are coming off at the next stoppage before that stoppage actually arrives, rather than waving them off the second play stops. A player who sees it coming jogs off with a plan for what to work on next. A player who is surprised mid play reads it as a punishment for whatever just happened on the field, even when it was simply their turn.

The same courtesy applies to parents, just earlier. Whatever system you land on, checkpoints, stretch lengths, groups, explain it before the season starts, ideally at the same preseason meeting where you cover playing time more broadly. A parent who understands the plan in advance can recognize a routine substitution for what it is. A parent who has never heard the plan is far more likely to read that same substitution as a benching, especially if it happens to land right after their kid's mistake.

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Subbing on memory alone, with no written plan to catch what you inevitably forget by the second half.
  • Making one change at a time, repeatedly, instead of one planned round at each checkpoint.
  • Reacting to the other bench instead of working from your own checkpoints.
  • Copying a stretch length that does not fit your roster or your age group, such as 10 on and 5 off with a bench of seven.
  • Stacking multiple subs from the same thin position group in one round, without a plan for getting all of them back through the rotation.
  • Pulling a player the instant a mistake happens, which reads as punishment even when the timing was already planned, and quietly teaches them to stop taking risks.
  • Never explaining the system to parents, so a routine, planned substitution gets read as a benching.
