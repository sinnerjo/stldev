Running a Team Social Media Page: A Record of Who They Are, Not the Scoreboard

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · October 8, 2026 · 8 min read

A team page is not a scoreboard and it is not a highlight reel for the coach. Done well, it becomes something parents actually want to see and share, a running record of who their kid is becoming, not just how the last game went. This is the system we use, what it protects against, and why the score never makes the post.

Who this guide is for

Coaches or team managers running a page for one team, on whatever platform your parents already use. Nothing here requires a budget, a camera beyond a phone, or an assigned team media person.

Rule oneThe score never makes the post

Not the final score, not the season record, not "we are now 6 and 2." Even a tournament or league championship gets celebrated for what it meant, not what it says on paper: the team that showed up early all season, the players who tried a new position and it worked, the group that kept its composure after a rough start to the day. A parent scrolling the page should come away with a feeling about their kid and their team, not a stat line they could have gotten from the league standings page.

This is not just a style preference. Our burnout guide covers real research on this directly: a motivational climate built around outcome and winning showed no measurable benefit to player engagement, while a climate built around mastery and effort, from parents, coaches, and peers alike, was linked to lower burnout and higher engagement. A team page is one more place that climate gets built or undermined, post by post. If every post is about the result, the page is teaching the same lesson as a coach who only talks about the scoreboard.

Before you post anythingProtect players first

This part matters more than anything else in this guide, and it is worth doing before your first post rather than after a parent asks you to take something down. A few real, sourced practices:

  • Get a media release, not just an assumption. Most clubs already collect a photo or media consent form at registration. Confirm yours does, and check it before you post, rather than assuming every family is fine with it.
  • Skip full names next to faces. AYSO's own published privacy policy is direct about this: "AYSO does not publish the names or locations of the children in the photo," and adds that "pictures posted on public sites should not include the names of individual players to avoid identifying children to the wrong individuals." A first name, or no name at all, tells the story just as well.
  • Never post a full roster publicly. The same AYSO policy states plainly that "team rosters with names, numbers and contact information must not be posted on any public website or social media page." Keep that information in whatever private team app or group text you already use.
  • Skip real time location tags, especially for an away tournament, since that broadcasts exactly where a group of children will be for the next several hours.
  • Honor an opt out immediately and without asking why. Some families have real reasons, custody situations, safety concerns, or simply a preference, and none of them are your business to evaluate.

One legal note worth getting right: COPPA, the federal law people sometimes bring up here, regulates websites and apps that collect data directly from children under 13, not an adult posting a photo. The FTC's own guidance says this directly, that the law "does not cover information collected from adults that may pertain to children," and specifically names "an adult uploading photos of children on a general audience site" as outside its scope. That does not mean anything goes, it means the real protection here is your club's consent process and your own judgment, not a law that was not built for this situation.

Rule twoPost on a rhythm you can actually keep

Once a week is a real, respectable pace. Every other day works too, if you have the time. What matters is that parents can predict roughly when something new will show up, not that you hit a specific number. A page that posts five times one week and then goes silent for a month teaches parents to stop checking. Pick a cadence you could keep up during your busiest week of the season, not your easiest one, and build from there.

The core habitOne player, one specific thing they did well

An action photo of an individual player, paired with one specific, technical thing they did in that exact moment: dribbling with her head up, finding the pass to the overlapping fullback, stepping in front of a pass to win it clean. Not "great game" and not "awesome player." The specific detail is the entire point.

This is one of the more directly research backed pieces of advice in this whole guide. In a set of controlled studies, researchers Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck praised fifth graders for either their intelligence or their effort after a task, then gave everyone a harder task designed to make them fail. Children praised for intelligence "attributed relatively more of their failure to a lack of ability" than those praised for effort, enjoyed the task less, and were less likely to want to keep trying. Performance told the same story: after failing, the ability praised group's scores on a follow up task dropped, while the effort praised group's scores actually improved over their earlier attempt. Perhaps the most telling finding: 38 percent of children praised for ability lied about their scores to a stranger afterward, compared with 13 percent of children praised for effort.

Naming the specific action does the same job that effort praise did in that study, it tells a player what actually worked, not just that they are talented. The Positive Coaching Alliance builds this directly into its own coaching guidance, with example phrasing that could be lifted straight into a caption: "you continued dribbling with your left hand today at practice, even when it was hard," or "I can see how much you practiced hitting the cutoff player every day at practice and that work helped you do that in a game today." That is the model. Vague praise can be waved off. A specific, true detail cannot.

Rule threeBuild a few traditions and repeat them

Pick one or two recurring features rather than reinventing the format every week. A weekly training award, an effort award voted on or chosen by the coaching staff, a rotating "who showed up early" shoutout. The content matters less than the repetition. A tradition parents recognize by week three is doing more work than a clever one time post, because it tells the team what actually gets noticed here week after week: not just who scored, but who worked.

A format worth tryingThe blind question video

Ask every player the same question, one at a time, without letting them hear each other's answers, then edit the responses together into a single video. Keep it simple: "what is one thing about this team you would tell a new player," or "what is the funniest thing that happened at practice this month." A few things make this format actually work:

  • Ask privately, one player at a time, so answers are not copied from whoever went first.
  • Keep it to one sentence or less per player. Long answers make the final video drag, and kids rarely need more than a sentence to say something honest.
  • Similar framing and lighting for each clip makes the final cut feel like one video instead of a pile of unrelated clips. You do not need matching backgrounds, just roughly the same distance and angle.
  • Cut straight from answer to answer, with no gaps. The format works because of the rhythm, the same question landing differently in nine or ten different voices in a row.

Rule fourCelebrate the whole kid, with the family's yes

Great grades, a volunteer shift, an award at school, these belong on a team page as much as anything that happens on the field, and they tell parents plainly what you value as a coach. Treat this category with a little more care than an action photo from a game everyone already watched together: ask the specific family before posting a specific achievement, rather than assuming the general media release covers it. A goal scored in front of forty people at a public game is different from a report card, even a good one, and a quick "would it be alright if I shared this" text costs you nothing and respects that difference.

Watch outWhat not to do

  • Posting the final score or season record, even to celebrate a win.
  • Full names next to faces, or a public roster. Keep identifying information off any public page.
  • Generic praise with no specific detail. "Great game" teaches a player nothing about what actually worked.
  • Posting a personal achievement, like grades, without asking that family first. A media release for game photos is not the same as consent for everything.
  • A burst of posts followed by silence. Pick a pace you can actually sustain all season.
