Running Fair Soccer Tryouts: Scoring Players Objectively, Without Burning Bridges

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 24, 2026 · 14 min read

Tryouts are the one part of the season where a coach's judgment gets examined the hardest, by kids who want to know where they stand and by parents watching every step of how that decision got made. A gut feeling about forty players over the course of an hour is exactly how a good coach ends up defending a decision after the fact instead of explaining it plainly up front. A written rubric, more than one set of eyes, and a plan for how you deliver results fix most of what actually goes wrong.

Who this guide is for

Coaches or clubs running any kind of competitive evaluation, whether that is a full club tryout, an all star selection, or simply deciding who moves up when a growing rec program needs to split into teams. If your roster is already set and the challenge now is playing time and communication all season, our preseason parent meeting guide and player development guide cover what comes next.

The short version
  • Score against a written rubric, not a memory of who looked good in the moment.
  • Score attitude, coachability, and effort alongside skill. They predict fit at least as well as any technical category.
  • Use more than one evaluator, calibrate them together, and consider adding a known player into the group as a live benchmark.
  • Note fit and level too, whether a player suits your existing group, and whether they might be better served a level up or down.
  • Tell families the process, timeline, and roster size before tryouts begin, not after.
  • Watch the parents too, and talk with them directly. A roster spot is a decision about a family, not just a player.
  • Deliver results the same way every time, promptly and without exceptions for whoever asks the loudest.
  • Never leave a family with false hope. A vague "it was close" feels kinder in the moment and lands worse the next day.

ScoringScore against a rubric, not a memory

A common way clubs structure tryout scoring is around a handful of categories such as technical ability, passing, tactical awareness, physical traits, and coachability. One youth sports platform puts the case for doing this on purpose plainly: organizations that use "a consistent rubric built around categories like these tend to produce more defensible, comparable rankings" than evaluations based on gut feel alone, and it gives every evaluator on your staff a shared language for discussing where a player actually stands.

You do not need eight categories to get the benefit. Four or five that you can genuinely watch for in real time, first touch and ball control, decision making with and without the ball, physical traits like speed and effort, and coachability, cover most of what separates players at a tryout. What matters more than the exact list is writing it down before tryouts start and scoring every player against the same list.

One category deserves a second look before you finalize that list. Physical traits like speed and size can end up rewarding whoever hit an early growth spurt rather than whoever has the most long term potential. Our equal playing time guide cites research from an elite academy showing that late maturing players received meaningfully less playing time specifically because selection leaned on physical development. Weigh technical and tactical categories at least as heavily as physical ones, and score effort as its own category rather than folding it in with speed, so a smaller player with a great first touch and a great attitude is not quietly scored behind a bigger player who is simply further along physically this year.

For the scale itself, a soccer specific evaluation guide recommends five levels rather than a simple pass or fail. As it explains, "a five-level rubric provides enough resolution to track meaningful progress without creating false precision," running from an emerging skill up through an elite one. A simple version of that scale, adapted to your own categories, might look like this.

LevelWhat it looks like
1. Emerging The skill shows up rarely or breaks down under any pressure. Still learning the basic mechanics.
2. Developing The skill is inconsistent. Works in open space, breaks down when a defender closes in.
3. Solid The skill holds up in a normal game situation most of the time.
4. Advanced The skill holds up under real pressure and the player can use it to create an advantage.
5. Elite The skill is a clear strength relative to the whole group being evaluated, not just for their age.

Sample formPut attitude and coachability on the same form as skill

Most evaluation forms cover technical, tactical, and physical categories well and stop there, which misses some of what actually predicts whether a player fits your team. A guide to building a tryout evaluation form makes the case directly: "rate the specific skills, then always include two things that predict a good season regardless of sport: effort and coachability." Another evaluation resource frames attitude the same way, describing it as one of the strongest predictors of how a player will develop over time, even though it is harder to quantify than a technical skill. Enjoyment belongs on the same form for a simpler reason, a player who is visibly having a good time and encouraging teammates is showing you something real about fit, not just a skill you can rate.

Score the categories below on the same one to five scale as the table above, first touch and passing included.

CategoryWhat to watch for
Technical First touch, ball control, passing accuracy.
Tactical Decision making, positioning, reading the game before the ball arrives.
Physical Speed and physical presence. Score cautiously, see the note above on early growth spurts.
Effort Work rate on every rep, hustle in transition, chasing a lost cause.
Attitude Competitiveness, and how they respond right after a mistake.
Coachability Response to a correction, and whether they try it again immediately.
Enjoyment Visibly having fun, encouraging teammates, engaged between reps.

Two more questions belong on the same form, though neither fits the one to five scale above. Team fit asks whether this player's personality and style mesh with the group already on your roster, the players who are not trying out again because they made the team last time. Level fit asks something a rubric alone will not tell you, whether this specific player, based on what you actually saw, would be better served at a level up or down from the one they tried out for. Our recreational to competitive guide makes the case that a player who is challenged and still having fun is the real goal, not a specific level. Pointing a player toward the level where that is true, even if it means recommending them to a different team, is part of running a fair evaluation, not a failure of one.

BiasGet more than one set of eyes on every player

A single coach trying to rate every player on every category at once is where most tryout bias creeps in, not out of bad intentions but because attention is limited. One evaluation guide is direct about the fix: "if you have multiple coaches on the session, assign one coach per skill category," since "one observer per category is more reliable than one coach rating everything."

That only works if evaluators are actually calibrated to the same standard before players arrive. A soccer specific tryout guide describes doing this with examples: run through video clips or live demonstrations together and agree out loud, this first touch is a 3, this one is a 4, "when evaluators see the same examples, they apply the same bar." Ten minutes of this before the first player steps on the field saves a lot of disagreement later.

When scores from different evaluators land far apart on the same player, treat that as useful information rather than an error to smooth over. The same guide frames it well: "players with wide score variance deserve discussion," since "disagreements often highlight players worth a second look," a player one evaluator noticed something the others missed on, not just a scoring mistake to average away.

One more way to keep first day scores grounded is worth trying, even though it is our own practical suggestion rather than something pulled from a published guide. Add a player whose skill level you already know well into the tryout group, without telling anyone why they are there. You do not need to score them. Watching how a new player's first touch or decision making compares to a known quantity gives evaluators a steadier anchor than an abstract number on a rubric, especially in the first ten minutes before anyone has settled in.

CommunicationTell families the rules before anyone shows up

Most of the frustration that shows up after a tryout was actually created weeks earlier, by families who did not know what to expect. One club communication guide recommends sharing tryout dates, locations, what to bring, expected skills, and roster sizes well in advance, ideally one to three months ahead, precisely so families can prepare their kids and understand that cuts are a real possibility rather than a surprise. A director quoted in that same piece sums up the underlying goal in one line: "I believe in transparency in the tryout process."

That advance communication is worth repeating in person on the day itself. Walk parents through who is evaluating, how tryout numbers work, how big the roster will be, and exactly when they will hear results. A sports psychologist's advice for this kind of written communication applies here too, treat it "as a resource to point parents back to if they have questions during or after tryouts," rather than something you only mention once and hope everyone remembers.

Tryout dayWhat the day itself should look like

Set up your grids, cones, and stations before players arrive, not while they are standing around waiting. One evaluation guide notes this plainly: preparing logistics ahead of time means evaluators can focus on players instead of setup, which is exactly where your attention should be during the limited time you have with each player.

If your format allows more than a single session, take it. A soccer evaluation resource makes the case for repeated looks over a one time snapshot: "two evaluations establish a baseline and one change point," while "three evaluations begin to show a trend," and five or more start to produce data reliable enough to make real decisions with confidence. Most tryouts cannot run five sessions, but even two nights instead of one meaningfully reduces how much a single bad ten minutes can define a player's whole evaluation. If you are limited to one day, give every player multiple touches across more than one small sided game rather than judging off one drill.

ParentsWatch the parents too

A roster spot is not only a decision about a player. It is a decision to spend a season with that player's family, and tryouts are one of the only chances you get to learn something about them before you commit to it.

Pay attention the same way you would at any other point in the season. How a parent says goodbye at drop off, whether they linger to coach quietly from behind the fence, and how they greet their child at pickup after a hard session all tell you something real, well before you have to make a decision. Notice the tone of the questions you get too, during a session and after one. A parent asking what their child can work on is telling you something different than a parent asking why their child did not get more repetitions in the drill you happened to be running.

This instinct is borrowed from a different setting, college coaches sizing up a recruit's family before a scholarship is on the line, but it transfers well to a youth tryout. As one former Division I coach put it, "coaches want to evaluate both the student-athlete and their parents," since a family's own behavior is often a preview of the player's. The practical test he described works just as well here: "are they complaining about playing time? Being overbearing?" How a parent takes hard news matters too, whether that is a piece of honest feedback mid session or the news that their child did not make the roster. As the same coach put it, "this is a family decision, so the more you know about them, the more you know about the student-athlete."

None of this is a reason to keep parents at a distance during tryouts. It is a reason to talk with them directly, on purpose, rather than only watching from across the field. Ask what they are hoping for this season, what they already understood about playing time and role before they signed up, and what they would consider a good outcome even if their child does not start. Our preseason parent meeting guide covers stating your policy plainly once a roster is set. This is the same conversation held a step earlier, before that policy has to apply to one specific family. A mismatch you catch in a five minute conversation during tryout week is far easier to handle than the same mismatch six weeks into the season.

ResultsDecide promptly, and share results the same way every time

A youth soccer club's own guidelines for this process put the standard directly: make decisions promptly and honestly, without stringing a family along with false hope, and communicate your selection criteria clearly before tryouts so the standard was never a secret in the first place.

How you share the news matters almost as much as the decision itself. One club director's recommendation is specific: "the best way to communicate results is an individual email to parents," while a sports psychologist advising on the same question favors a phone call or one on one conversation over any group announcement, adding that a coach should "be authentic, share reasons why," and let the moment be "a growth opportunity" rather than a verdict. Whichever method fits your club, remain available afterward. Parents and players who have specific questions deserve a real answer, not just a posted list and silence.

Age changes who hears the news first. For younger players, tell the parents first and let them decide how and when to tell their child, in a way that fits their own kid. For middle and high school aged players, talk to the player directly, so they hear firsthand what to work on and what making the team next time would actually require.

The hard conversationWhat to say when a player does not make the team

The same club guidelines above are blunt about the one thing to avoid: do not soften a cut by shifting the focus. "Be honest and caring, and don't try to shift the focus, e.g. don't say, 'it was a close decision.'" A vague explanation feels gentler to deliver in the moment, but it leaves a family with nothing to actually work on, and often more hurt once they realize there was a real reason that was never said out loud.

Give one honest, specific reason tied to what you actually saw, and pair it with something the player can do next. Where you can, point the family toward a real next step, a lower level team, a specific skill to develop, a coach who might be a better fit. And keep some perspective available for the family too. As one youth sports resource puts it plainly, "it's incredibly rare for a person to make every single team they tryout for," a fact worth saying out loud in the moment, because it usually does not feel true to a disappointed kid or parent.

ExampleWhat an honest conversation can sound like

For a player who made the team, keep it short and specific: "Congratulations, you made the team. Your first touch under pressure and your work rate really stood out this weekend. We will send full details on the schedule by Friday."

For a player who did not: "Thank you for trying out. This was not a decision about effort, you worked hard all weekend. Right now, the players we selected were a bit further along with quick decision making under pressure, and that is something very learnable between now and next season. I would love to see [Player's Name] try out again, and in the meantime, [Lower Level Team or League] would be a great fit this year."

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Scoring off memory or reputation instead of a rubric written down before tryouts started.
  • Scoring only technical, tactical, and physical categories, and skipping attitude, coachability, and effort entirely.
  • Letting one adult evaluate every player alone, with no second set of eyes to catch what got missed.
  • Announcing roster size or criteria only after tryouts, once it is too late for it to feel like it was ever fair.
  • Never talking to parents directly about their expectations, and finding out about a mismatch six weeks into the season instead.
  • Softening a cut with a vague reason instead of one honest, specific one a family can actually use.
  • Posting a public list as the only communication, with no way for a family to ask a real question.
  • Never following up with a cut player about what to work on before the next opportunity.
