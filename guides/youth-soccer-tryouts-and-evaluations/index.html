Running Fair Soccer Tryouts: Scoring Players Objectively, Without Burning Bridges

By Joseph Warren · Youth soccer coach and creator of SubGo · September 24, 2026, updated October 19, 2026 · 18 min read

Tryouts are the one part of the season where a coach's judgment gets examined the hardest, by kids who want to know where they stand and by parents watching every step of how that decision got made. A gut feeling about forty players over the course of an hour is exactly how a good coach ends up defending a decision after the fact instead of explaining it plainly up front. A written rubric, more than one set of eyes, and a plan for how you deliver results fix most of what actually goes wrong.

Who this guide is for

Coaches or clubs running any kind of competitive evaluation, whether that is a full club tryout, an all star selection, or simply deciding who moves up when a growing rec program needs to split into teams. If your roster is already set and the challenge now is playing time and communication all season, our preseason parent meeting guide and player development guide cover what comes next.

The short version
  • Score against a written rubric, not a memory of who looked good in the moment.
  • Score effort, coachability, and attitude alongside skill. One tryout form guide says coaches wish they had weighted effort and coachability more (a vendor claim with no data).
  • Use more than one evaluator, calibrate them together, and never let anyone score their own child or a relative.
  • Note fit and level too, whether a player suits your existing group, and whether they might be better served a level up or down.
  • Tell families the process, criteria, and roster size before tryouts begin, not after.
  • Talk with parents directly about expectations, but never score a child on a parent's behavior.
  • Deliver results the same way every time, promptly, by individual message, and never as a public list of names or scores.
  • Never leave a family with false hope. A vague "it was close" feels kinder in the moment and lands worse the next day.

ScoringScore against a rubric, not a memory

A common way clubs structure tryout scoring is around a handful of categories. TeamGenius, a youth sports software company, lists eight common ones: technical, passing, receiving, dribbling, tactical, physical, athleticism, and attitude, where attitude covers coachability, effort level, and competitiveness. Its case for doing this on purpose is that organizations that use "a consistent rubric built around categories like these tend to produce more defensible, comparable rankings" than evaluations based on gut feel alone, and it gives every evaluator on your staff a shared language for discussing where a player actually stands.

You do not need eight categories to get the benefit. Four or five that you can genuinely watch for in real time, first touch and ball control, decision making with and without the ball, physical traits like speed and effort, and coachability, cover most of what separates players at a tryout. What matters more than the exact list is writing it down before tryouts start and scoring every player against the same list.

One category deserves a second look before you finalize that list. Physical traits like speed and size can end up rewarding whoever hit an early growth spurt rather than whoever has the most long term potential. Our equal playing time guide cites research from an elite academy showing that late maturing players received meaningfully less playing time specifically because selection leaned on physical development, and our growth and maturity guide goes deeper into just how large that gap can be between two players the same age. Weigh technical and tactical categories at least as heavily as physical ones, and score effort as its own category rather than folding it in with speed, so a smaller player with a great first touch and a great attitude is not quietly scored behind a bigger player who is simply further along physically this year.

For the scale itself, a soccer specific evaluation guide recommends five levels rather than a simple pass or fail. As it explains, "a five-level rubric provides enough resolution to track meaningful progress without creating false precision," running from an emerging skill up through an elite one. A simple version of that scale, adapted to your own categories, might look like this.

LevelWhat it looks like
1. Emerging The skill shows up rarely or breaks down under any pressure. Still learning the basic mechanics.
2. Developing The skill is inconsistent. Works in open space, breaks down when a defender closes in.
3. Solid The skill holds up in a normal game situation most of the time.
4. Advanced The skill holds up under real pressure and the player can use it to create an advantage.
5. Elite The skill is a clear strength relative to the whole group being evaluated, not just for their age.

Sample formPut attitude and coachability on the same form as skill

Most evaluation forms cover technical, tactical, and physical categories well and stop there, which can miss some of what makes a player a good fit for your team. Rizzler's guide to tryout evaluation forms says to include "two things that predict a good season regardless of sport: effort and coachability," and adds that coaches say those are the two they wish they had weighted more. LaceUp's evaluation guide makes a related point about psychological skills, saying they are "frequently the strongest predictor" of which players develop through their teen years and which plateau. Neither source tests this in youth soccer tryouts, so treat it as practitioner advice, not proof. Enjoyment belongs on the form as a note, not a score, for a simpler reason, our own view: a player who is visibly having a good time and encouraging teammates is showing you something real about fit, but a score would penalize kids who show it less.

Score the categories below on the same one to five scale as the table above, first touch and passing included.

CategoryWhat to watch for
Technical First touch, ball control, passing accuracy.
Tactical Decision making, positioning, reading the game before the ball arrives.
Physical Speed and physical presence. Score cautiously, see the note above on early growth spurts.
Effort Work rate on every rep, hustle in transition, chasing a lost cause.
Attitude Observable behavior: stays engaged right after a mistake, competes for loose balls.
Coachability Response to a correction, and whether they try it again immediately.

Two more questions belong on the form as notes, not scores, since neither fits the one to five scale above and both can penalize quiet or shy players. Team fit asks whether this player's personality and style mesh with the group already on your roster, the players who are not trying out again because they made the team last time. Level fit asks something a rubric alone will not tell you, whether this specific player, based on what you actually saw, would be better served at a level up or down from the one they tried out for. Our recreational to competitive guide makes the case that a player who is challenged and still having fun is the real goal, not a specific level. Pointing a player toward the level where that is true, even if it means recommending them to a different team, is part of running a fair evaluation, not a failure of one.

BiasGet more than one set of eyes on every player

A single coach trying to rate every player on every category at once is where most tryout bias creeps in, not out of bad intentions but because attention is limited. LaceUp's guide advises that "one observer per category is more reliable than one coach rating everything," and suggests assigning one coach per skill category when you have several coaches on the session. StriveOn's soccer evaluation form guide takes the other approach, with every evaluator scoring the whole sheet. Either can work. Pick one before tryouts and stay with it.

That only works if evaluators are calibrated to the same standard before players arrive. StriveOn recommends putting a number on a few examples at the pre tryout staff meeting, so two coaches scoring a skill are scoring against the same description. Our adaptation is to run through video clips or live demonstrations together and agree out loud, this first touch is a 3, this one is a 4. Ten minutes of this before the first player steps on the field saves a lot of disagreement later.

When scores from different evaluators land far apart on the same player, treat that as useful information rather than an error to smooth over. StriveOn's sheet puts a player up for discussion when scores spread by 5 or more points out of 30, before anyone is ranked, and for players still level after discussion it says to "settle it with another look, in the scrimmage or on video, before you choose." Its numbers fit its own six skill sheet, so scale them to yours.

A worked example

This example is illustrative. Evaluator A scores player 14 a 4 on first touch, and Evaluator B scores a 2. Do not average them to a 3. Ask each what they saw. A watched a clean first touch in open space. B watched it break down when a defender closed in. That matches the Developing level in the scale above, so you agree to look again in the next small sided game with pressure before you finalize the score. The disagreement just told you where to look.

Keep conflicts out of the room

TeamGenius recommends evaluators with no personal connection to the players, and describes one association that had boys' coaches score girls and the reverse. It also suggests scoring by tryout numbers instead of names, and keeping a coach feedback section so a player with a weak drill but a good track record is not automatically eliminated. Our own rule is simple: no one scores their own child or a relative, and if your child is trying out, step out of that group. When the scores are in, check the birth month spread of your top scorers. If they cluster early in the year, ask whether you scored maturity instead of skill, and see our growth and maturity guide.

One more way to keep first day scores grounded is our own practical suggestion, not something from a published guide. Add a player whose skill level you already know well into the tryout group as a guest. Tell that player and their parents they are there to help the evaluators, and do not score them. Watching how a new player's first touch or decision making compares to a known quantity gives evaluators a steadier anchor than an abstract number on a rubric, especially in the first ten minutes before anyone has settled in.

CommunicationTell families the rules before anyone shows up

Most of the frustration that shows up after a tryout was created weeks earlier, by families who did not know what to expect. TeamGenius advises sending parents information in advance of tryout dates, including dates and locations, what to wear and bring, expected skills, and team and roster sizes, so families can prepare their kids and understand that cuts are a real possibility. Check your state association or league rules for tryout dates and notification windows before you announce a schedule. Andrew Lenhardt, Director of Coaching at the Springfield Area Soccer Association, says in that piece, "I believe in transparency in the tryout process." TeamGenius also describes making evaluation forms available to parents afterward. Decide in advance what you will share. We suggest sharing the written reasons and next steps for that child, and never another child's scores.

Repeat that information in person on the day itself. Walk parents through who is evaluating, how tryout numbers work, how big the roster will be, and exactly when they will hear results. Keep the written version handy as something to point parents back to if they have questions during or after tryouts.

Tryout dayWhat the day itself should look like

Set up your grids, cones, and stations before players arrive, not while they are standing around waiting. StriveOn advises marking the field early so the first half hour goes to watching players instead of moving cones, which is exactly where your attention should be during the limited time you have with each player.

If your format allows more than a single session, take it. LaceUp's evaluation guide is about tracking player progress over time, not tryouts, but its logic transfers. It says two evaluations establish a baseline and one change point, three begin to show a trend, and five or more produce data reliable enough to make training decisions with confidence. Most tryouts cannot run five sessions, but even two nights instead of one reduces how much a single bad ten minutes can define a player's whole evaluation, which is our inference from that logic. If you are limited to one day, give every player multiple touches across more than one small sided game rather than judging off one drill.

Before, during, and after

This timeline is our suggestion, built from the steps above.

WhenWhat to do
Weeks beforeWrite the rubric, choose evaluators, check for conflicts, calibrate the staff, and send families the process, criteria, and roster size.
Tryout daySet up early, score by tryout number during play, and keep score talk away from parents.
Same or next dayCompare scores, discuss large gaps, and take a second look where needed.
Within a few daysTell every family the same way, offer to talk, and follow up with players who did not make it.

ParentsTalk with the parents, but score the player

A roster spot is not only a decision about a player. It commits you to a season alongside that player's family, and tryouts are one of the few chances to set expectations with them before that season starts. How a parent says goodbye at drop off, and the tone of the questions you get, tell you something about what conversations you will need to have. A parent asking what their child can work on is telling you something different than a parent asking why their child did not get more repetitions in the drill you happened to be running.

Those observations tell you what to talk about. They should never lower a child's score. A child cannot control their parents, and a parent's behavior is better handled through a code of conduct than through the score sheet. Keep your evaluation form about the player only.

The idea of evaluating a family comes from college recruiting, and it is worth being careful with. JC Field, a former Division I baseball coach, told USA Today High School Sports that college coaches evaluate both the athlete and the parents, and asked whether parents are complaining about playing time or being overbearing. That is a different setting, with a scholarship at stake, and we do not recommend importing it into youth tryouts as a scoring factor.

What does transfer is talking with parents directly, on purpose. Ask what they are hoping for this season, what they already understood about playing time and role before they signed up, and what they would consider a good outcome even if their child does not start. Our preseason parent meeting guide covers stating your policy plainly once a roster is set. This is the same conversation held a step earlier. If it reveals a real mismatch, such as a family who cannot accept your playing time policy, say so plainly and let them decide whether the team fits, instead of lowering their child's score.

ResultsDecide promptly, and share results the same way every time

Braddock Road Youth Club's guidelines for cutting players put the standard directly: have established criteria for your selections, do not drag out the final decision, and do not give false hope. They add that coaches should not tell a player they will probably make the team, even if they believe it, because players compare notes and a child may be crushed if replaced.

How you share the news matters almost as much as the decision itself. TeamGenius's results article suggests individual emails, which let you add a personal note, and one on one phone or in person conversations, which sports psychologist Dr. Justin Anderson calls the best way to share news with youth players. Andrew Lenhardt says, "I feel the best way to communicate results is an individual email to parents." Anderson advises treating a cut as a growth opportunity and giving the player specific areas to work on. If you post results online, TeamGenius advises using tryout numbers instead of names, and notes that this is not fully private, since families may still identify their child. We recommend individual messages and no public list with names or scores. Whichever method fits your club, remain available afterward, and include a contact number. Parents and players with specific questions deserve a real answer, not just a posted list and silence.

Age changes who hears the news first. For younger players, tell the parents first and let them decide how and when to tell their child, in a way that fits their own kid. For middle and high school aged players, talk to the player directly, so they hear firsthand what to work on and what making the team next time would actually require.

The hard conversationWhat to say when a player does not make the team

The same club guidelines say to be honest and caring and not to try to shift the focus, giving "It was a close decision" as an example of what not to say. A vague explanation feels gentler to deliver in the moment, but it leaves a family with nothing to actually work on, and often more hurt once they realize there was a real reason that was never said out loud.

Give one honest, specific reason tied to what you actually saw, and pair it with something the player can do next. Where you can, point the family toward a real next step, a lower level team, a specific skill to develop, a coach who might be a better fit. And keep some perspective available for the family too. TeamSnap's advice for parents, in a section on showing empathy, notes that it is incredibly rare for a person to make every single team they try out for, a fact worth saying out loud, because it usually does not feel true to a disappointed kid or parent.

ExampleWhat an honest conversation can sound like

For a player who made the team, keep it short and specific: "Congratulations, you made the team. Your first touch under pressure and your work rate really stood out this weekend. We will send full details on the schedule by Friday."

For a player who did not: "Thank you for trying out. This was not a decision about effort, you worked hard all weekend. Right now, the players we selected were a bit further along with quick decision making under pressure, and that is something very learnable between now and next season. I would love to see [Player's Name] try out again, and in the meantime, [Lower Level Team or League] would be a great fit this year."

Watch outCommon mistakes

  • Scoring off memory or reputation instead of a rubric written down before tryouts started.
  • Scoring only technical, tactical, and physical categories, and skipping attitude, coachability, and effort entirely.
  • Letting one adult evaluate every player alone, with no second set of eyes to catch what got missed.
  • Announcing roster size or criteria only after tryouts, once it is too late for it to feel like it was ever fair.
  • Never talking to parents directly about their expectations, and finding out about a mismatch six weeks into the season instead.
  • Softening a cut with a vague reason instead of one honest, specific one a family can actually use.
  • Posting a public list with names or scores, or using it as the only communication, with no way for a family to ask a real question.
  • Letting an evaluator score their own child or a relative, even with the best intentions.
  • Lowering a child's score because of a parent's behavior, instead of handling it through a code of conduct.
  • Never following up with a cut player about what to work on before the next opportunity.

Ask your AI assistant coachTurn the rubric into a form, and plan how you will share results

AI is useful here for forms, messages, and finding holes in your plan. It should never rank children or decide who makes the team. That is your judgment, made against your written rubric. If you are new to AI, start with our beginner guide, and for answers that sound like you, set up a coaching profile first.

1. A scoring sheet. Good output is a printable sheet with a one line description of each level for each category.

"I am running tryouts for [age group] [format], evaluating about [number] players over [number] sessions, with [number] evaluators. Using a five level scale from emerging to elite, build me a printable scoring sheet with these categories: technical, tactical, physical, effort, attitude, and coachability. Add a short one line description of what each level looks like for each category. Add an unscored notes column for enjoyment and team fit. Leave a column for tryout number, not name." Paste this before tryouts, once your categories are set

2. A results plan. Good output is a plan for when and how families hear, with a contact path for questions.

"I am running tryouts for [age group] [format] with a roster of about [number] spots. Draft a general communication plan describing when and how I will notify families of results by individual message, and how I will respond when a family asks why their child did not make the team. Keep it about the process, not any specific player's scores or outcome." Paste this before tryouts begin, once you know your roster size and timeline

3. Message drafts. Good output is two short templates with placeholders you can personalize.

"Draft two short message templates for tryout results: one for a player who made the team and one for a player who did not. Use [Player Name] and [Specific Strength] and [Next Step] as placeholders. Be honest and caring, do not say it was a close decision, and do not promise anything about next year." Fill in the placeholders on your own copy, never in the AI tool

4. A bias check. Good output is a list of problems, not a rewrite.

"Here is my tryout plan: [paste it, with no names, and no tryout numbers if a name list exists elsewhere in the same document]. Act as an experienced youth club director and point out anywhere it could feel unfair to a family, let a conflict of interest slip in, or reward physical maturity over skill. List the problems only." Fix what it finds before tryouts, not after

Tryout scores and names are about children. Keep real player names, tryout numbers linked to names, and actual scores out of a general AI tool. Build and test the blank form and the communication plan with placeholder players, then fill in real names and outcomes only on your own private copy afterward. AI can also invent research and quotes, so check any source it names before you repeat it.
